Flashback · coming week, 28 September – 4 October

A first US veto override, a rejected peace deal, and Haiti's deadliest storm since 2005

10 years ago2016A veto overridden · a peace vote lost · a storm that killed hundreds
POLITICS2016Sep 28, 2016

Congress overrides a presidential veto for the first time in eight years, opening Saudi Arabia to 9/11 lawsuits

On September 28, 2016, the US Senate voted 97–1 and the House of Representatives voted 348–77 to override President Barack Obama's veto of the Justice Against Sponsors of Terrorism Act (JASTA) — comfortably clearing the two-thirds majority each chamber needs to override a presidential veto, and the first time Congress had overturned one of his vetoes in nearly eight years in office. The law lets American courts hear lawsuits filed by families of the September 11, 2001 attacks against foreign governments accused of supporting the hijackers, aimed chiefly at Saudi Arabia, which has long denied any government role in the attacks.

Obama had vetoed the bill days earlier, warning it would strip foreign officials of legal protection and expose US diplomats and troops abroad to reciprocal lawsuits — an argument that persuaded almost no one in a Congress facing re-election pressure from 9/11 families weeks later.

Context & background. Existing law generally shielded foreign governments from being sued in US courts except in narrow terrorism-exception cases. The Justice Against Sponsors of Terrorism Act (JASTA) created a new exception specifically for claims tied to acts of international terrorism on US soil, letting courts consider whether a foreign government's officials, agents or employees had provided material support — a defined legal term covering things like funding, training, weapons or other direct assistance to those who carried out an attack. Families of 9/11 victims had been pursuing Saudi Arabia in court for over a decade by the time the bill reached the floor.

The details. The override followed years of lobbying by families and survivors, sharpened by the July 2016 declassification of the so-called "28 pages" from the original congressional inquiry into the attacks, which described possible Saudi connections. Saudi Arabia's government lobbied hard against the bill, reportedly warning it might sell off US assets in response, and after the override it moved quickly to fund its own advocacy campaign against the new law. Several senators later admitted, once questioned by reporters, that they had not fully studied the bill's implications before voting for it.

Why it still matters. Families of 9/11 victims have used the law in the years since to pursue civil claims against the Saudi government in federal court, a case that has moved slowly through jurisdictional fights and evidence disputes. It remains the clearest instance of Congress choosing victims' access to the courts over the executive branch's preferred diplomatic caution, and it still comes up whenever lawmakers debate stripping immunity from other states accused of backing attacks on Americans.

CONFLICT2016Oct 2, 2016

Colombians narrowly vote down a peace deal meant to end 52 years of civil war

On October 2, 2016, Colombian voters rejected a peace agreement between the government and the Revolutionary Armed Forces of Colombia (FARC) rebel group by the narrowest of margins — 50.2% against to 49.8% in favor, on a turnout under 38% — dealing an unexpected blow to a deal meant to end a conflict that had killed roughly 220,000 people and displaced about 6 million over more than five decades.

The accord would have granted reduced sentences and limited amnesty to rank-and-file fighters in exchange for disarmament; opponents, led by former president Álvaro Uribe, argued the terms let killers escape real punishment. Despite the defeat, the government and the guerrilla group both said they intended to keep pursuing peace rather than return to war.

Context & background. The war between the Colombian state and Revolutionary Armed Forces of Colombia (FARC) guerrillas began in 1964, rooted in rural land disputes, and became one of the world's longest-running civil conflicts, financed on the rebel side partly through the cocaine trade. Formal talks between the government of President Juan Manuel Santos and the group's leadership had run since 2012 in Havana, Cuba, producing a nearly 300-page agreement that was signed at a televised ceremony in Cartagena, Colombia, on September 26, 2016 — six days before the vote.

The details. Santos had promised a public referendum to give the deal democratic legitimacy, expecting comfortable approval. Instead, a low-turnout "No" campaign led by Uribe's allies — helped further by Hurricane Matthew disrupting voting on the Caribbean coast, a region that had leaned toward the deal — produced the upset. Santos was awarded that year's Nobel Peace Prize within days of the vote, an award the prize committee said was also meant to encourage the process to continue despite the setback.

Why it still matters. Rather than restart negotiations from scratch, the government revised the agreement to answer critics' main objections and pushed the amended text through Congress instead of a second public vote; Colombia's Congress ratified it weeks later, in November 2016. The country has continued to struggle with dissident fighters and other armed groups since, a reminder that a peace agreement's signing rarely marks the end of a conflict's story.

DISASTER2016Oct 4, 2016

Hurricane Matthew kills more than 500 people in Haiti, its deadliest disaster since the 2010 earthquake

Hurricane Matthew came ashore near Les Anglais on Haiti's Tiburon Peninsula on the morning of October 4, 2016, as a Category 4 storm with sustained winds near 150 miles an hour, before curving north toward the eastern United States. In Haiti it killed at least 546 people, destroyed roughly 210,000 homes, and wiped out around 90% of buildings and coconut trees along the peninsula's southern coast.

The storm cut off the hardest-hit areas for days, slowing aid delivery, and was followed within weeks by a resurgence of cholera, the water-borne disease first brought to Haiti by United Nations peacekeepers after the 2010 earthquake; nearly 10,000 suspected cases were recorded in Matthew's aftermath.

Context & background. Matthew formed as the Atlantic's first major hurricane of the 2016 season and rapidly intensified over the warm Caribbean Sea, briefly reaching the top category before weakening slightly on approach to Haiti. Six years after a magnitude-7.0 earthquake had killed an estimated 100,000–160,000 people and left the country's infrastructure fragile, the poorest nation in the Western Hemisphere had little capacity to absorb another disaster of this scale.

The details. The storm's slow approach gave forecasters days of warning, but the government had limited ability to evacuate the rural south, where most houses were built of unreinforced masonry or wood with tin roofs. Entire coffee and cocoa plantations, a major source of rural income, were destroyed alongside roughly 350,000 farm animals. The bridge carrying the only paved road between the south and the capital, Port-au-Prince, was washed away, cutting off aid convoys for days.

Why it still matters. Hurricane Matthew remains the deadliest storm the Atlantic basin had produced since Hurricane Stan in 2005, and it became a case study in how a storm's human toll can outrun its wind-speed category in a country with weak infrastructure — a pattern researchers have pointed to since as Caribbean storms intensify faster before making landfall.

25 years ago2001A United Nations (UN) resolution · an airline's collapse · a missile's mistake
POLITICS2001Sep 28, 2001

The Security Council orders every country to cut off terrorist financing, seventeen days after the September 11 attacks

On September 28, 2001, the United Nations (UN) Security Council unanimously adopted Resolution 1373, a binding measure requiring every member state to freeze terrorist assets, criminalize terrorism financing, deny safe haven to terrorism suspects, and share intelligence on terrorist groups — obligations that applied to all United Nations (UN) members, not just the fifteen countries that voted for it: the Security Council has only fifteen members, while the United Nations (UN) as a whole has far more.

The resolution created a Counter-Terrorism Committee to track compliance, turning what had been a set of voluntary anti-terrorism guidelines into enforceable international law for the first time, and it set the template that later United Nations (UN) sanctions regimes against groups such as al-Qaeda and the Islamic State group would follow.

Context & background. In the weeks after the September 11, 2001 attacks on the United States, world leaders faced pressure to respond collectively rather than let one country act alone. The Security Council had already condemned the attacks on September 12, but Resolution 1373 went further: instead of authorizing action against one target, it wrote binding, general obligations into international law under Chapter VII (legally binding, not just advisory) of the United Nations (UN) Charter.

The details. Every member state had to report back to the new Counter-Terrorism Committee on the laws and controls it had put in place, effectively forcing a global review of banking-secrecy rules, extradition treaties and border-security practices within months. Countries that had resisted anti-money-laundering reforms for years passed them within weeks rather than be named as non-compliant.

Why it still matters. Resolution 1373's financing and information-sharing requirements remain the legal backbone for freezing the assets of groups the Council later designated, and its Counter-Terrorism Committee still reviews member states' reports today, a quarter-century on — one of the few pieces of emergency post-9/11 law-making that has proven durable rather than temporary.

BUSINESS2001Oct 2, 2001

Switzerland's national airline runs out of cash mid-schedule and grounds its entire fleet overnight

On October 2, 2001, Swissair — long a byword for Swiss precision and reliability — abruptly grounded its entire fleet after its parent company, SAirGroup, ran out of cash to pay for fuel and airport fees, stranding around 39,000 passengers worldwide with tickets that other airlines would not honor.

The collapse came three weeks after the September 11 attacks had frozen global air travel and credit markets, but Swissair's troubles were years in the making: an aggressive strategy of buying stakes in smaller, financially weak European airlines had already pushed the group toward insolvency before that month's downturn made further borrowing impossible.

Context & background. Swissair had been one of the world's most trusted airlines for decades, financially conservative enough to be nicknamed the "flying bank." That reputation collapsed under an expansion plan under which the parent group bought minority stakes in struggling airlines including Belgium's Sabena and a French regional carrier, hoping to build a European alliance; several of those investments instead drained cash as the airlines they had bought into kept losing money.

The details. By late September 2001, the group's banks had grown reluctant to extend further credit, and on the morning of October 2 finance staff told management there was no longer enough money in the accounts to pay for fuel or landing fees at multiple airports. Flights already in the air were allowed to land, but no new departures were permitted, leaving passengers and crews stranded from Zurich to Johannesburg. A later independent inquiry by the accounting firm Ernst & Young disputed the company's own account of how little cash it actually had on hand that morning, suggesting the grounding may not have been strictly unavoidable.

Why it still matters. The grounding — which Switzerland's own government called a national humiliation — forced the state to organize an emergency loan within days so a scaled-down successor could keep flying. The remains of the group were folded in 2002 into what became Swiss International Air Lines, still the country's flag carrier today, and the episode remains a standard case study in how quickly a cash shortfall, not a weak balance sheet on paper, can end an airline's operations overnight.

DISASTER2001Oct 4, 2001

A stray Ukrainian military missile brings down a passenger jet over the Black Sea, killing all 78 aboard

On October 4, 2001, Siberia Airlines Flight 1812, a Tupolev Tu-154 flying from Tel Aviv to Novosibirsk, broke apart over the Black Sea and crashed, killing all 78 people on board — most of them Israeli citizens visiting relatives in Russia. Ukraine initially denied any involvement, but investigators later concluded that a Ukrainian missile, fired during a joint Russian-Ukrainian military exercise in Crimea, had overshot its intended target drone and locked onto the airliner roughly 150 miles further away instead.

Ukraine's government did not publicly acknowledge responsibility until roughly ten days later, and only agreed in 2003 to pay compensation to victims' families — without formally admitting legal liability, a distinction Ukrainian courts upheld years afterward.

Context & background. Ukraine and Russia held a joint military exercise in Crimea in early October 2001, involving live-fire air-defense drills with an aging Soviet-designed long-range anti-aircraft missile system, better suited to Cold War-era airspace than to a region that had since filled with civilian flight paths.

The details. Investigators from the Interstate Aviation Committee, the body that investigates air accidents across former Soviet states, matched radar tracks and missile-fragment damage to conclude that a missile aimed at a target drone overshot it, failed to self-destruct as designed, and instead struck Flight 1812 at cruising altitude, killing everyone aboard instantly. Ukrainian officials spent the first week after the crash insisting no missile had come anywhere near the airliner, a denial that only crumbled once radar data became difficult to explain any other way.

Why it still matters. The crash remains one of the clearest documented cases of a civilian airliner destroyed by a military accident rather than an attack, and it is still cited in aviation-safety discussions about keeping live-fire military exercises away from commercial flight corridors — a lesson that has resurfaced with new urgency each time a civilian airliner has since been shot down over a conflict zone.

50 years ago1976A currency crisis · a first at the anchor desk · a faster train
BUSINESS1976Sep 28–30, 1976

Britain's finance chief turns back at Heathrow as the pound crashes, forcing an emergency loan request

On September 28, 1976, the pound sterling fell to its lowest-ever value against the US dollar, and Denis Healey, Britain's Chancellor of the Exchequer and senior finance minister, abandoned a trip to an International Monetary Fund (IMF) meeting in Manila from the tarmac at Heathrow Airport and returned to the Treasury instead, judging the crisis too severe to leave the country. Two days later, on September 30, he traveled to the governing Labour Party's annual conference in Blackpool to defend the government's decision in person against furious delegates who saw the coming request for emergency funding as a surrender to foreign creditors.

The confrontation became one of the defining images of 1970s British politics: a cabinet minister publicly overruling his own party's instincts because, as Healey told the hall, the alternative to accepting outside help was letting the opposition Conservative Party take power and cut public spending even more deeply than his own government proposed.

Context & background. Britain's economy had struggled with high inflation and a persistent trade deficit since the 1973 oil shock, when major oil-producing nations cut output and prices spiked worldwide, and by mid-1976 investors had lost confidence that the pound could hold its value, selling it steadily through the summer even as Prime Minister James Callaghan's government tried public spending cuts to reassure markets. Healey, as Chancellor of the Exchequer, was the minister ultimately responsible for negotiating any outside help.

The details. The Heathrow turnback on September 28 became instant political theatre: photographers captured Healey walking back through the terminal rather than boarding his flight, a visual shorthand for a currency in freefall. Two days later in Blackpool, delegates at the Labour Party's conference — many from the party's left — jeered proposals to seek help from the International Monetary Fund (IMF), arguing its conditions would force cuts to public services a Labour government should never accept. Healey stayed and argued the case anyway, telling the conference bluntly that rejecting the fund's terms would only hand power to the opposition. Negotiations over the exact conditions ran for weeks afterward and were not finalized until December 1976.

Why it still matters. The 1976 crisis became the reference point every subsequent British government has been measured against whenever the pound comes under pressure — including in September 2022, when Prime Minister Liz Truss's government announced a package of unfunded tax cuts that triggered comparisons to 1976 within days. It also marked a turning point in postwar British politics, widely credited with pushing the Labour government toward spending discipline that opponents later said paved the way for the free-market, privatization-driven policies of the 1980s.

CULTURE1976Oct 4, 1976

Barbara Walters becomes the first woman to co-anchor a nightly US network newscast

On October 4, 1976, Barbara Walters made her debut as co-anchor of the ABC (American Broadcasting Company) Evening News alongside Harry Reasoner, a veteran anchor who did not want to share the desk with her, becoming the first woman to anchor a major US network's nightly news broadcast. The network had lured her from NBC (National Broadcasting Company) with a five-year, reported $1-million-a-year contract — an unprecedented sum for a news anchor at the time that made headlines nearly as large as her appointment did.

Reasoner made his discomfort visible on air, and the pairing was largely judged a ratings disappointment; ABC (American Broadcasting Company) dissolved the co-anchor arrangement within two years. Walters went on to a much longer career as a host and interviewer, becoming one of American television's most recognized figures for decades afterward.

Context & background. Television news in the 1970s was an overwhelmingly male domain; no woman had ever anchored a nightly network newscast in the United States. Walters had spent more than a decade proving herself as an interviewer and reporter on NBC (National Broadcasting Company)'s morning program before ABC (American Broadcasting Company) offered to make her the first female co-anchor of an evening newscast, hoping to close the ratings gap with its rivals.

The details. The reported $1-million salary — roughly equivalent to several million dollars today — was covered almost as heavily as the appointment itself, with critics and even some colleagues questioning whether any news anchor, let alone a woman, deserved to be paid so much. Reasoner's on-air resentment became an open secret, and ABC (American Broadcasting Company) quietly split the pair in 1978, moving Walters to a newsmagazine format where her interviewing skills could shine without a reluctant co-anchor beside her.

Why it still matters. Though the co-anchor experiment itself is remembered as a failure, Walters's appointment is widely credited with opening the anchor chair to the generation of women broadcasters who followed her. The specialist interview format she built afterward — mixing celebrity access with pointed political questions in the same sitting — became a template that outlasted the evening-news job that first made her famous.

SCIENCE1976Oct 4, 1976

Britain puts the world's fastest diesel train into daily service at 125 miles an hour

On October 4, 1976, British Rail began running the InterCity 125 — officially the High Speed Train — in regular passenger service between London Paddington, Bristol and South Wales, reaching a top speed of 125 miles an hour and cutting journey times on some of the country's busiest inter-city routes by up to a third.

Designed as a stopgap until a more ambitious tilting "Advanced Passenger Train" was ready, a project later abandoned, the diesel-powered InterCity 125 instead became the workhorse of Britain's railways for decades, staying in front-line service on some routes for more than 40 years and still holding the record for the world's fastest diesel train on a conventional railway.

Context & background. British Rail had struggled through the 1960s and early 1970s with an inter-city network that felt slow next to newer high-speed lines opening in Japan and France, and had originally pinned its hopes on the Advanced Passenger Train, an experimental tilting design meant to run even faster on Britain's older, curvier tracks. When that project ran years behind schedule, engineers led by Terry Miller adapted an existing diesel locomotive design into a simpler, more conservative high-speed train that could be built and running years sooner.

The details. The InterCity 125's first public week of 125-mile-an-hour running, in early October 1976, covered the routes from London to Bristol, through the Severn Tunnel to Swansea, and via Chipping Sodbury — the same stretches a fiftieth-anniversary heritage railtour was set to retrace in October 2026. The trains proved reliable enough that British Rail and its successor operators kept ordering more of them well into the 1980s, eventually building 197 power cars — the locomotive units at each end of the train — in total.

Why it still matters. Decades after the tilting train it was meant to be a stopgap for was cancelled, the InterCity 125 remained in daily passenger service on some British routes until 2019, and derivative High Speed Train sets still run in Britain and abroad today — a rare case of an interim technical fix outliving the more ambitious programme it was built to cover for.

Editor's view

Every era this week produced a shock that forced an immediate, improvised response rather than a planned one: Denis Healey turning back from Heathrow because the pound could not wait for him to fly to Manila; a Ukrainian air-defense exercise that killed 78 people it was never aimed at; a Colombian electorate voting down a deal its own negotiators believed was already settled. None of these stories ends where its anniversary week does — the loan was finalized months later, the peace deal was rewritten and ratified without a second vote, the airline dispute over responsibility took until 2003 to reach even a partial settlement. The week is as much a set of starting points as a set of endings.
