Markets & Macro · Weekly

Wall Street's most violent week in months ends narrowly higher: a hawkish Fed hold and Iran's first direct missile attack on US forces since the truce triggered the worst single session since April, before Big Tech's earnings split cleanly into AI's monetizers and its spenders — while Seoul's chipmakers crashed a second time before posting the biggest one-day rally in Kospi history.

Last week's edition closed with two dated events still pending and a geopolitical pause still unsigned: the Fed's 29 July rate decision, the Microsoft-Meta-Apple-Amazon earnings gauntlet, and a US-Iran ceasefire built on Oman-mediated talks rather than a deal. All three resolved this week, and none of them resolved quietly. Wednesday brought a genuinely hawkish Fed — a split vote, three dissents pushing for a hike, and a chair still refusing forward guidance — landing the same day Iran launched a direct missile attack on US forces; together they produced Wall Street's worst single session in well over a year. Thursday and Friday's earnings then delivered the clearest verdict yet on which hyperscalers are turning AI spending into cash and which are still burning it, in reactions violent enough to erase Wednesday's losses entirely. And Seoul's Kospi, hit by a second circuit breaker mid-week, capped its worst month since the AI boom began with the single largest one-day gain in the index's history.

Semiconductors · KospiTue 28 – Fri 31 Jul

Seoul's Kospi crashes to a second circuit breaker, then stages the single largest one-day rally in its history — and still closes July down 22%

After Tuesday's 10.84% CXMT-driven crash flagged in last week's edition, South Korea's sell-off deepened dramatically on Wednesday: panic, leverage-fuelled selling triggered an unprecedented second consecutive circuit breaker, dragging the Kospi as low as 5,262.77. Regulators moved fast — Finance Minister Koo Yun-cheol convened an emergency market-monitoring meeting, and South Korea capped individual investment in single-stock leveraged ETFs at 20% of an investor's total portfolio, while insisting the country's underlying fundamentals were sound. The index fell a further 1.2% Thursday even as Wall Street rallied hard, before Friday brought the reversal: Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix both surged more than 26%, their largest single-day percentage gains on record, and the Kospi itself jumped 17.91% to 6,595.45 — the biggest one-day gain in the index's history. Even after that historic rally, the Kospi still finished July down roughly 22% for the month, having more than doubled in the year's first half.

Context & background: last week's edition covered Tuesday's 10.84% Kospi drop as a shock triggered by China's CXMT memory-chip IPO; this week's sourcing shows that shock had a second, sharper leg the very next day. South Korean financial authorities are separately investigating whether foreign high-frequency traders amplified the swings — program trading had nearly doubled since the launch of leveraged single-stock ETFs tied to Samsung and SK Hynix, a mechanism this briefing flagged two weeks ago as a source of mechanical amplification.

The details: the leveraged-ETF cap (20% of an investor's total portfolio in any single-stock leveraged product) was announced alongside the emergency meeting, and Korea Investment's CEO had already been warning investors to stop betting on these products, noting they "can inflict losses even when the underlying shares eventually recover" — a caution vindicated within days as leverage-driven selling forced the week's second circuit breaker, an event without recent precedent for the Kospi. Friday's rebound was driven by two catalysts landing almost simultaneously: Samsung and SK Hynix's own quarterly earnings, and Wall Street's Thursday rally on Microsoft's blowout results (see below), which read as confirmation that hyperscaler AI demand for memory remains intact regardless of the CXMT competitive threat.

Context: a week that opens with a circuit-breaker crash and closes with a record rally, in the same index, is not a market finding its level — it is a market whose price discovery briefly broke down under leverage, and the 20% leveraged-ETF cap is the clearest evidence regulators agree. The month's arithmetic is unforgiving regardless of Friday's headline: the Kospi is down roughly a third from its earlier-July highs even after the best day in its history, meaning "record rebound" describes velocity, not a recovery of value.

Watch-Items: whether the foreign-HFT investigation into leveraged-ETF-driven volatility produces findings or further restrictions. Whether Samsung and SK Hynix hold Friday's gains once the earnings-driven relief fades. Whether the 20% leveraged-ETF cap dampens future single-day swings or merely shifts leverage into unregulated channels. And whether CXMT's competitive threat, the original trigger, resurfaces once the memory-market story stops being overshadowed by US hyperscaler earnings.

Big Tech · Microsoft/MetaWed 29 – Thu 30 Jul

Microsoft's best day in 18 years and Meta's worst give Wall Street its first clean answer on AI capex — and a rally big enough to erase the Fed's damage

Microsoft and Meta reported Wednesday after the close, delivering opposite verdicts on the question this briefing has tracked for a month: whether AI capex reads as demand evidence or a warning sign. Microsoft beat on revenue and profit, with Azure growth accelerating and CEO Satya Nadella framing it as customers moving into AI at scale — and, crucially, without announcing a further increase to its already-elevated spending plans. The stock leapt 15.5% Thursday, its best single day since 2008. Meta told the opposite story: profit missed estimates even as revenue landed slightly ahead, and the company raised the low end of its 2026 capex forecast — precisely the "beat plus heavier spending" combination that punished Alphabet the week before. Meta fell roughly 8%. The Thursday rally that followed was broad enough to erase Wednesday's entire Fed-driven selloff: the S&P rallied 1.7%, the Dow gained 613 points and the Nasdaq jumped 2.8%, with chip suppliers Micron (+18.4%) and Lam Research (+18%) among the biggest beneficiaries.

Context & background: last week's edition called this pair of earnings "a strictly larger version" of the Alphabet/Tesla test, noting Microsoft's planned capex trajectory ($97.2B to $118.9B) was smaller in percentage terms than Meta's ($75.8B to $135.2B, +79%). That asymmetry predicted almost exactly what happened: the company with the more moderate spending increase and clearer monetization story was rewarded, while the company with the steeper spending raise and a profit miss was punished, even though Meta's revenue technically beat.

The details: Microsoft's Azure growth and Nadella's explicit framing of the results as evidence that "customers are using Microsoft to move into AI" gave investors a monetization story to attach to the capex, not just a spending number — analysts read the absence of a fresh capex hike as almost as important as the beat itself. Meta's forecast, by contrast, effectively pre-announced heavier spending on the same call where it missed profit expectations, a combination that reads to markets as capex without a matching revenue signal. The rally's breadth — chip suppliers Micron and Lam Research posting their own double-digit gains — shows markets read Microsoft's results as confirmation of continued hyperscaler memory and equipment demand broadly, not a Microsoft-specific story.

Context: this is the cleanest single data point yet in the month-long "AI spending versus returns" debate this briefing has tracked through Alphabet, Tesla and now these two: the market is not against AI capex per se, it is pricing capex against a demonstrated monetization path, name by name. That the Thursday rally fully erased Wednesday's Fed-driven selloff also says something about relative weighting: for one session, an earnings-and-AI signal outweighed a monetary-policy signal.

Watch-Items: whether Microsoft's Azure growth proves durable through the next quarter or was flattered by one-off enterprise migrations. Whether Meta's raised capex floor keeps rising in subsequent quarters, repeating the pattern that punished Alphabet. Whether Thursday's chip-supplier rally holds or fades once earnings season ends, given it partly reflects a rebound from Kospi-driven oversold levels. And whether the market keeps rewarding "spend less, monetize now" over "spend more, monetize later" through Apple's and Amazon's reports the very next day.

Big Tech · Apple/AmazonThu 30 – Fri 31 Jul

Amazon's profit triples and AWS posts its fastest growth in 18 quarters; Apple beats on paper but falls 7.4% on Tim Cook's final earnings call as CEO

A day after Microsoft and Meta split, Apple and Amazon repeated the pattern. Amazon's revenue rose 20% to $200.6B, comfortably ahead of estimates, while profit more than tripled to $62.65B; AWS revenue grew 37% to $42.2B, nearly $2B above expectations and its fastest growth in 18 quarters. It was, notably, Tim Cook's final earnings call as Apple's chief executive; he closed it with a warning about a "hundred-year flood" in memory-chip pricing.

Fed · FOMCWed 29 Jul

The Fed holds rates 9–3, but three dissents and no forward guidance send the Dow to its worst day since April — before Iran's missile attack even hit the tape

The FOMC voted 9 to 3 on Wednesday to hold its target range at 3.50–3.75%, but the dissent itself was the story: Cleveland Fed President Beth Hammack, Minneapolis Fed President Neel Kashkari and Dallas Fed President Lorie Logan each voted for a 25-basis-point hike, an unusually large hawkish dissent bloc for a hold decision. Chair Kevin Warsh again declined to give forward guidance, saying he wants markets' "unfiltered" reaction rather than an echo of Fed hints, and noted tightening financial conditions were already doing some of the Fed's job. Markets took that as confirmation the Fed isn't done tightening in spirit even if it held in practice: Treasury yields spiked, with the 30-year yield reaching its highest level since 2007, and stocks fell hard — the S&P dropped 1.52%, the Nasdaq 1.74%, and the Dow roughly 1,100 points (−2.19%) to 51,594.14, its worst single session since April 2025.

Context & background: last week's edition flagged a wide gap between Polymarket's hold odds (73–78%) and rising hike-probability pricing (20–27%), reading that divergence as genuine uncertainty rather than noise. The outcome split the difference unusually: the Committee held, exactly as the majority of positioned money expected, but the size and seniority of the hawkish dissent — three regional Fed presidents, including two of the more market-influential voices in Hammack and Kashkari — validated the minority's hike bet almost as strongly as an actual hike would have.

The details: Warsh's press conference reaffirmed the 2% inflation target while explicitly declining to explain the reasoning behind holding rather than hiking — a combination Bank of America's economists, in a note cited this week, called a "growing credibility problem," arguing the Fed needs to "pass the September test" by hiking and delivering an internally consistent narrative if data doesn't improve. The 30-year Treasury yield's move — from roughly 5.09% before the meeting to 5.22% by Thursday's close — is a genuinely large multi-day repricing for the long end of the curve, and it has already pushed the average 30-year mortgage rate to its highest level in a year.

Context: a Fed that holds while three officials publicly dissent for a hike, while its chair refuses to explain why, is a Fed that has effectively let the bond market set policy expectations rather than setting them itself — precisely the dynamic Warsh described when he said tightening financial conditions were "doing some of the Fed's job." That the S&P recovered its entire Wednesday loss by Thursday's close, on Microsoft's earnings rather than any Fed news, is itself informative: markets treated Wednesday's selloff as a policy-uncertainty shock, reversible the moment a clear earnings signal arrived.

Watch-Items: whether incoming inflation data before the September meeting supports the dissenters' case for a hike, per Bank of America's framing. Whether the 30-year yield's climb toward multi-decade highs keeps pressuring mortgage rates and housing activity. Whether Warsh's no-guidance approach persists or comes under political pressure, given President Trump's public preference for lower rates. And whether Wednesday's Fed-driven selloff proves fully absorbed, or resurfaces once earnings season's distraction fades.

Iran · HormuzTue 28 Jul – ongoing

Iran's ceasefire cracks: Tehran launches a ballistic-missile attack on US forces, all intercepted, and oil swings from a two-week low back above $90

The fragile US-Iran pause flagged in last week's edition did not survive the week. On Tuesday, Iranian Revolutionary Guard Corps forces launched multiple ballistic missiles at US forces in the Middle East at 5:45pm ET in what CENTCOM called an "attempted surprise attack"; every missile was intercepted, and no casualties were reported. The US reinstated its naval blockade of Iranian ports near the Strait of Hormuz, and President Trump threatened Iran with a "beating" if attacks continued. Oil, which had fallen toward $79–82 on the prior pause and Oman-mediated talks, reversed sharply: WTI jumped 4.4% to $82.73 immediately on the interception news, and Brent spiked back above $90 the next day on Trump's threat, before settling in the high $80s by Friday ($87.93, +1.2%) as the immediate escalation risk cooled without resolving.

Context & background: last week's edition described the US-Iran pause as the first concrete de-escalation step in nearly two weeks of strikes, while flagging that it addressed only the direct US-Iran exchange and not the separate Houthi threat to Saudi shipping. This week shows how fragile that framing was: the pause lasted roughly three days before Iran itself initiated a direct attack on US forces, a materially more serious escalation than the Houthi tanker strikes that drove July's earlier price spikes, since it targets US personnel directly rather than third-country shipping.

The details: CENTCOM's "all missiles intercepted, no casualties" framing let markets treat the event as a contained escalation rather than an opening salvo, which is why oil's reaction, while sharp, stayed within July's established $72–102 range rather than setting new highs. The US response — reinstating the naval blockade near Hormuz rather than immediate retaliatory strikes — reads as calibrated pressure rather than open-ended escalation, though Trump's public "beating" threat the next day shows the diplomatic track (the Oman-mediated Hormuz talks flagged last week) has not replaced the military one.

Context: oil's month-long round trip — from roughly $72 early in July, to $101–102 on the Houthi strikes, back down to $79–82 on the pause, and now bouncing between $83–90 on this attack — describes a market that has stopped trying to price a resolution and started pricing a range. That is itself a shift from earlier in the conflict, when each escalation or de-escalation moved the whole curve; now, each new event moves price within bounds the market has already learned to expect from this specific conflict.

Watch-Items: whether Iran's attack was a one-off show of capability or the start of a new escalation cycle, per Trump's explicit threat of retaliation for further attacks. Whether the Oman-mediated Hormuz talks continue in parallel with renewed military exchanges, or collapse entirely. Whether gasoline prices, already up from $3.85 to roughly $4.11 a gallon nationally over the past month, continue climbing into August. And whether the naval blockade near Hormuz becomes a new flashpoint independent of the missile exchange.

lightbulbEditor's view

The market found a dividing line for AI spending this week — and it isn't the spending total

1. AI capex now has a name for its dividing line, and it isn't the spending total. Microsoft and Amazon spend tens of billions and were rewarded; Meta spends similarly and was punished for a profit miss; Apple, the lightest AI spender of the four, was punished anyway for a supply-chain-driven guidance miss. The market is pricing monetization evidence, not spending discipline.

2. Korea's leverage problem is now systemic, not sector-specific. Two circuit breakers and a record rebound inside one week, in an index dominated by two stocks, is a market-structure story as much as an AI story — and the 20% leveraged-ETF cap is regulators' own admission of that.

3. The Fed's credibility gap widened, not narrowed. A 9–3 hold with three hawkish dissents and no forward guidance produced the worst Dow session since April 2025 — a reaction that reads less like disagreement with the decision than uncertainty about what the Fed will do next.

4. Iran's ceasefire was a pause, not a resolution — and this week proved it. A direct missile attack on US forces, intercepted without casualties, shows both sides still willing to test the line even while diplomatic talks continue in parallel.

5. Crypto's insulation from equity chaos partially returned. Last week's edition flagged Bitcoin's decoupling cracking under the Kospi shock; this week, even a hawkish Fed and a real missile attack didn't move it out of a narrow range — suggesting last week's dip was a Kospi-specific event, not a durable new correlation.

6. Washington's crypto clock and Wall Street's earnings clock are visibly out of sync. The CLARITY Act's window is closing on a legislative calendar — an unrelated Graham tribute bill has floor priority — that has nothing to do with crypto's market fundamentals, a reminder that policy timing risk doesn't track market-attention cycles.

Friday's rally leaves US equities with a narrow weekly gain despite the sharpest three-day round trip in months. Whether Wednesday's Fed-and-Iran shock or Thursday-Friday's earnings relief better describes the next two weeks likely depends on two open questions: whether Iran's attack proves a one-off or the start of a new cycle, and whether Wednesday's dissenting Fed voices gain company before the September meeting.
