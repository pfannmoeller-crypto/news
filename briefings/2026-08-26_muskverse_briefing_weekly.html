The 19 August edition closed with the week's outcome still open: a Minnesota hearing on xAI's nudification-law challenge had happened, but no ruling had been reported. Nevada clears Tesla for up to 2,500 robotaxis as the Cybercab production reveal slides to 3 September

The Nevada Transportation Authority approved Tesla on 21 August to operate commercial robotaxis in Clark County (the Las Vegas area), authorizing deployment of up to roughly 2,500 Cybercabs within a fleet cap of 8,000 autonomous vehicles — putting Tesla alongside Uber and Waymo in the market, and building on Robotaxi service Tesla already runs there with modified Model Y vehicles. The news drove a sharp rally: TSLA jumped 5.14% on 23 August to close at $362.86. Separately, Tesla now says the Cybercab's production-version reveal event is set for 3 September in Austin, Texas — later and more specific than the "as soon as this month" employee messaging this briefing reported on 19 August. By 25 August the rally had partly cooled, with TSLA closing at $350.25 and the tracked analyst consensus at Buy, $390.09 (+11.4% upside) — down slightly from the $395.34 target reported a week earlier.

Context & background. The 19 August edition reported Tesla telling employees a driverless Cybercab launch was coming "as soon as this month," sourced only to an aggregator citing Reuters and The Information. This window sharpens and partly revises that: the concrete, dated event is a 3 September production-version reveal, not necessarily a public robotaxi launch this month — a more specific claim than last week's, but also a later one.

A state permit plus a firm reveal date are more concrete than last week's employee-messaging leak, and they land in the same week Tesla also picked up its first confirmed China recall (next card) — meaning the regulatory story this window cuts in both directions for Tesla, not only positively.

China orders a recall of roughly 3 million Tesla vehicles over door-handle and camera defects

China's State Administration for Market Regulation (SAMR) announced a recall affecting nearly 3 million Tesla vehicles, targeting emergency door-handle mechanisms and safety cameras, according to reporting captured alongside this window's Tesla stock coverage. China is one of Tesla's largest single markets, and a recall at this scale — covering door-handle and camera systems rather than a software-only fix — is a meaningfully larger action than a routine over-the-air update.

Grok Bot's beta shares one cloud computer across all of a user's bots — and Grok 4.7's training data has no disclosed opt-out for SpaceX staff

A trade-press retrospective surfaced two xAI product-governance details this briefing had not previously reported: Grok Bot's documentation reportedly discloses that, despite marketing suggesting individually isolated bots, "all of your Bots use the same persistent cloud computer" per account — meaning credentials and files are shared across a user's entire bot roster. Separately, Musk is reported to have said Grok 4.7 will train on "the sum total of all SpaceX information" from an estimated 14,000–15,000 employees, with no disclosed opt-out mechanism — a sharper and more specific claim than the "massive amount of SpaceX engineering data" framing this briefing reported on 19 August. Both facts date to the 11–19 August window rather than this one; this briefing did not have them last week and flags that gap here.

edit_noteEditor's view

A week of real progress on two fronts and real silence on two others

1. The Minnesota non-ruling is now the week's most consequential fact, by default. A week without a decision means Minnesota's $500,000-per-violation nudification law keeps operating exactly as written, with no injunction — the practical stakes favor the state regardless of how Judge Frank eventually rules.

2. Tesla's regulatory story cuts both ways this window. Nevada's robotaxi approval and a dated Cybercab reveal are genuine forward progress; a roughly 3-million-vehicle China recall, surfacing in the same window, is a genuine setback this briefing could only source thinly.

3. SpaceX's Cursor deal has gone from a financial transaction to an organizational fact. A named division, an expanding Grok Bot beta, and a bank's first revenue projection make the acquisition look more like a real integration than a one-off dilutive purchase — though the "SpaceXAI" name itself needs independent confirmation.

4. This briefing found a genuine reporting gap from last week and is naming it rather than quietly folding it in. Grok Bot's shared-compute architecture and Grok 4.7's employee-data training scope both date to before 19 August; they were not in that edition, and this one flags the gap explicitly rather than presenting them as brand-new.

5. Starship's hardware-reliability story keeps diverging from SpaceX's corporate momentum. A month-long silence on the Flight 13 booster failure, alongside a confidently expanding Cursor integration, is the same split this briefing has now flagged for two consecutive editions.

6. Away from Tesla, SpaceX and the Minnesota case, this was a genuinely quiet week. Politics, Boring Company, Neuralink and X Money produced no independently verifiable new developments — this briefing says so rather than manufacturing items to fill sections.

The next edition should carry: the Minnesota ruling, if it lands; confirmation or denial of the "SpaceXAI" branding and organizational structure; whether Starship Flight 14 gets an official SpaceX-announced date; and independent corroboration of this window's China recall.
