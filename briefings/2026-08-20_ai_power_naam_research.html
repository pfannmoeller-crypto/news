Ramez Naam on powering AI: the grid is the bottleneck, and 200 gigawatts are already sitting there

A clean-energy investor walks a podcast through seven ways to get electricity to AI data centres — from rewiring how the existing grid is used, through solar and nuclear, to computers on buoys and in orbit. His central move is to reframe the problem: electricity is cheap, but the wait for a connection is everything. This piece follows his argument, checks the numbers, and puts the options side by side on the one measure that matters.

1

His argument in brief

Stated as strongly as he would state it himself.

  • Electricity is not an AI cost problem. It is an AI schedule problem. On a gigawatt-scale build, chips dominate capital cost so completely that five years of power is a rounding error. A hyperscaler offered power at twice the price tomorrow takes it without hesitating. What it cannot buy is time.
  • The binding constraint is poles and wires, not generation. The United States can still build solar, wind, batteries and gas quickly. What it can no longer do quickly is connect things. Utilities have been re-engineered around regulatory compliance rather than speed, and permitting sits with states, counties and the federal government at once.
  • Roughly 200 GW of usable capacity already exists inside the grid — the difference between average and peak load. It is stranded because everything is designed for a peak that occurs a few hours a year. Make a load flexible for a hundred hours annually and much of that gap opens up.
  • That, not new generation, is where the near-term returns are, and it is badly undervalued because it is unglamorous. Texas changed its rules in June 2026 and cut multi-year waits to a year or so for interruptible loads.
  • Solar plus batteries is now genuinely competitive for round-the-clock power in sunny places, and it is the fastest thing anyone can build — twelve months, against gas turbines sold out for years.
  • Learning rates, not calendars, drive cost. Costs fall with cumulative volume, which is why factory-built things get cheap and construction does not. This is the argument for small modular reactors and against large bespoke ones — and it is also why he says solar has perhaps four to eight more doublings of cost decline in it, not a thousandfold.
  • Fission is expensive because the West does it rarely. Nothing done occasionally is cheap. China and Korea are cheaper because they build continuously.
  • Fusion is no longer a joke, but it is still a bet. The regulatory treatment changed decisively in its favour; the timelines announced by startups will slip; some of these companies will fail outright, and some may succeed and still be too expensive.
  • Space and ocean compute are real hedges against regulation, not near-term answers. On his own arithmetic, a single gigawatt in orbit needs several times SpaceX's best launch year.
  • The largest unlock is one nobody can forecast: algorithms that do more with less. A brain runs on 20 watts. Scaling was never the efficient path, only the predictable one.

A framework for judging any of these claims

Most arguments in this field are won or lost on which number gets quoted. Five axes have to be stated together, or a comparison is meaningless. Four of them Naam uses himself; the fifth is the one his conversation never reaches.

AxisThe question it forcesWhere claims usually break
1. Time-to-powerMonths from decision to electronsIgnored entirely in cost comparisons. Naam's central and strongest point.
2. Firm capex, $/WPer watt delivered around the clock, not per watt of nameplateThe most abused number in the debate. A gigawatt of solar is not a gigawatt of gas.
3. Delivered cost, $/MWhAt the availability the buyer actually needsHeadline LCOE usually assumes no firming and no curtailment.
4. Doublings remainingHow much learning-curve headroom is actually leftPast cost declines get extrapolated forward without asking how many doublings the market can still supply.
5. Incidence — who paysWhose bill absorbs the cost of accommodating the loadAbsent from this conversation. It is the axis with the hardest available numbers.
Axis 2 in one example, because it explains most of the confusion. The Abu Dhabi solar-plus-storage project is 5.2 GW of panels and 19 GWh of batteries for about $6.1bn, contracted to deliver 1 GW continuously. Divide by nameplate and you get $1.17 per watt. Divide by firm output and you get $6.10 per watt. Both are true. Only the second can be compared with a nuclear plant — and Naam does use the firm figure, correctly. Almost everyone quoting him does not.

Axis 4 is the one that disciplines the optimism, and Naam applies it against his own field — while describing his own method with unusual flatness: "I'm one of the top five forecasters of solar costs in the world. And it's not because I'm that smart. It's because I came out of tech and I came from a Moore's law world and came into energy and just applied Moore's law to it." Solar follows Wright's law, roughly 30 % cost decline per cumulative doubling. He counts four to six doublings left in the plausible market, which implies a further four- to eightfold decline — not another thousandfold. That is a striking piece of self-limitation from the person best known for the solar cost curve, and it should survive into any summary of what he said.

Axis 5 is my addition, and it is where the episode has a blind spot. The entire conversation treats power as a procurement problem for the buyer. Nobody asks who absorbs the cost of accommodating a 500 MW load on a shared network. That question has an answer with real numbers in it, and it appears in the walkthrough below.

3

The argument, walked through

One — AI is power hungry, but not power poor

"If you look at building a gigawatt data center you're going to spend $50 billion, 35 billion of that for chips… When you say AI is power hungry, it's not really a cost issue. It is that energy is the bottleneck for AI." Ramez Naam

The move here is to separate two things that get bundled together. Energy is a bottleneck in the sense that a shortage of it stops the project — but not in the sense that its price changes the economics. His illustration is blunt: tell OpenAI or Anthropic they can have power at twice the price starting tomorrow and they take it, because the revenue per unit of electricity dwarfs its cost. They will not say so publicly, because every hyperscaler runs teams whose job is to shave that cost. But the ratio decides.

Check — the $50bn / $35bn split mostly true Epoch AI puts total capex for a 1 GW AI data centre at around $38bn; industry breakdowns put servers and accelerators at 60–70 % of capex, so $25–35bn for chips is the right shape. Five years of electricity at market rates lands in the low single-digit billions — a small fraction, as claimed. The exact pairing $50bn/$35bn is not traceable to a single published source. C 3/5

Underneath sits a stronger claim, drawn out by Alex Wissner-Gross: that intelligence is roughly logarithmic in compute, so exponentially more energy buys linearly more capability. Naam accepts the framing and then complicates it in his own favour and against it at once — algorithmic progress keeps bending the curve, so it is better than log-linear, but still somewhere between a steep power law and a true exponential in difficulty. Asked whether recursive self-improvement changes this, he does not hedge:

"Every model of RSI in software fizzles over time… There is no mathematical model of RSI that's valid that I can see that leads to an actual, like, vertical asymptote take-off." Ramez Naam

Worth noticing how unfashionable that is in the room he is in. He is on an abundance-themed podcast, and he uses his slot to say the intelligence explosion does not close.

He goes further later, and it is the sharpest thing he says all episode. Pressed by Wissner-Gross on whether AI systems are becoming autonomous agents, he declines the premise:

"I do not see AI as a being and I do not see it as particularly volitional… We anthropomorphize these things… AI models don't actually have a built-in desire to even survive." Ramez Naam

His reasoning is evolutionary rather than technical: our drives to survive, propagate and dominate are products of hundreds of millions of years of being animals, and language models mimic our language and logic without inheriting any of that. He immediately supplies the counterweight — he is, he says, not a carbon chauvinist, digital intelligence has every reason to surpass us, and if we wanted to build actual beings we probably could. He also flags that he can be persuaded by evidence and knows the view is unpopular. It is a position held with stated conditions, not a dismissal.

Two — speed to power, and why the queue is the whole story

"If you put in a request for a hundreds of megawatts of power to build a data center today, good luck getting that power before 2031, 2032." Ramez Naam, on ERCOT — the fastest-moving grid in the United States

He is careful about which queue he means. Generation interconnection — plugging in a new power plant — has stretched from around fifteen months two decades ago to something approaching forty-five. The load side, where data centres sit, has worse data but the same direction: three American locations he shows added six or seven months of waiting in a seven-month window. The cause is two-part and neither part is villainous. Demand growth slowed for decades, so utilities reorganised themselves around customer service and regulatory compliance rather than speed. And permitting is fragmented across land controlled by counties, states and the federal government.

Check — 15 months to 45 months misleading as stated The direction is right and well documented; the specific numbers are not the Berkeley Lab metric. LBNL's "Queued Up" series measures typical duration from request to commercial operation rising from under two years for projects built 2000–2007 to over four years for 2018–2023, with a five-year median for projects completed in 2023. Naam's figures are in the right region but should not be cited as LBNL's. C 3/5
Check — ERCOT at 80 GW peak against a 200 GW queue mostly true, and conservative ERCOT's summer peak runs nearer 85–90 GW. The large-load queue stood at roughly 238 GW in March 2026, with further filings pushing toward 380 GW — so "well over 200" understates it. His own caveat holds up too: only about 9 GW has approval to energize and observed large-load consumption is around 3.9 GW, which is exactly the picture of a queue stuffed with speculative filings that he describes. C 4/5

He does not treat the speculative filings as fraud, but as congestion. Anyone can file without financing or a customer; most of it evaporates; and in the meantime the operator is buried. That is a systems observation rather than a complaint about bad actors, and it survives checking.

Other views — is it really the wires? Mostly supported, with a refinement. The World Economic Forum frames grid connectivity as the strategic bottleneck in the same terms. A more granular reading breaks it into three sub-constraints — high-voltage transformers, switchgear and grid-side batteries, with substation lead times of three to five years. That is a friendly amendment rather than a rebuttal.

Negative finding worth stating: I looked for a named expert arguing the opposite — that generation, turbines or skilled labour is the real bottleneck — and did not find one. Gas turbine lead times get treated as part of the same complex rather than as a competing explanation.

Three — behind the meter, because waiting is worse

If the grid makes you wait, build your own. The default is a large gas turbine, and here the constraint moves rather than disappears: several-hundred-megawatt units are sold out for roughly seven years. So buyers move down the size curve to 38 MW units mounted on trailers — forty of them make a gigawatt — and even those have backlogs. Naam's example of how far the pivot has spread is Boom Supersonic adapting its engine design into a gas turbine for data centres, because the demand is that intense.

Check — turbines sold out seven years true Lead times for large H/J-class turbines run five to seven years; GE Vernova's backlog stands at around 116 GW with slots booked through 2029–2030. C 4/5 — The Boom Supersonic pivot could not be confirmed. unverifiable C 2/5

Alex Wissner-Gross presses the obvious question twice: if power is the overhang on Nvidia's ability to sell GPUs, why is Nvidia not in the power business? Naam's answer is that the incentives are already large enough without them: behind-the-meter economics are so good that Nvidia does not have to, though it might invest in some of the companies doing it. (Wissner-Gross then offers the margin explanation — that power generation is simply lower-margin than chips — and Naam neither adopts nor rejects it.) What he would do instead is change the incentive structure for regulated utilities, which are paid a percentage above capital spend:

"Utilities instead of just getting paid for a percentage over capex, they should be paid on things like how fast they can deliver power. Their executives should get bonuses for delivering power fast." Ramez Naam

Also in this stretch, and easy to miss, is his most falsifiable near-term prediction. Nvidia's moat, he argues, was never the silicon — AMD's chips are comparable and Huawei is closing — but CUDA, the software layer. And he thinks it is going: "the CUDA moat is broken this year and next year", partly because AI can now recompile code onto rival hardware. He names a portfolio company working on it, whose name the transcript renders unrecognisably. That is a dated, checkable call on a company worth trillions, and it is worth returning to in 2027.

He follows the utilities point with an admission that doubles as evidence: in his own deal flow he has seen only two or three startups working on speeding up the building of poles and wires, and none he thought were good. Not because the problem is unsolvable, but because nobody is paid to solve it.

Four — the 200 gigawatts already in the wires

This is the centre of the argument and the part he is most emphatic about. American grids average around 500 GW of demand, dropping to roughly 400 GW on winter nights and rising to about 600 GW on summer afternoons. Everything — transmission lines, substations, transformers — is sized for the top of that range. The gap is real capacity that exists and goes unused.

"If we just used the poles and wires more efficiently, we could power up a lot of stuff because we're not short on generation. We're not short on power plants. We are short on capacity in the poles and wires." Ramez Naam
Check — the load numbers, and which way the error runs unverified, and probably understated I could not confirm the 400/500/600 GW figures against EIA series within budget. One data point suggests US summer peaks have reached around 769 GW, well above the 600 GW he cites. If so, the headroom between average and peak is larger than his 200 GW, not smaller — the error runs against his own thesis. C 2/5

The intellectual anchor is a paper he praises without reservation — Tyler Norris and colleagues at Duke's Nicholas Institute, which he calls the best electricity paper of the year. His summary: be flexible for a hundred hours a year, about 1 % downtime, and roughly 100 GW of capacity opens up, worth some $5tn of data centre capex including chips.

Check — the Norris paper, precisely threshold mislabelled The paper gives a schedule, not a single number: 0.25 % curtailment unlocks 76 GW; 0.5 % unlocks 98 GW; 1.0 % unlocks 126 GW. Naam pairs "1 %" with "100 GW", which belongs to 0.5 %. Note the direction: at the 1 % he actually states, the paper says 126 GW — so his headline number is conservative even though his threshold is wrong.

The authors' own limits matter more than the mislabel. It is a system-level model built on historical load shapes, not a line-by-line congestion study, and it assumes reductions that are reliable and predictable. Practitioners have pushed back that interconnection is decided far more granularly than the model represents. C 5/5
Other views — will data centres actually flex? This is the strongest objection to his strongest section, and it is not technical. Latitude Media asked directly whether data centre flexibility is a "regulatory fiction": operators can throttle, but the value of uninterrupted compute exceeds almost any flexibility payment, and a grid operator left holding an unmet promise must buy expensive reserve.

Where the authoritative reference supports him: the money is moving as if flexibility is real. Emerald AI — founded by Varun Sivaram, correctly identified in the episode — has raised from Nvidia's venture arm alongside Eaton, GE Vernova, Siemens and In-Q-Tel, with pilots at utilities including AES, Constellation and NextEra, and a first deployment at an Nvidia site in Virginia. That is not proof the thesis works. It is evidence that serious counterparties are betting it does.
And here is axis five, which the conversation never reaches. PJM's capacity price rose from $77.78 to $136.53 per MWh between the 2025 and 2026 auctions, up 75 %. PJM's independent market monitor attributes about 63 % of that increase to data centres — roughly $9.3bn of additional consumer cost in a single auction, and $29.4bn across four.

This does not contradict anything Naam says. His claim is that power is cheap relative to GPUs, and it is. But "cheap for the buyer" and "cheap" are different statements, and the difference is landing on household bills in Ohio, Maryland and Virginia. In three and a half hours on the economics of powering AI, the question of who pays does not come up. C 4/5 — the monitor's attribution is contested by PJM itself; the auction figures are not

Five — solar, and the winter he does not skip

The cost history is the chart he is best known for: a watt of solar cost about $100 in 1975 and costs roughly 8 cents from China today, for a panel that is smaller, more durable and longer-lived. Batteries have fallen by a factor of fourteen since 2010. His claim for what comes next is sodium-ion, which he says could cut battery cost by a factor of ten.

Check — solar, lithium, and sodium solar and lithium: true Mid-1970s modules exceeded $100/W; current Chinese spot prices are $0.08–0.10/W. BloombergNEF puts lithium-ion pack prices down 93 % since 2010 to $108/kWh in 2025 — a 93 % fall is a factor of 14.3, so his figure is exact.

sodium at 10x: unsupported as a projection He says sodium-ion "could" cut battery cost tenfold, and separately that batteries will fall another 10x "ultimately" — both hedged, neither given a date. As a statement about today it is wrong by roughly fivefold: sodium-ion cells run $55–70/kWh against $105–108 for lithium, a factor of 1.7 to 1.8. As a claim about an eventual floor it is simply unevidenced rather than false. CATL's Naxtra reaches 175 Wh/kg in mass production and HiNa targets 8,000–10,000 cycles by 2027, so the technology is real and improving fast. C 4/5

On whether solar simply wins, he declines the easy answer and names the unsolved problem himself. Cost is not the obstacle and nor is night, because batteries handle a daily cycle and are still falling. Winter is the obstacle:

"In London, for instance, you get a sixteenth or seventeenth as much insolation in January as you do in June or July. So you're going to build out your solar plant by a factor of six or seven? No." Ramez Naam

The economics of seasonal storage are, as he puts it, battery capex divided by how many times it cycles — daily cycling amortises over thousands of uses, seasonal shifting over two. Compressed air, sand and power-to-gas exist as ideas; none is close. And electrifying heat makes it worse, roughly doubling UK winter demand. His conclusion is not a solar conclusion:

"I do believe nuclear is super useful, as are efforts to get seasonal storage, as are all of our efforts on fusion, as is advanced geothermal — especially for those places that are further from the equator." Ramez Naam
Other views — on extrapolating learning curves The Oxford Institute for Energy Studies has published a critical assessment of exactly this method, warning against straight extrapolation of solar and wind learning curves. On the winter gap, published modelling suggests winter output can fall to 20–30 % of summer and that heating loads can raise winter demand several-fold.

Negative finding: I could not find anyone engaging with Naam's specific concession. The literature exists; a direct exchange does not. His own statement of the problem is currently the sharpest version of it in this conversation.
Check — Abu Dhabi, and the nuclear comparison one figure is wrong, and it cuts against him The Masdar/EWEC project is confirmed: 5.2 GW solar, 19 GWh storage, about $6.1bn, contracted for 1 GW round the clock — so $6.10 per firm watt, as he says. It does not begin operating until 2027, so the 24/7 guarantee is contracted rather than demonstrated.

Vogtle at roughly $15/W is confirmed. But standardised Chinese plants come in nearer $2/W, not the $4/W he quotes — $4/W is closer to recent French builds. Correcting this makes nuclear look better than he presented it, and narrows the case for solar. Not every error here runs his way. C 4/5

Six — fission and fusion

His fission diagnosis is a single sentence with a lot of load on it: it is ruinously expensive outside China and perhaps Korea because we do it rarely, and nothing done occasionally is cheap. From that follows both the case for large reactors — stamp out enough AP1000s and the price falls — and the case for small modular ones, because factories learn faster than construction sites. He is precise about the mechanism and about the cost of ignoring it: the first unit of any new design runs over on time and budget, and an industry that stops building loses its supply chain, its crews and its engineers, and then everything gets more expensive.

He is also candid that he has been round this loop before — enthusiastic about SMRs fifteen years ago, less so later, cautiously hopeful again now — and that the sector is running hot. His example is Valar Atomics raising $1bn at a $6bn valuation without a working reactor.

Check — Valar, France, and a naming slip Valar: true A Sequoia-led $1bn round in August 2026 at $6bn, tripling a prior $2bn valuation, pre-operational. C 4/5
France: overstated — but not by him France has 56–57 reactors; asked whether it was "like 60", Naam said "something on that order", which is right. The 80 % figure that follows comes from a different speaker after a speaker change in the transcript, and Naam's own sentence resumes afterwards. The correct share is about 70 %. I had originally scored this against him; on checking the transcript it is not his number. C 4/5 — speaker attribution in an auto-transcript
Commonwealth Fusion is an MIT spin-out, not Harvard. A slip in passing, not an argument. C 5/5

On his own SMR timelines he is the sceptic in the room. Optimistic company projections cluster around 2030 to the early 2030s; he expects them to be missed, says plainly that he does not expect a commercial SMR in 2030 or 2031, and adds that the first units will not be cheap regardless. He also names his own uncertainty about the middle of the size range — designs like Natrium and the BWRX-300 that are neither large enough for scale economies nor small enough to be truly factory-built — while allowing they might turn out to be optimal.

Check — ITER, and what Commonwealth actually shrank the ITER figure is wrong by an order of magnitude ITER is designed for 500 MW of fusion power, not the "at least 5 GW" stated. The transcript is auto-generated and this may be a transcription artefact rather than his error, but as it stands the claim overstates by 10x — and it flatters the achievement of shrinking the design. Commonwealth's SPARC targets roughly 50–140 MW of fusion power and the planned ARC around 400 MW electric; the "600 MW" figure matches neither exactly. C 4/5
Check — the regulatory change, which is the real news true and consequential The NRC has moved commercial fusion out of the power-reactor framework and under byproduct-material rules — the same regime as radiological equipment — on the reasoning that fusion has no chain reaction and no decay-heat risk. Naam's framing of the distinction as "default on versus default off" is a fair summary of the physics. Helion's power purchase agreement with Microsoft for 50 MW targeted at 2028 is confirmed. C 5/5

Asked whether fusion being close means the problem is solved, he refuses the invitation twice over:

"All of these companies might fail. They might 100 % fail. And in addition to that, they might succeed but be too expensive. Just because your fuel source costs very little doesn't mean your energy will cost little." Ramez Naam

Seven — orbit and ocean

He places himself explicitly in the middle of a polarised debate, and then does arithmetic that lands closer to the sceptics. One gigawatt of compute in orbit, on SpaceX's own design, takes roughly six times SpaceX's best launch year and twice its cumulative launch mass to date. Ten gigawatts a year is five or six Starship launches a day. Elon Musk's stated target of 100 GW a year implies something near 30,000 launches annually — a launch every fifteen minutes.

"Unless we hit that exponential absolutely full-on and go right down that path, this looks prohibitive for 15, 20 years." Ramez Naam

What he grants is the strategic logic rather than the near-term economics. Space compute is a hedge against terrestrial regulation, and — the more interesting point — AI demand may be the thing that finally finances the launch cadence Mars requires, because there is not enough communications demand on Earth to pay for it. He caps his own expectation precisely: if SpaceX has a single gigawatt in orbit by 2030, he will be impressed.

Other views — the heat problem Technical analysis is, if anything, harsher than he is. Radiating one megawatt of waste heat at around 300 K needs roughly 1,200 m² of radiator; a 250,000 m² radiator array implies 100–500 Starship flights on its own. Google's own Project Suncatcher estimate is over 50 dedicated launches for a 100 MW constellation.

The named dissent, for balance: engineer Sean Goedecke argues the cooling problem is overstated and more tractable than presented, and Starcloud already runs an H100 in orbit under immersion cooling — an existence proof at 700 watts. Economic parity is generally pegged to launch costs around $200–250/kg.

The public-opinion constraint runs underneath all of this, and he cites it in passing: "71 % of Americans are against data centers, which is a higher percentage than are against a nuclear plant in their backyard." Gallup's April 2026 polling puts opposition to nearby AI data centres at roughly 70 %, with 48 % strongly opposed — close enough to stand. The comparison with nuclear I could not verify directly. It is the reason his siting arguments keep pointing abroad: he describes advising the state of Chihuahua in Mexico to change a law capping private generation at half a megawatt, and names Australia — friendly government, space, world-class sun — as where he would build if he were starting now.

The ocean version is the same idea with better thermodynamics. Buoys off Antarctica-adjacent waters, where the strongest waves are, bobbing to drive a turbine, with free cooling from 4 °C seawater that appears to extend GPU life. He discloses the miss in his own words: "I'm an idiot because I said no to these guys five years ago, when they were raising a seed round. And I invested twice this year at much, much, much higher valuations."

Check — the wave-power company mostly confirmed Panthalassa, of Oregon, founded by Garth Sheldon-Coulson. A $140m Series B led by Peter Thiel at a valuation near $1bn in May 2026; target cost $0.02/kWh; connectivity via satellite rather than subsea cable. Naam's investment is confirmed by his own public post. The prior Bitcoin-mining history he mentions could not be confirmed. C 4/5

And the coda he calls the real unlock

"Scaling is not everything in AI. Scaling is just what we knew how to do… If you want to know what the biggest unlock that we cannot predict of AI and power will be — to learn new ways to manipulate information, to do more with less." Ramez Naam

A brain runs inference on 20 watts. He is careful not to make this a claim of human superiority — he says outright he is not a carbon chauvinist and that digital intelligence has every reason to surpass us — but rather a claim that current architectures are leaving efficiency on the table that evolution already found. It is the one place where the energy problem could dissolve rather than be solved, and he flags that he has no graph for it.

Three things fall out of this table that the conversation does not say.

One. On firm capex, the cheapest round-the-clock power on the board is not solar and not American — it is a Chinese nuclear plant at roughly $2 per firm watt, against $6.10 for the Abu Dhabi solar-and-storage project. Naam's own quoted figure of $4/W obscures this by half.

Two. Time-to-power and cost point in opposite directions almost everywhere. The cheapest firm option takes five to six years and cannot be built in the United States; the fastest options are the mobile turbines and the twelve-month solar build. If his axis-one argument is right — that schedule dominates — then the cost table is close to irrelevant, which is a stronger version of his own case than he makes.

Three. The Vogtle-to-Barakah-to-China spread of roughly seven-to-one is the single largest number in the table, and it is not a technology difference. Barakah unit 4 cost about 40 % of unit 1 on the same site with the same design. That is the learning-rate argument with the cleanest available evidence, and it is an argument for building repeatedly, whatever the technology.

Gaps I could not close: firm winter economics for northern Europe, per-dollar capacity unlocked by flexibility investment, and any independent costing of wave or orbital compute. The German Energy Efficiency Act requires data centres to run on 100 % renewable electricity from January 2027, which makes the missing winter figure a live commercial question rather than an academic one.

5

Stepping back

Which way the errors lean

Across thirteen checks the pattern is genuinely mixed, which is worth stating plainly because in earlier pieces in this series it has not been.

  • Leaning toward his thesis: the sodium-ion "10x", hedged as a possibility but roughly fivefold beyond today's 1.8x gap — the largest single overstatement, and the one that most flatters the storage case. The ITER figure, which makes fusion's compaction look more dramatic. The Texas and FERC actions, described as one movement when they are separate and ERCOT is not FERC-jurisdictional at all.
  • Leaning against his thesis: Chinese nuclear quoted at twice its actual cost, which understates his own competition. Summer peak load quoted at 600 GW when the real figure may be nearer 769, which understates the headroom his central argument depends on. ERCOT's queue described as "over 200 GW" when it is 238 and rising.
  • Conservative even where mislabelled: on the Duke paper he states a 1 % threshold and a 100 GW result. At 1 % the paper actually says 126 GW.

A speaker whose errors run in both directions is behaving differently from a speaker whose errors all run one way. Three or four favourable overstatements against three unfavourable ones is not a pattern of advocacy. It is closer to the error profile of someone working from memory at speed. One run is not a base rate, and this is a single episode.

The messenger

Naam's reputation rests on solar cost forecasting, and the documented record is unusual: his 2011 and 2015 projections were too conservative — prices fell roughly twice as fast as he predicted — and he has said so publicly himself. Independent write-ups confirm it. I searched specifically for cases where he was too optimistic and found none, but the search was oriented toward finding errors of conservatism, so treat that as an incomplete test rather than a clean bill.

The venue is not adversarial. Moonshots is a sponsored weekly roundtable hosted by Peter Diamandis with recurring co-hosts and an explicit abundance orientation; guests are ideologically adjacent and there is no fact-checking function in the room. That said, the pushback in this episode is real: Alex Wissner-Gross presses hard on scaling laws, on why Nvidia is not vertically integrating, and on orbital cooling, and Naam gives ground on none of them by reflex. It is a friendly format in which the guest is nonetheless argued with.

What survives

  • The central reframe. Time-to-power, not cost per megawatt-hour, is the binding constraint. Every independent source consulted points the same way, and the comparison table makes it visible: the cheapest firm power on the board is also among the slowest and cannot be built in the United States.
  • The flexibility argument, with its threshold corrected. The Duke paper is real, the capacity is real, and the regulatory movement is real. Whether data centre operators will actually accept curtailment is the open question, and it is commercial rather than technical.
  • His scepticism. On SMR dates, on fusion companies failing, on orbital compute for fifteen to twenty years, and on recursive self-improvement — in each case he is the most conservative voice in an optimistic room, and in each case the outside evidence supports him.
  • The learning-rate argument, best evidenced not by solar but by Barakah unit 4 costing roughly 40 % of unit 1.

What to discount

  • The sodium-ion tenfold, if read as a present-day figure. Today the gap is under two. He hedged it as something sodium "could" eventually deliver, which is a weaker and more defensible claim than the number sounds when repeated without the hedge — and it will be repeated without the hedge.
  • Any single-number version of the Duke finding. The paper is a schedule across curtailment levels; the headline "100 GW" only means anything attached to a threshold.
  • His $4/W for Chinese nuclear — in the other direction. Corrected, it strengthens the nuclear case relative to everything else in the table.
  • The framing that power is cheap, full stop. It is cheap for the buyer. PJM's capacity market says the accommodation cost is real and lands elsewhere.
  • Enthusiasm on either side of the orbital question. The heat-rejection numbers are unforgiving and the launch-cadence numbers are his own — but Starcloud's H100 in orbit is a real existence proof and the sceptics should account for it.
