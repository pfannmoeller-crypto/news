Weekly edition · July 21 – July 28, 2026 · Prior edition July 25

Both sides in the Iran war simply stopped shooting over the weekend. The Fed's live-or-hold call now arrives tomorrow. And the board's most stubbornly stale price finally moved — against the incumbent.

Three days ago this dashboard was tracking its worst edition yet for the Iran cluster: 13 nights of strikes, Gulf states counter-attacking, Brent above $100 for the first time since May. None of that reversed on its own — but late Friday the Pentagon quietly paused the bombing campaign, Iran held its own retaliation in response, and by Monday President Trump was telling reporters aboard Air Force One that "we are having good talks" and "good things can happen." Three different clusters, three different kinds of "finally, something happened."

Iran de-escalation

Geopolitics · 5 underlying markets · Both sides stopped shooting, conditionally
trending_upImproving — first genuinely positive edition for this cluster in seven weeks, though the pause is explicitly conditional on both sides

After 13 consecutive nights of American airstrikes and a first-ever round of Gulf states (Bahrain, Kuwait) striking Iran directly, the campaign simply stopped. The Pentagon suspended strikes late Friday, July 24, and held off for a second straight night through the weekend; Iran's own military spokesman, Mohammad Akraminia, confirmed the reciprocity directly: "These attacks continued until two nights ago, but over the past two nights the Americans have stopped." A US official told reporters the pause was meant to let talks develop, and separate reporting attributed it to advisers warning the Pentagon was running low on precision-interceptor stock — the munitions-sustainability concern this board flagged as a new variable last edition. By Monday, July 27, Trump told reporters aboard Air Force One "we are having good talks" with Iran and that "good things can happen," while also insisting the US is not low on munitions and that he has no interest in prolonged negotiations. NPR: US pauses attacks for a second straight day, Tehran does too Al Jazeera: Iran halts retaliatory strikes after pause in US attacks

The conditionality is the whole story here: an Iranian official told Reuters the position remains strictly "attack for attack" — if the US resumes, so does Iran, and there is no framework agreement, just a mutual stand-down that either side can end unilaterally. Iran's top joint military command separately warned that any US attempt to enforce its "illegal" maritime blockade would itself constitute an escalation, and Iran issued a warning to Ukraine over an unrelated Caspian Sea strike, a reminder that Tehran is fighting on more than one front narratively even as the US-facing war quiets. Oil, which had settled at $100.69 on July 24 for the first close above $100 since May, gave back much of that spike as the pause and talk of talks took hold — reported anywhere from the high-$80s to around $97 depending on the exact hour and source, a real gap this edition's price caveat exists precisely to flag. The signals in detail

The reciprocal pause itself: the most important structural fact of the week, because it is the first de-escalatory move driven by both sides simultaneously rather than a one-sided ceasefire offer that one side declines. Iran's own spokesman confirming the US stopped first, and that Iran is matching rather than initiating, is a meaningfully different shape than the collapsed mediator framework two editions ago, which required an active agreement neither side ultimately signed. The munitions-stockpile driver: this board flagged interceptor-stock strain as a slow-moving pressure toward de-escalation last edition; reporting this week suggests it may have been the actual proximate trigger for the pause, independent of any diplomatic breakthrough — a genuinely different causal story than "talks succeeded." Trump's own hedged language: "there's a good chance that something could happen, and if it does good. If it doesn't, we go back to doing what we were doing" is about as unresolved a statement as a president can make about an active war, and markets should be pricing that ambiguity rather than either extreme. Iran's maritime-blockade warning: a reminder that even during a bombing pause, Tehran is actively contesting a different axis (shipping/Hormuz) that could reignite fighting on its own timeline independent of the bombing track.

Szenarien

(1) Pause holds and formalizes into a real framework (base, ~40%): the current stand-down survives past this week, talks produce something closer to the collapsed Qatar-Egypt-Pakistan-Oman mediator proposal, and Brent settles into the $75–85 range as war-risk premium unwinds further. (2) Pause holds but stays informal and fragile (~35%): no signed agreement emerges, both sides simply avoid triggering the other, and the situation drifts in an uneasy quiet that could break at any provocation — oil stays choppy in a wide $85–100 band on headline risk alone. (3) Pause breaks within two weeks (~25%): talks stall, one side resumes strikes, and the ladder resumes adding rungs from where it left off, likely re-testing $100+ oil quickly given how compressed the prior spike-to-pause cycle was.

This edition adds the next chapter rather than a reversal: futures-implied odds of a hike tomorrow spiked further to a peak near 40% last Thursday, July 23, then cooled back to roughly one-in-three (~33%) by Monday morning as Brent's retreat from $100 removed some of the inflation-scare premium. That is still a dramatically wider range than the ~10–12% this market carried just two weeks ago. Complicating the picture further, new Fed Chair Kevin Warsh has systematically dismantled forward guidance since taking office, submitting no personal rate projection at the June meeting and running a 130-word policy statement against a historical norm of 300+ words — meaning Wednesday's statement and press-conference Q&A, not any dot plot (July has none), are the market's only live signal.
