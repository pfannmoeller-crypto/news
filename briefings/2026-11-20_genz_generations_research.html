Is Gen Z Really Worse Off? Five Generations Compared

Young people across Europe are blockading schools, camping in squares and striking over rent, and young Americans are voting against the economic status quo. This piece first states their case at full strength, then compares Gen Z with every Western generation since 1950 on twelve criteria, then shows what the studies say and where we land.

Key takeaways

  • The protests. In autumn 2026, students in France, tenants and unions in Spain and Portugal, and a youth letter to Chancellor Merz in Germany all name the same grievances: unaffordable housing, shrinking entry jobs, a rigged split between old and young, costly education, mental strain and politics that does not listen.
  • The comparison. On our twelve-criteria scoring (1 = best conditions, 5 = worst), the Millennials had it hardest (41 points), then the Boomers (36), then Gen Z (34), Gen X (31) and the Silent generation (27). On the seven economic criteria alone the order is Millennials 28, Gen Z 21, then Boomers and Gen X level at 18, Silent 11. This is our assessment, not a statistic.
  • What is true in the critique. Housing and the wealth gap between young and old are the worst in the data we found (mostly US, since 1950), Gen Z inherits the largest pension, debt and climate bill, and its mental-health data are the weakest we found.
  • What is overstated. On starting pay and, in the US and Germany, youth unemployment, Gen Z is ahead of the Millennials. On safety and freedom it scores best in the data we found (mostly US, since 1950). Southern Europe is the exception on jobs.
  • Our verdict, with medium confidence. Gen Z is not the worst-off Western generation overall. Its grievance is relative more than absolute: the gap between earning and owning, and between young and old, has never been wider.

Definitions: who is compared with whom

The five generations

We use the Pew convention for birth years. The “entered work” years are rough and show when each cohort faced the labour market as young adults.

GenerationBornEntered work (approx.)
Silent1928–45~1946–70
Boomers1946–64~1964–90
Gen X1965–80~1983–2005
Millennials1981–96~1999–2020
Gen Z1997–2012~2015–now; the oldest are 29

“The Western world”

We mean the USA plus Western Europe (Germany, France, the UK, Spain and Italy). US data are by far the richest, so the US is our anchor. European data differ sharply between North and South: Germany and the UK sit in one world, Spain and Italy in another. We say so wherever it changes the result.

Same life stage, two caveats

Every generation is compared at the same life stage: entry into adult life, roughly ages 18 to 34. That is the only fair test. It has two caveats. First, Gen Z is only partly through this stage, so some criteria are provisional. Second, a snapshot mixes two things researchers separate: a cohort effect (something lasting about a birth-year group) and a period effect (a recession year that hits whoever is young at that moment). Bobby Duffy (C2, not fetched), who studies generations, argues that period and age effects matter more than the generation labels.

How the scoring worksFor each of twelve criteria we show the data, add two to four sentences, and give each generation a score from 1 (best conditions) to 5 (worst). Ties are allowed. All criteria carry equal weight, and each row has a confidence level. The scores are our assessment of the data shown, not a measurement. Part II shows the full method and the sensitivity of the result to different weights.
Part I

The critique: what young people say is broken

The case as the protesters and their advocates would sign it, at full strength and before any testing. Part II then tests each claim against the past.

⚑

What is happening on the streets and at the ballot box

France

In autumn 2026 French students blockaded schools for more than three weeks over understaffed, dilapidated buildings. The protest widened into a fight over living costs, the CGT trade union joined, and about 2,400 people were arrested C3 · Fortune, 20 Oct 2026; CNN via KRDO, 24 Oct.

Spain and Portugal

Tent camps returned to the Puerta del Sol in Madrid after the eviction of an 87-year-old, and Lisbon and Porto saw rent protests demanding rent caps. On 11 Nov 2026 Spain held its first general strike in 14 years, called by the unions UGT and CCOO over housing, 18 days before the snap election of 29 Nov. Average rent has nearly doubled in ten years. According to the Youth Council of Spain, a 50 m² flat in Madrid costs about €1,170 a month, roughly 98.7% of the average young person’s income C4 · Al Jazeera.

Italy, Greece, Albania, Serbia

The “Italia in Piazza” protests led to 271 arrests in Milan. Greece saw protests over education and fuel prices, Albania over corruption (“Flamingo”), and Serbia has a student anti-corruption movement C3 · Fortune/CNN.

Germany: “Gen Z regelt”

On 31 Oct 2026 protesters in more than 30 cities, tens of thousands in all, backed an open letter to Chancellor Merz. It asks for more school funding, psychotherapy and addiction support instead of cuts, affordable living, fairer burden-sharing instead of saving at the expense of the young, no return of conscription, consistent climate policy and no more surveillance. Education minister Prien (CDU) called the concerns legitimate C3 · Berliner ZeitungC4 · ZDFheute. One caveat on the organisers: their structure is opaque, with a changed imprint and a Swiss address.

United States: votes and polls, not marches

There is no mass street protest, but the numbers point the same way.

  • Socialism versus capitalism. In Gallup’s September 2026 poll, 18–34s view socialism positively at 57% against 43% for capitalism. Among those 55 and older it is 65% capitalism and 34% socialism C4 · via Axios.
  • Midterms. 18–29s backed Democratic House candidates 66–31 in the 2026 midterms C4 · CIRCLE, preliminary.
  • New York City 2025. 75% of 18–29 voters backed Zohran Mamdani, who ran on a cost-of-living platform C4 · CIRCLE/Tufts.
  • Worry. In the Axios–Generation Lab poll of Aug 2026, 96% worry about rent, groceries, gas or utilities, and 36% trust neither party on the cost of living C3.

Reading note: “socialism” in such polls mostly means a stronger welfare state, and the poll bases differ (positive only versus positive-or-neutral), so the figures are not directly comparable across surveys.

What the pattern shows

The protests have different triggers in different countries, which is part of why the critique is hard to dismiss as a single mood. In France the spark was schools: understaffed, dilapidated buildings, then living costs. In Spain and Portugal it was housing and rents, with a general strike and tent camps. In Italy, Greece, Albania and Serbia the mix is education, fuel prices and corruption. In Germany the open letter is the broadest, covering school money, mental-health care, burden-sharing, conscription, climate and surveillance. In the US the channel is electoral and polls rather than the street.

What links them is a shared sentence: the system as it stands is organised around people who are already established. The seven theses below put that sentence into seven testable claims. Two reliability notes: the arrest counts and most event details come from single credible media sources (C3), and the German organisers’ structure is opaque, so we treat turnout and demands as reported rather than audited.

T1

The broken promise

The core claim is moral before it is statistical: “study hard, work hard, and you will do better than your parents” no longer holds. CNN’s coverage of the protests quotes the idea that liberal democracy’s promise “simply doesn’t hold true anymore” C3.

The German youth study 2026 (Schnetzer and Hurrelmann, n = 2,012 people aged 14–29) gives the claim a shape. Willingness to perform is high, while doubts that effort pays off are growing. 21% plan to emigrate and 41% can imagine it C5 · publisher’s release. Caveat: the sample is online-recruited, which can skew who answers.

The strongest form of the argument. A promise is different from a hardship. A hardship can be endured if it is temporary or shared; a broken promise is a charge against the people who made it. That is why the protesters aim at governments and not only at markets, and why a high willingness to perform combined with doubts that effort pays is the pairing to watch. The emigration figures show the doubt turning into behaviour or at least into intent.

This thesis matters because it reframes everything else. If the complaint were only “times are hard”, every generation could sign it. The protesters’ claim is that the deal has changed: the effort is the same, the reward is not.

T2

Housing: you can’t leave home or buy

This is the thesis protesters lead with, and the evidence they cite is the strongest of the seven.

  • Spain. 67.1% of 18–34s live with their parents. The average age of leaving home is 30.2, against 26.3 for the EU C3 · Eurostat/INE via EUobserver.
  • EU prices. House prices rose 55.4% and rents 26.7% between 2010 and 2024 C3 · Eurofound.
  • Paris. Half of private tenants under 30 pay more than 51% of their income in rent C3.
  • UK. 63% of 20–24s live with their parents, 12 percentage points more than in 2011 C5 · Resolution Foundation 2026.
  • US. The median first-time buyer is 40 years old C2 · National Association of Realtors via Fortune.

The strongest form of the argument. Housing is the one criterion where effort cannot substitute for timing. If house prices grow faster than rents, and rents grow faster than pay, the entry fee to independent adult life rises for everyone who arrives later, however hard they work. Living with parents then looks less like a choice and more like a queue. The figures also span several countries, so the point is not a Spanish quirk.

The protesters’ reading: when the typical young Madrid renter would hand over nearly all of their income for a small flat, the question of saving for a deposit does not even arise.

T3

Jobs and the AI ladder

The claim is that entry-level jobs, the first rung of every career, are disappearing, and that AI is now removing more of them.

Under-25 unemployment, Jul 2026Rate
Spain22.9%
France20.7%
Italy18.9%
EU15.1%
Germany7.2%

Eurostat C5

The strongest form of the argument. A career is built on a first job, and the first job is where skills and pay start to compound. If employers stop hiring juniors, the damage lands on a whole entry cohort at once, and it does so unevenly: Southern Europe already has youth unemployment of roughly one in five, while Germany does not. AI, in this telling, is a second shock on top of a market that was already hard to enter.

On AI, the Stanford Digital Economy Lab (Brynjolfsson and colleagues, Aug 2026) finds that employment of 22–25-year-olds in the most AI-exposed jobs is about 19% below trend, through reduced hiring. The authors themselves call the finding descriptive, not causal C5. In the US, recent-graduate unemployment is 5.6% against 4.2% for all workers C3 · NY Fed via a secondary source.

T4

A rigged distribution between generations

Here the claim is that wealth and the state both favour the old.

  • UK wealth gap. The gap between the typical wealth of people in their early 60s and in their early 30s grew from £135,000 (2006–08) to £310,000 (2020–22). Those aged 60 and over hold 49% of wealth C4 · Resolution Foundation.
  • US shares. Scott Galloway, in “War on the Young”, says under-40s hold 7% of US household wealth against 12% in 1989, and that seniors receive eight times the federal spending per head compared with children C3 · his figures.
  • German pensions. There were 6 contributors per pensioner in 1962, 1.8 in 2020, and about 1.3 are projected for 2050 C3 · INSM, an employer lobby citing official data. The pension contribution rate of 18.6% is projected to reach about 21% by 2035 C4.
  • “Financial nihilism.” Galloway’s term for the sense that government rescues protect people who already own assets, in other words, that the system protects those who already own C4 · SXSW 2026.

The strongest form of the argument. The claim is not that the old have done something wrong, but that the rules reward owning over earning, and that the old own. When the typical wealth of people in their early 60s pulls £310,000 ahead of those in their early 30s, and the pension arithmetic loads more onto fewer contributors, the young pay twice: once in the cost of getting in, and once in the cost of keeping the system going. The protesters’ demand for “fairer burden-sharing” is this point in political language.

T5

Education costs more and pays less

  • US debt. Student debt stands at $1.86 trillion across about 42.6 million federal borrowers, with an average of about $39,500 in federal debt C3.
  • England fees. £0 until 1998, then £1,000 (1998), £9,000 (2012) and £9,535 (2025) C5 · Commons Library.
  • The payoff. The US college wage premium rose from about 39% (1980) to about 79% (2000) and was slightly below its 2000 level by 2023. It has plateaued C5 · Minneapolis Fed.

The strongest form of the argument. Education used to be the escape route from a bad starting position. If in England it costs several times more, is financed by debt, and returns a premium that has stopped rising, then the route still exists but is steeper.

The protesters’ point is the combination: the price of the ticket has gone up several times over, while the prize has stopped growing.

T6

Mental health and a sense of no control

In Germany 29% of 14–29-year-olds say they need psychological support, a new high, and 60% report addiction-like smartphone use (Jugend in Deutschland 2026) C5 · release. In the US, 42% of high-schoolers reported persistent sadness in 2021 and 39.7% in 2023 C5 · CDC. The sociologist María Silvestre describes “a strong sense of uncertainty and a lack of control” C3 · Fortune.

The German open letter asks for psychotherapy and addiction support instead of cuts. The strongest form of the argument. A sense of no control is the psychological end of the other theses: if housing, jobs and pay cannot be planned, uncertainty is rational, not a disorder. In the protesters’ telling, strain on the young is not a private weakness but a consequence of the conditions in T1 to T5.

T7

Politics doesn’t listen

The last thesis is about voice. If the young feel that mainstream politics protects the old, the trust gap shows up in the vote, and it moves toward the edges. In Germany, Die Linke leads among the young with 25%, and the AfD gains among young men (Jugend in Deutschland 2026). In the US the same trust gap appears as 36% saying they trust neither party on the cost of living (Axios–Generation Lab), and as the large margins for Mamdani and for Democratic House candidates described above.

What Part II does with this. Seven theses give twelve test criteria. T1 becomes “beating your parents” (criterion 3), T2 “a home of your own” (4), T3 “getting a job” (1), T4 “wealth and assets” (5) and “the bill that comes due” (11), T5 “education” (6), T6 “mental health” (10). T7 is political and has no direct score. We added criteria the protesters do not mention (pay, stability, war, safety, freedom), because a fair comparison has to include what is better as well as what is worse.
Part II

The comparison: five generations, twelve criteria

Same life stage, our own scoring, every row shown with its data. The master table and ranking follow the twelve cards.

Method

  • Life stage. Entry into adult life, roughly ages 18–34, for each generation.
  • Geography. A Western average, with the US as the anchor and European notes where they differ.
  • Scores. 1 means the best conditions, 5 the worst. Ties are allowed. All twelve criteria carry equal weight.
  • Basis. The scores are our assessment from the data shown in each card, with a confidence level per criterion. They are a judgement on the evidence, not a statistic.
  • Provisional. An asterisk (*) marks a Gen Z score for which no real data exist yet.
Why twelve criteria and not oneA single statistic can be chosen to favour either side: income says Gen Z is fine, housing says it is not. We therefore score twelve different parts of a young adult’s life and add them up, so that no single choice decides the result. We then test how fragile the ranking is by changing the weights (see the sensitivity table). This protects against cherry-picking but does not remove judgement, because each score is still our reading of the data.

The “economic core” is criteria 1 to 7 (jobs, pay, mobility, home, wealth, education, stability). Criteria 8 to 12 (war, safety, mental health, the bill, freedom) are the rest of life.

A home of your own

The data here are the richest and the most one-sided. We group them in three sets: price and rates, ownership rates, and Europe.

Price and credit

  • US price-to-income. About 3.5x in 1975 (BCG via Fortune, C3) against about 5x in 2024 (Harvard JCHS, C5). BCG puts it as “nearly 6 years of median income”.
  • Mortgage rates (Freddie Mac via Bankrate C2): 1972 7.4%; 1979 11.2%; 1981 16.6% on average with an 18.4% peak; 2006 6.5%; 2021 3.15%; 2023–25 about 6.7–7%; Aug 2026 6.7%.
  • Today. The median home costs 43.9% of the median household income, with a record monthly cost of $3,172 C5 · Atlanta Fed.
  • England. Price-to-earnings was 3–4x in 1997 and 7.6x in 2025 C5 · ONS.

The figures disagree because they are measuring different things: averages are pulled up by a few rich households, medians are not, and the age and dollar base differ. Three of the four use averages. The medians at 30 are close to one another ($21.5k, $27.2k, $22.1k), with Gen X highest.

Relative position

  • UK. The typical wealth of people in their early 30s is only about £8,000 higher than in 2006–08, while that of people in their early 60s gained about £150,000. Total wealth rose from about 3x GDP in the mid-1980s to about 7.5x in 2020–22 C4 · Resolution Foundation.
  • Piketty and Zucman. Private wealth as a share of national income rose from about 300% in 1970 to about 400–500% in the US, and from about 200% to about 400% (2010) in Germany C4.
  • Inheritance. The US faces a transfer of about $84 trillion through 2045 C3 · Cerulli. In Germany €300–400 billion a year is inherited, two thirds of it by the top 20% C4 · DIW.

The inheritance numbers are why we score Gen Z provisionally: the cohort is too young for an outcome, and it is likely to split between heirs and non-heirs.

Our assessment (relative position and access to assets): Silent 1 · Boomers 1 · Gen X 2 · Millennials 4 · Gen Z 4* (too young; heirs versus non-heirs). Our scores follow relative position and access (UK wealth gap, inheritance concentration), not the US age-30 medians, which do not separate Boomers and Millennials. Confidence: medium.

Part III

What the studies say

The research splits into camps. They often use the same data and still disagree, because they pick different measures. Camps are ordered from “worse off” to “complicated”.

CampCore viewMain sources
A · Worse offYoung people have less wealth, fewer homes and weaker mobility than their elders hadResolution Foundation, Chetty, OECD, Galloway, U.S. Bank
B · Better off, or complicatedIncome is up; perception runs ahead of reality; period and age matter more than cohortBCG, Fed DFA via Horpedahl, Burn-Murdoch, Duffy
C · The mental-health disputeWhether phones and social media cause the worse numbersHaidt versus Odgers, Przybylski, Ferguson

A · “Worse off”

Resolution Foundation (UK) C5 · Chetty C5 · OECD C3–C4 · Galloway C3 · U.S. Bank survey C2

Resolution Foundation. Young wealth is stagnant and ownership has collapsed: among 19–29s it fell from 22% in 1989 to 11% in 2023–24, while for those aged 65 and over it rose from 53% to 76%. More young people live at home. It also concedes a point for the other side: after-housing-cost income for the 1996–2000 birth cohort is about 18% above that of the 1986–90 cohort, though half of that gain comes from other household members.

Chetty. Absolute mobility, the share earning more than their parents, fell from about 90% to about 50% (criterion 3).

OECD, “Under Pressure” (2019). The share of people in their 20s who are middle class is 68% for the Boomers and 60% for the Millennials. Housing costs rose about three times faster than median income.

Galloway. Relative wealth and state spending: under-40s hold 7% of US household wealth against 12% in 1989, and seniors get 8x per-capita federal spending versus kids (his figures).

U.S. Bank survey, 2026. Gen Z starts saving at 19, Boomers at 32. 56% say they “did everything right and still behind”, and 29% have given up on owning a home. This is a survey of attitudes, reported through an aggregator.

Caveats on this side: Chetty’s cohorts stop in the 1980s and include no Gen Z; Galloway’s figures are his own; the Resolution Foundation data are mostly UK; the pension-burden numbers this camp leans on come from INSM (employer lobby) and Stiftung Marktwirtschaft (advocacy think tank, method contested).

Why it differs: this camp reads stocks (wealth, homes) and the old-young gap, not the flow of income.

B · “Better off”, or “it’s complicated”

BCG C3 · Fed DFA via Horpedahl C3 · Resolution Foundation pay release C4 · Burn-Murdoch C3 · Bobby Duffy C2 · not fetched here

BCG finds higher income and speaks of “financial dysmorphia”: for the first time the young are more pessimistic than the old. It concedes that housing and student debt are real. Keep in mind that BCG is a corporate advisory firm, so this is a corporate-advisory source, and that we read it via Fortune, not in the report itself.

Fed DFA via Jeremy Horpedahl. Millennials and Gen Z together are ahead of older cohorts at a similar stage, but the figure is an average, not a median.

Resolution Foundation, pay release. Its own press release is headed “it’s not all doom and gloom”: young people’s pay packets outstrip those of the Millennials.

Burn-Murdoch. The story is a transatlantic divergence: young Americans do better than young Western Europeans.

Bobby Duffy (King’s College London, “Generations”) argues that period effects and age effects matter more than cohort labels. We did not fetch his work, so treat this as a pointer, not a finding.

Why it differs: this camp reads the flow of income and compares Gen Z with the Millennials, often with averages.

C · The mental-health dispute

Haidt C3 versus Odgers, Przybylski, Ferguson C3 · Fortune 2024

Jonathan Haidt points to smartphones and social media since about 2010 as the cause of falling teenage wellbeing. Candice Odgers, Andrew Przybylski and Chris Ferguson say the evidence for causation is weak. Both sides accept that the self-reported distress numbers are high; they dispute why.

Why it differs: correlation in the timing versus evidence for a causal mechanism.

How to read this section. The camps are not equally well sourced. The Resolution Foundation, Chetty, the St. Louis Fed and the CDC are primary sources (C5), while the U.S. Bank survey, the Equitable figure and the Bobby Duffy point are aggregator-level (C2) here. A weaker source can still be right, but it should not outweigh a primary one in our reading. Both camps A and B cite the Resolution Foundation, which shows that the disagreement is mostly about framing, not about the numbers.

The five cruxes

The camps rarely contradict each other on facts. They disagree on five choices:

  • Which measure. Flow income points one way, stock wealth and housing the other.
  • Which comparison. Versus the Millennials (Gen Z looks good), versus your parents (unknown, provisional), or versus today’s old (Gen Z looks poor).
  • Who earns it. If income gains come from living at home or from other household members, is that a gain or a symptom? The Resolution Foundation’s own 18% figure has exactly this problem.
  • US versus Europe. The data for young Americans look better than for Western Europeans, and Southern Europe is a different world from Germany.
  • Perception versus reality. BCG’s “dysmorphia” says the young misjudge their position; the AI-hiring gap and the housing arithmetic say that at least part of the worry is accurate.
Our verdict

Is Gen Z really worse off?

Our assessment, with medium confidence. We say what we can and cannot support.

Our assessment. Gen Z is not the worst-off Western generation overall. It is worst off on housing, and joint-worst on assets (level with the Millennials, provisional): the two things that decide whether you can build a life.
  • Not the worst overall. On our scoring the Millennials had it hardest. They entered the labour market in the worst crisis since the 1930s, earned less than their predecessors at 30, and lost the housing ladder.
  • Better than the headlines on pay, jobs and safety. Gen Z is the first generation since the Boomers to start with higher real pay and, in the US and Germany, low youth unemployment. On safety and freedom it scores best in the data we found (mostly US, since 1950).
  • Worst on housing, joint-worst on assets, and the bill. Its home position is the worst in the comparison, and its asset position is level with the Millennials’ (provisional). It faces the worst starting position in the data we found (mostly US, since 1950), and it inherits the biggest bill (ageing, debt, climate). Its mental-health data are the worst we can find, with contested causes.
  • So, who is right? The protests are right on housing, wealth distribution and the generational bill. They overstate the jobs and income story in the US and Germany. They are right on jobs in Southern Europe.
  • Relative, not absolute. The critique is relative more than absolute: the gap between earning and owning, and between young and old, has never been wider. That makes the grievance real even where incomes rise.
  • Confidence: medium. Gen Z is only partly through the life stage, many series are US-only, and several “better off” studies use averages.

What we can and cannot support

We can support: that the Millennials score hardest on our twelve criteria in every weighting we tried; that Gen Z’s home position is the worst in the comparison and its asset position is level with the Millennials’ (provisional); that its pay and (in the US and Germany) job position are better than the Millennials’; and that its safety and freedom are the best. We cannot support: a claim about how Gen Z will end up at 40, because the data stop at about 29; a claim that AI is the cause of weaker entry hiring, because the Stanford authors say their finding is descriptive; or a clean ranking between neighbouring generations, because the gaps of two to three points are within judgement error.

Where the sensitivity matters. The conclusion “not the worst overall” holds in all four variants. The conclusion “third-hardest” does not: if housing and wealth count double, Gen Z moves to second-hardest, and if its Southern European job position is used, it ties with the Boomers. In other words, how bad Gen Z looks depends mainly on how much weight you give to housing and wealth, and on whether you live in Madrid or in Munich.

◆

The big picture

Our synthesis, not a forecast. Two conditional scenarios, stated symmetrically.

If housing eases and AI creates entry jobs

If housing supply and rates ease and AI creates more entry jobs than it removes, Gen Z could end up ahead of the Millennials. US data already point that way.

If housing stays dear and AI keeps young hiring low

If housing stays at about 44% of income for the median buyer, inheritance decides who owns, and AI keeps hiring of the young below trend, the divide inside Gen Z, between heirs and non-heirs, becomes the main story.

Part IV

Worth knowing

Data caveats that change how the comparison reads. No verdicts, only what the sources allow.

  • Gen Z is not finished. The oldest are 29. Every Gen Z score rests on a partial life stage, and rows 3 and 5 are provisional.
  • Much of the data is American. Several rows (safety, mental health, wealth at 30) are US-only. European data differ sharply between North and South.
  • Averages versus medians. “Better off” studies often use averages, which a few rich households lift. Medians tell a flatter story.
  • Cohort versus period. A snapshot of a recession year says more about the year than about the cohort.
  • Unemployment excludes students. Far fewer young people were in education in the 1950s, so the old rates are not like-for-like.
  • Not retrieved. 1950s–70s European youth unemployment, many Silent-generation figures, and the 1970s–90s homicide peaks.
  • The Vietnam draft was about 1.86 million inductions, not the often-cited 2.2 million. The Korea-era Selective Service figures are internally inconsistent.
  • BCG was read via Fortune, not in the report, and BCG is a corporate-advisory source.
  • The organisers of “Gen Z regelt” are opaque (changed imprint, Swiss address), so the turnout and demands rest on press reports.
