10 years ago2016Rousseff impeached · Falcon 9 explosion · Mother Teresa canonized
POLITICS2016August 31, 2016

Brazil's Senate convicts and removes President Dilma Rousseff, 61–20

After a five-day trial, the Brazilian Senate votes 61 to 20 — seven votes past the two-thirds threshold needed — to convict Rousseff of illegally manipulating government accounts to mask a budget deficit ahead of her 2014 re-election, and removes her from office with sixteen months left in her term. Vice President Michel Temer, who had governed as acting president since Rousseff's May suspension, is sworn in as her full successor the same afternoon.

Rousseff, Brazil's first woman president and a former Marxist guerrilla jailed and tortured under the 1964–85 military dictatorship, calls the vote a "coup" against a president who committed no crime deserving removal; her supporters note she was never charged in the sprawling Lava Jato corruption probe that had jailed dozens of her own coalition's politicians.

Context & background. The accounting maneuver at issue, nicknamed "fiscal pedaling," involved state-owned banks covering federal budget shortfalls without those loans appearing on the books — a practice several of Rousseff's predecessors had also used, which her defenders argued made her prosecution selective. The impeachment drive gathered force only after her own former ally, House Speaker Eduardo Cunha, opened proceedings in December 2015 while himself under corruption investigation.

The details. A separate Senate vote, held immediately after the conviction, would have barred Rousseff from holding public office for eight years; it fell short at 42–36 against the same 54-vote bar, so the removal stood but the ban did not. Temer, whose own approval ratings were already in single digits, inherited a recession-hit economy and a Congress in which more than half the members faced some form of legal investigation.

Why it still matters. The Temer government that followed pushed through austerity and labor reforms deeply unpopular on the Brazilian left, feeding the political vacuum that Jair Bolsonaro filled in the 2018 election; Rousseff's own Workers' Party would not return to the presidency until Lula's narrow 2022 win, making 2016 the hinge point of the decade of Brazilian political instability that followed.

DISASTERSCIENCE2016September 1, 2016

A SpaceX Falcon 9 explodes on the pad, destroying a $200 million satellite

During a routine pre-launch "static fire" test at Cape Canaveral's Launch Complex 40, a Falcon 9 rocket and the Amos-6 communications satellite it was due to carry two days later erupt in a fireball. No one is hurt, but the pad itself is wrecked for months.

Amos-6 had been built for Israel's Spacecom, with Facebook under contract to lease its capacity for internet.org broadband access across sub-Saharan Africa; Mark Zuckerberg, touring Africa that week, posts that he is "deeply disappointed" by the loss.

Context & background. SpaceX had flown 28 consecutive successful Falcon 9 missions since its last failure, an in-flight breakup in June 2015, and the static fire was meant to be a routine final check before an already-delayed launch. Investigators traced the blast to a failure mode nobody had modeled: a composite-overwrapped pressure vessel inside the second-stage liquid-oxygen tank buckled under super-chilled propellant loading and ignited the oxygen around it.

The details. The finding forced SpaceX to redesign COPV loading procedures and helium-tank hardware before Falcon 9 could fly again, which it did four months later, in January 2017, from a different, undamaged pad. NASA subsequently demanded extra scrutiny of the same hardware before certifying Falcon 9 for crewed flights.

Why it still matters. Facebook's plan to reach unconnected users through leased satellite capacity effectively ended with Amos-6; Spacecom instead sold itself to a Chinese buyer weeks later, a deal Israeli regulators later blocked. The COPV failure mode identified here remains a standard case study in aerospace pressure-vessel design.

CULTURE2016September 4, 2016

Pope Francis canonizes Mother Teresa before a crowd of roughly 120,000

In St Peter's Square, Pope Francis declares Mother Teresa a saint, praising her "generous dispensing of divine mercy" toward "the abandoned, the discarded." The ceremony falls a day before the 19th anniversary of her death in Kolkata on 5 September 1997.

Canonization required a second miracle attributed to her intercession, confirmed the previous December: the unexplained recovery of a Brazilian man with multiple brain tumors after his family prayed to her.

Context & background. Nineteen years from death to sainthood is unusually fast by Vatican standards, which normally require a five-year waiting period before the process even opens; Pope John Paul II waived that wait in 1999, two years after her death, letting the cause begin immediately.

The details. Roughly 1,500 homeless people from around Rome were bused in and seated with cardinals at the ceremony's request, and thirteen heads of state or government attended. Marcilio Haddad Andrino — the Brazilian man whose 2008 recovery supplied the second miracle — was present with his family.

Why it still matters. The canonization arrived alongside renewed scrutiny of medical practices at some Missionaries of Charity facilities, a debate that has run in parallel with her sainthood ever since and that the Vatican ceremony did not resolve.

CONFLICT2001September 3, 2001

The US and Israel walk out of the UN's Durban racism conference

Three days into the UN World Conference against Racism in Durban, South Africa — which opened August 31 — the US and Israeli delegations withdraw over draft language equating Zionism with racism and singling out Israel for war-crimes wording. Secretary of State Colin Powell had already decided in July not to send a delegation at all; a lower-level US team walks out after failing to get the language removed.

The conference continues for another five days without them, adopting a final declaration with the most contested passages softened, but the walkout becomes the episode most remembered from it.

Context & background. The disputed language had been pushed largely by an Arab-states bloc within the drafting process and by a parallel NGO forum meeting alongside the official conference; earlier preparatory sessions in Tehran and Geneva had already produced similar disputes over Israel-related wording, so the walkout was the culmination of a fight months in the making, not a surprise on the day.

The details. Because the conference closed just three days before the September 11 attacks, its findings and news coverage were almost immediately overshadowed; the compromise final declaration Durban eventually adopted dropped the "Zionism is racism" language but still drew criticism from Israel and Jewish organizations over other passages on the Middle East.

Why it still matters. The dispute set the template for every subsequent UN racism review conference: follow-up sessions in 2009 ("Durban II") and 2011 ("Durban III") were both boycotted by the US, Israel and a growing list of Western governments citing the same concerns, making the 2001 walkout the opening move in a diplomatic pattern still in force today.

BUSINESS2001September 4, 2001

Hewlett-Packard agrees to buy Compaq in a $25 billion all-stock deal

HP chief executive Carly Fiorina and Compaq's Michael Capellas announce an all-stock merger valued around $25 billion, betting that combining the world's two largest PC makers can offset a slumping PC market and challenge Dell and IBM. The announcement, made over the US Labor Day holiday weekend, catches much of the tech press off guard.

It becomes the largest merger in computer-industry history to that point — and one of the most bitterly contested, as HP co-founder heir Walter Hewlett launches a public proxy fight against his own company's board within weeks.

Context & background. Both companies had spent 2001 watching PC demand slow sharply after the dot-com crash; Fiorina's pitch, internally contested from the start, was that scale in a shrinking market beat standing alone. Walter Hewlett and other founding-family shareholders, controlling over 100 million shares between them, argued the opposite — that HP was buying its way into Compaq's own troubled low-margin PC business at a bad moment.

The details. The March 2002 shareholder vote passed by a margin narrow enough that Hewlett sued to overturn it, alleging HP had improperly pressured a major fund manager; a Delaware judge rejected the challenge and the deal closed in May 2002. Roughly 15,000 of the promised layoffs followed as HP absorbed Compaq's workforce.

Why it still matters. The integration struggled for years, and Fiorina was ousted as CEO in February 2005 over board dissatisfaction with results; the deal is still taught in business schools as a cautionary case on scale-for-its-own-sake mergers, even though the combined PC and printer business remained a top-two global vendor for the following two decades.

50 years ago1976Viking 2 lands on Mars · Belenko defects · the Harrison verdict
SCIENCE1976September 3, 1976

NASA's Viking 2 lander touches down on Mars at Utopia Planitia

Six weeks after its twin, Viking 1, became the first US spacecraft to land safely on Mars, Viking 2 sets down in the northern plain of Utopia Planitia and begins transmitting the first close-up images from that part of the planet. Both landers carry biology experiments designed to test Martian soil for signs of microbial life.

Neither lander's results settle the life question one way or the other; the debate over how to interpret them continues among planetary scientists to this day.

Context & background. A Wikipedia monthly-events page for September 1976 lists this landing under September 7 instead, apparently conflating touchdown with a later imaging milestone; NASA's own mission page, JPL's anniversary release and an independent astronomy-history record all state September 3, and that earlier, better-corroborated date is used here — the exact kind of aggregator drift this briefing is built to catch.

The details. Viking 2's most-cited experiment, the "labeled release" test, produced a positive-looking result that its own designer, Gilbert Levin, argued for decades afterward was genuine evidence of microbial metabolism; the mission's other instruments found no organic molecules at all, a contradiction later attributed to reactive Martian soil chemistry rather than biology, though the argument was never fully closed.

Why it still matters. The unresolved Viking biology results are the direct reason NASA's subsequent Mars missions — from Pathfinder through Curiosity and Perseverance — were deliberately designed around habitability and sample-return rather than a repeat life-detection gamble, a caution that traces straight back to this landing's ambiguous data.

CONFLICT1976September 6, 1976

Soviet pilot Viktor Belenko defects to Japan, handing the West a MiG-25

Flying a routine training sortie from a Soviet Far East air base, Lieutenant Viktor Belenko breaks formation, drops to treetop level to evade radar, and flies his MiG-25P interceptor to Hakodate, Japan, landing with roughly 30 seconds of fuel left and overrunning the runway onto a public road. He immediately requests asylum.

The aircraft — the same "Foxbat" interceptor Western intelligence had rated as the most feared fighter in the Soviet arsenal — gives American and Japanese engineers their first direct look at the jet's actual construction.

Context & background. Belenko had planned the defection alone for months, telling no one; his aircraft nearly collided with a Japanese airliner during its low-altitude dash and, once on the ground, Japanese police had to physically hold back Soviet officials before US technical teams arrived to examine it.

The details. Dismantling the jet found it was built mostly of heavy stainless steel rather than titanium, its cockpit avionics ran on nuvistors — hardened vacuum-tube-style components chosen for radiation resistance and battlefield ruggedness rather than sophistication — and its top speed was constrained well below the figures Western analysts had assumed, because pushing it further risked destroying the engines.

Why it still matters. The inspection deflated years of Western threat assessments built on the MiG-25's reputation and directly shaped how the US approached its next generation of air-superiority fighters; Belenko was resettled in the US under a new identity and the Soviets, furious, forced Japan into weeks of tense negotiation before agreeing to take the disassembled aircraft back at all.

CULTURE1976August 31, 1976

A US judge finds George Harrison guilty of "subconsciously" copying a hit song

Ruling in a five-year-old suit, US District Judge Richard Owen finds that Harrison's 1970 chart-topper "My Sweet Lord" unintentionally copied the melody of the Chiffons' 1963 hit "He's So Fine," written by Ronnie Mack. Owen accepts that Harrison did not deliberately plagiarize the song but rules that is no defense, coining the phrase "subconscious plagiarism."

The case, still cited in copyright disputes today, drags on for years after this verdict over exactly how much Harrison owes and to whom.

Context & background. The twist that made the case notorious: Allen Klein, Harrison's own former manager, had negotiated on his behalf early in the dispute, then after their acrimonious split switched sides and began advising the plaintiff, Bright Tunes, against his former client.

The details. In 1978, with damages still unresolved, Klein's company ABKCO bought Bright Tunes outright for $587,000 — putting Harrison's former manager in position to collect the very damages he was suing him for. A 1981 ruling capped what Klein could recover at his purchase price and ordered any excess redirected to Harrison, closing off the conflict-of-interest windfall.

Why it still matters. "Subconscious plagiarism" became a standing legal doctrine, invoked directly in later high-profile music cases including the "Blurred Lines" and "Stairway to Heaven" disputes, making Owen's 1976 finding one of the most consequential single rulings in modern music-copyright law.

Editor's view

Three of this week's eight stories turn on a single vote or verdict that closed out a much longer fight: Rousseff's removal followed nine months of suspension and trial, the Harrison ruling capped five years of litigation not even then fully resolved, and HP-Compaq's announcement was the public surfacing of weeks of secret boardroom negotiation. Read together with the Durban walkout and Belenko's defection, both hinge points in disputes that ran for years afterward, the week's throughline is that "on this day" headlines mark endings and beginnings at once — the dated event is real, but it is rarely where the story actually starts or stops.
