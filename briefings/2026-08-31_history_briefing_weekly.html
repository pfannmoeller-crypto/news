10 years ago2016Falcon 9 explosion · Mother Teresa canonized · Paris Agreement ratified
DISASTERSCIENCE2016September 1, 2016

A SpaceX Falcon 9 explodes on the pad, destroying a $200 million Israeli satellite

During a routine pre-launch "static fire" test at Cape Canaveral's Launch Complex 40, a Falcon 9 rocket and the Amos-6 communications satellite it was due to carry two days later erupt in a fireball. No one is hurt, but the pad itself is wrecked.

Amos-6 had been built for Spacecom of Israel, with Facebook under contract to lease its capacity for internet.org broadband access across sub-Saharan Africa; Mark Zuckerberg, touring Africa that week, posts that he is "deeply disappointed."

Context & background. SpaceX had flown 28 consecutive successful Falcon 9 missions since its last failure, an in-flight breakup in June 2015, and the static fire was meant to be the routine final check before an already-delayed launch. Investigators later traced the blast to a failure mode nobody had modeled: a composite-overwrapped pressure vessel (COPV) inside the second-stage liquid-oxygen tank buckled under super-chilled propellant loading, igniting the oxygen around it.

The details. The finding forced SpaceX to redesign COPV loading procedures and helium-tank hardware before it could fly Falcon 9 again, which happened four months later, in January 2017, from a different, undamaged pad. NASA subsequently required extra scrutiny of the same hardware before allowing crewed Falcon 9 flights.

Why it still matters. Facebook's plan to reach unconnected users through leased satellite capacity, rather than the low-orbit constellation it would experiment with later, effectively ended with Amos-6; Spacecom sold itself to a Chinese buyer weeks later, a deal that was itself later blocked by Israeli regulators. The COPV failure mode identified here remains a standard case study in aerospace pressure-vessel design.

CULTURE2016September 4, 2016

Pope Francis canonizes Mother Teresa before roughly 120,000 people in Rome

In St Peter's Square, Pope Francis declares Mother Teresa a saint, crediting her with "generous dispensing of divine mercy" toward "the abandoned, the discarded." The ceremony falls a day before the 19th anniversary of her death in Kolkata on 5 September 1997.

Canonization required a second miracle attributed to her intercession, confirmed the previous December: the healing of a Brazilian man with multiple brain tumors, reportedly with no medical explanation.

Context & background. Nineteen years from death to sainthood is unusually fast by Vatican standards, which normally require a five-year waiting period before the process even opens; Pope John Paul II waived that wait for Mother Teresa in 1999, two years after her death, letting the cause begin immediately.

The details. Roughly 1,500 homeless people from around Rome were bused in and seated with cardinals at the ceremony's request, and thirteen heads of state or government attended, including the presidents of Albania and Kosovo, Marcilio Haddad Andrino — the Brazilian man whose 2008 recovery supplied the second miracle — was present with his family.

Why it still matters. The canonization came alongside renewed scrutiny of the Missionaries of Charity's medical practices in some of its facilities, a debate that has run in parallel with her sainthood ever since and that the Vatican ceremony did not resolve or address.

POLITICS2016September 3, 2016

The US and China jointly ratify the Paris climate agreement ahead of the G20

In a ceremony in Hangzhou the day before the G20 summit opens, Barack Obama and Xi Jinping hand UN Secretary-General Ban Ki-moon their countries' instruments of ratification for the Paris Agreement, formally committing the world's two largest emitters — together roughly 38% of global greenhouse gas output — to the climate pact agreed in December 2015.

The joint move is timed deliberately, both to set the tone for the G20 and to push the treaty's entry-into-force threshold within reach.

Context & background. The Paris Agreement's entry into force needed 55 countries covering at least 55% of global emissions to ratify; before this ceremony the tally stood well short on the emissions threshold, since most ratifiers so far had been smaller economies. The US-China joint move, agreed after months of quiet bilateral diplomacy, was designed specifically to clear that bar.

The details. It worked faster than expected: with the US and China aboard, enough further countries ratified within weeks that the treaty entered into force on 4 November 2016 — just four days before the US presidential election that would put Donald Trump, who had pledged to withdraw from the pact, in office.

Why it still matters. The US did subsequently withdraw under Trump in 2017, re-joined under Biden in 2021, and withdrew again in 2025 — a pattern of reversal this 2016 ceremony did not anticipate, and one that has made the treaty's durability, rather than its initial ratification math, the more consequential story since.

25 years ago2001Durban walkout · HP–Compaq merger
POLITICS2001September 3, 2001

The US and Israel walk out of the UN's Durban racism conference

Three days into the UN World Conference against Racism in Durban, South Africa, the US and Israeli delegations withdraw over a draft declaration that would have equated Zionism with racism and singled out Israel for war-crimes language. Secretary of State Colin Powell had already decided in July not to attend at all.

The conference continues without them for another five days, adopting a final declaration with the most contested language removed, but the walkout becomes the episode most remembered from it.

Context & background. The draft language the delegations objected to had been pushed largely by an Arab-states bloc and some NGO forums running in parallel with the official conference; earlier preparatory sessions in Tehran and Geneva had already produced similar disputes over Israel-related wording, so the Durban walkout was the culmination of a fight that had been building for months, not a surprise on the day.

The details. Because the conference closed just three days before the 11 September attacks, its findings and news coverage were almost immediately overshadowed; the compromise final declaration that Durban did eventually adopt dropped the "Zionism is racism" language but still drew criticism from Israel and Jewish organizations over other passages on the Middle East.

Why it still matters. The dispute set the template for every subsequent UN racism review conference: follow-up sessions in 2009 ("Durban II") and 2011 ("Durban III") were both boycotted by the US, Israel and a growing list of Western governments citing the same concerns, making the 2001 walkout the opening move in a diplomatic pattern still in force today.

BUSINESS2001September 4, 2001

Hewlett-Packard agrees to buy Compaq in a $25 billion all-stock deal

HP chief executive Carly Fiorina and Compaq's Michael Capellas announce an all-stock merger valued around $25 billion, betting that combining the world's two largest PC makers can offset a slumping PC market and challenge Dell and IBM. The announcement, made over the US Labor Day holiday, catches much of the tech press off guard.

It becomes the largest merger in computer-industry history to that point — and one of the most bitterly contested, as HP co-founder heir Walter Hewlett launches a public proxy fight against his own company's board.

Context & background. Both companies had spent 2001 watching PC demand slow sharply after the dot-com crash; Fiorina's pitch, internally contested from the start, was that scale in a shrinking market beat standing alone. Walter Hewlett and other founding-family shareholders, controlling over 100 million shares, argued the opposite — that HP was buying its way into Compaq's own troubled low-margin PC business at a bad moment.

The details. The March 2002 shareholder vote passed by a margin narrow enough that Hewlett sued to overturn it, alleging HP had improperly pressured a major fund manager; a Delaware judge rejected the challenge and the deal closed in May 2002. Roughly 15,000 of the promised layoffs followed as HP absorbed Compaq's workforce.

Why it still matters. The integration struggled for years, and Fiorina was ousted as CEO in February 2005 over the board's dissatisfaction with results; the deal is still taught in business schools as a cautionary case on scale-for-its-own-sake mergers, even though HP's PC and printer business, built substantially on Compaq's assets, remained a top-two global PC vendor for the following two decades.

50 years ago1976Viking 2 lands on Mars · Belenko defects · the Harrison verdict
SCIENCE1976September 3, 1976

NASA's Viking 2 lander touches down on Mars at Utopia Planitia

Six weeks after its twin, Viking 1, became the first US spacecraft to land safely on Mars, Viking 2 sets down in the northern plain of Utopia Planitia and begins transmitting the first color panoramas from that part of the planet. Both landers carry biology experiments designed to test Martian soil for signs of microbial life.

Neither lander's results settle the life question one way or the other; the debate over how to interpret them continues among planetary scientists to this day.

Context & background. A separate Wikipedia month-chronicle page lists this landing under 6 September instead, apparently conflating it with a later imaging milestone; NASA's own mission record states 3 September directly, and that is the date used here — a small but instructive case of exactly the aggregator drift this briefing is built to catch.

The details. Viking 2's most-cited experiment, the "labeled release" test, produced a positive-looking result that its own designer, Gilbert Levin, argued for decades afterward was genuine evidence of microbial metabolism; the mission's other instruments found no organic molecules at all, a contradiction later attributed to a reactive Martian soil chemistry rather than biology, though the argument was never fully closed.

Why it still matters. The unresolved Viking biology results are the direct reason NASA's subsequent Mars missions — from Pathfinder through Curiosity and Perseverance — were deliberately designed around habitability and sample-return rather than a repeat life-detection gamble, a caution that traces straight back to this landing's ambiguous data.

POLITICS1976September 6, 1976

Soviet pilot Viktor Belenko defects to Japan, handing the West a MiG-25

Flying a routine training sortie from a Soviet Far East air base, Lieutenant Viktor Belenko breaks formation, drops to treetop level to evade radar, and flies his MiG-25P interceptor to Hakodate, Japan, landing with roughly 30 seconds of fuel left and overrunning the runway. He immediately requests asylum.

The aircraft — the same "Foxbat" interceptor Western intelligence had rated as the most feared fighter in the Soviet arsenal — gives American and Japanese engineers their first direct look at the jet's actual construction.

Context & background. Belenko had planned the defection alone for months, telling no one; his aircraft nearly collided with a Japanese airliner during its low-altitude dash and, once on the ground, Japanese police had to physically block Soviet officials from approaching it before US technical teams arrived.

The details. Dismantling the jet found it was built mostly of heavy stainless steel rather than titanium, its cockpit avionics ran on nuvistors — hardened vacuum-tube-style components chosen for radiation resistance and battlefield ruggedness rather than sophistication — and its top speed was constrained well below the figures Western analysts had assumed, because pushing it further risked destroying the engines.

Why it still matters. The inspection deflated years of Western threat assessments built on the MiG-25's reputation and directly shaped how the US approached its next air-superiority fighters; Belenko was resettled in the US under a new identity, wrote the bestselling account "MiG Pilot" in 1980, and the Soviets, furious, forced Japan into weeks of tense negotiation before agreeing to take the disassembled aircraft back at all.

CULTURE1976August 31, 1976

A US judge finds George Harrison guilty of "subconsciously" copying a hit song

Ruling in a five-year-old suit, US District Judge Richard Owen finds that Harrison's 1970 chart-topper "My Sweet Lord" unintentionally copied the melody of the Chiffons' 1963 hit "He's So Fine," written by Ronnie Mack. Owen accepts that Harrison did not deliberately plagiarize the song but rules that is no defense, coining the phrase "subconscious plagiarism."

The case, still cited in copyright disputes today, drags on for years after this verdict over exactly how much Harrison owes and to whom.

Context & background. The twist that made the case notorious: Allen Klein, Harrison's own former manager, had negotiated on his behalf early in the dispute, then after their acrimonious split switched sides and began advising the plaintiff, Bright Tunes, against his former client.

The details. In 1978, with damages still unresolved, Klein's company ABKCO bought Bright Tunes outright for $587,000 — putting Harrison's former manager in position to collect the very damages he was suing him for. A 1981 ruling capped what Klein could recover at his purchase price and ordered any excess redirected to Harrison, closing off the conflict-of-interest windfall.

Why it still matters. "Subconscious plagiarism" became a standing legal doctrine, invoked directly in later high-profile music cases including the "Blurred Lines" and "Stairway to Heaven" disputes, making Owen's 1976 finding one of the most consequential single rulings in modern music-copyright law.

Editor's view

Five of this week's eight stories are a single ceremonial date standing in for a much longer process: Mother Teresa's canonization closed out 19 years of Vatican process, the Paris Agreement ratification was the last step in nine months of quiet US-China diplomacy, the Harrison verdict capped five years of litigation still not fully resolved, and even HP-Compaq's announcement was the public surfacing of weeks of secret boardroom negotiation. The dated headline is real, but in each case it is the visible tip of something that took far longer to build — a reminder that "on this day" framing, this briefing's own included, always compresses more than it shows.
