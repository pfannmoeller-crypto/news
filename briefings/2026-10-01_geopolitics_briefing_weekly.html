Geopolitics · Week of 25 September – 1 October 2026

Washington and Tehran trade counteroffers as Russia opens its winter strike campaign

The United States and Iran have been at war since US strikes on Iran on 28 February 2026. Russia’s full-scale invasion of Ukraine began in February 2022 and continues.

This week Iran and the US swapped ceasefire proposals but disagree about what must come first: reopening the Strait of Hormuz, the narrow sea passage that much of the world’s oil ships through, and ending the US naval blockade of Iranian ports, or Iran’s nuclear programme. Ships in the strait have been hit by projectiles nobody has claimed. In Ukraine, Ukraine’s offer to stop attacks on power plants and fuel facilities, if Russia does the same, was followed by a large Russian strike on Ukraine’s power system, and Russia’s government took over the Russian businesses of several well-known European companies in response to Western economic penalties. Along the border between NATO (North Atlantic Treaty Organisation), the Western military alliance, and Russia, drone and airspace incidents continue, and one drone has now come down in Moldova, which is outside the alliance.

Where things stood a week ago

bookmarkRecap of 18–24 September

  • Ukraine’s diplomacy: On 22 September Ukraine’s president, Volodymyr Zelensky, met US president Donald Trump for about 40 minutes at the United Nations General Assembly in New York. Zelensky said Ukraine was “ready for any kind of format of energy ceasefire”, meaning each side would stop attacking the other’s power plants and fuel facilities. The Institute for the Study of War, a Washington research group that tracks the war daily, reported that Russian government officials had already rejected a similar proposal. No meeting between Trump, Zelensky and Russia’s president, Vladimir Putin, had a date.
  • Russian politics: Russia’s parliamentary election ended with Putin’s party, United Russia, certified at just under 58% of the vote and 355 of the 450 seats in the State Duma (the lower house of parliament), on 59.32% turnout. Yabloko, the only anti-war party, had been barred from the ballot.
  • Iran’s halt in attacks: By one website’s tally, Iran had gone 14 days without firing a ballistic missile and 18 without a drone attack at its Gulf neighbours, though neither government had declared a ceasefire. On 22 September US envoys Steve Witkoff (Trump’s special envoy for the Middle East) and Jared Kushner (a senior White House adviser) met Iran’s foreign minister, Abbas Araghchi, for about three hours, the first direct talks since June. Iran then asked for $24 billion of Iranian money frozen abroad to be released up front, and for Saudi Arabia to end its blockade of areas of Yemen held by the Houthis, a Yemeni armed group Iran backs.
  • Hormuz shipping: The Strait of Hormuz is the narrow sea passage between Iran and Oman through which much of the world’s seaborne oil travels. On 20 September PortWatch, a ship-tracking project of the International Monetary Fund (the global lending body), counted one commercial ship passing through, against about 85 a day before the war. Earlier, Reuters (a news agency) and Windward (a ship-tracking company) had reported different numbers on back-to-back days. Brent crude, the benchmark oil price, had fallen to $98.80 a barrel on 22 September.
  • Accountability: United Nations investigators said on 21 September that there are “reasonable grounds to believe” the US committed war crimes in the strikes that began the war on 28 February, and the UN Human Rights Council extended their work to 2028. Neither the US nor Iran’s government engaged with the findings.
  • Sanctions: A US law signed on 18 September, the Lindsey O. Graham Sanctioning Russia and Iran Act, lets the president put tariffs of up to 100% on imports from countries that buy Russian energy. Analysts expected it to be applied unevenly: India, a large buyer of Russian oil, was given a 25% tariff, not the full rate, under similar powers in August 2025, and it was removed in February 2026.
  • Drones and airspace: Lithuania’s president said the drone shot down over his country on 15 September was a Gerbera-2, a cheap Russian decoy drone, though another account called its origin unconfirmed. On 23 September a Russian Mi-8 military helicopter spent 42 seconds in Polish airspace near Kaliningrad, the Russian territory between Poland and Lithuania.

Ukraine / Russia — Russia strikes Ukraine’s power system while talks stall

3 items
Strikes29–30 Sep

Russia’s strike on Kyiv’s power plants is the biggest energy attack in months, Ukraine’s prime minister says

Overnight into 30 September Russia fired at least 188 drones plus hypersonic, ballistic and cruise missiles (several types of long-range missile) at Ukrainian power facilities, including a hydroelectric plant at Vyshhorod and a thermal plant at Trypilska, both near Kyiv, according to United Press International, a news agency. At least three people were killed and five injured. Ukraine’s prime minister, Serhii Koretskyi, called it “the biggest energy attack in months”, saying such a massive combined strike had not happened “since the end of the last heating season”, the cold months when homes need power for warmth.

The Institute for the Study of War, a Washington research group that tracks the war daily, reports a second wave in daylight on 30 September: 285 drones and some ballistic missiles, on top of the overnight 188.

Context & background. On 22 September Ukraine’s president, Volodymyr Zelensky, offered to stop Ukrainian attacks on Russian oil facilities if Russia stopped hitting Ukraine’s power system. Russia has not publicly accepted. Al Jazeera reported that Russian attacks killed six people the day after Zelensky’s talks with US president Donald Trump, so this is at least the second large strike since the offer.

The details. The two figures, 188 and 285, are separate waves. The Institute for the Study of War says Russia’s president, Vladimir Putin, now aims to force Ukraine to surrender by crippling its power supply over winter 2026–27, with the campaign starting ahead of the cold months. That is the Institute’s reading of Russian intent; Russia has not said so. Local power distributors restored electricity to 8,000 customers in eastern Kyiv, while Ukraine’s national grid operator reported outages in several regions; no overall figure for people without power has been published. Ukraine’s energy minister said in August that more than 80% of the country’s power generation had been destroyed or damaged, according to Russia Matters, a research project that publishes a weekly war summary, so the system being hit was already badly degraded.

Why it still matters. Zelensky has offered an energy pause, and Russia has said it would accept one only if it also covered its oil and gas exports by sea and came with relief from Western sanctions (Al Jazeera, 22 September); no pause is in place. The prime minister’s “in months” wording shows earlier large strikes happened, and the partial restoration of power in Kyiv shows repair crews are still working. A sustained run of strikes at this scale, or a public Russian reply to Zelensky’s offer, would change the picture.

Diplomacy28 Sep

Trump says Zelensky will “probably” ease Ukraine’s drone strikes on Russian oil refineries; Kyiv has not confirmed it

On 28 September US president Donald Trump told reporters he asked Ukraine’s president, Volodymyr Zelensky, to “slow down” Ukrainian attacks on Russian oil refineries, the plants that turn crude oil into petrol and diesel. Trump said “the big problem is the refineries in Russia are being blown up” and that the strikes could cause a “world problem”. Asked how Zelensky replied, Trump said: “Well, we’ll probably do that.”

Only Trump has described the exchange. Neither Zelensky nor his office has confirmed it, and Russia has not commented. It came six days after Zelensky said Ukraine would stop attacking Russian refineries if Russia stopped attacking Ukraine’s power system, and two days before the large Russian strike on that system (see the card above).

Context & background. Ukraine’s attacks on Russian refineries are its most effective long-range pressure on Russia, and they cost Washington something: on 22 September Trump said they were pushing up global diesel prices before the US congressional elections on 3 November. Russia’s stated position, according to Al Jazeera, is that it would accept a pause in energy attacks only if it also covered Russian oil and gas exports by sea and came with relief from Western sanctions.

The details. Russia Matters’ weekly war summary puts damage to Russian refining at 20–40% of capacity by August, and Russian refining at 3.8 million barrels a day against 5.3–5.5 million a year earlier; it does not say how much of the fall is permanent. A Trump claim about an energy halt has been disputed by Kyiv before: on 13 September CNN (Cable News Network) ran the headline that Trump said Ukraine and Russia had agreed to stop targeting energy sites, while Kyiv called it a “proposal for a mutual halt”. Zelensky’s “probably” is a hedge, not a promise, and Ukraine’s condition, that Russia stop too, has not been met: Russia struck Kyiv’s power plants two days later.

Why it still matters. If Ukraine slowed its refinery strikes while Russia kept hitting Ukrainian power, it would be giving up its main pressure on Russia for nothing visible in return, so confirmation from Kyiv or Moscow matters more than Trump’s account. Open questions: whether any slowdown has begun, whether Russia ties it to sanctions relief, and whether the 30 September strike was Russia’s answer. The remark may also describe a general understanding rather than a specific promise.

Front line22–30 Sep

The front line barely moves: Russia lost net ground in the week to 29 September

Russia captured about 14 square miles of Ukrainian territory between 1 and 29 September, up from about 5 square miles in the previous four weeks, but lost a net 6 square miles in the final week, 22–29 September, according to Russia Matters, a research project that publishes a weekly war summary. The Institute for the Study of War, a Washington research group that tracks the war daily, says Ukrainian forces recently advanced north of Sumy, a city in north-eastern Ukraine.

Russia now holds roughly 12% of Ukraine, about 28,210 square miles gained since its invasion in February 2022. Ground change is small; the weight of this week’s war is in the strikes on energy.

Context & background. The line has been largely static for weeks, so a month of small Russian gains followed by a week in which Ukraine regained more than Russia took fits the pattern.

The details. Territory estimates vary by source, and one week of net loss is a small sample; September’s total is still larger than the previous four weeks’. Russia Matters puts Russian military casualties (killed and wounded) near 1 million and Ukrainian military losses at 250,000–300,000, with more than 17,000 Ukrainian civilians killed and nearly 54,000 injured; about 9.6 million Ukrainians (22% of the pre-war population) are displaced. Opinion has moved toward talks on both sides: 59% of Russians favour peace negotiations and 69% of Ukrainians support a negotiated settlement. Russia’s government claims it has captured about 2,050 square miles since 2026 began, a figure independent analysts dispute significantly (Al Jazeera, 25 September).

Why it still matters. With the ground nearly still, both countries are trying to change the balance by hitting each other’s energy, and both publics say they want talks. That is the setting for the two cards above. A multi-week run of Russian net gains, or a Ukrainian advance beyond local ground near Sumy, would change this picture.

Iran / Hormuz — counteroffers, a US blockade and unexplained attacks on tankers

3 items
Diplomacy26–30 Sep

Trump rejects Iran’s seven-day ceasefire offer, then sends a counterproposal Iran’s government is now reviewing

Iran offered to reopen the Strait of Hormuz, the narrow sea passage that much of the world’s oil ships through and that has been effectively closed since the war began, and to restart talks on its nuclear programme within seven days, if the United States ended its naval blockade of Iranian ports, waived sanctions on Iranian oil sales and observed a ceasefire that includes Lebanon, the news agency Associated Press reported. On 26 September US president Donald Trump said “that deal would not be acceptable”, adding that Iran wanted talks because it was “losing so badly”. On 27 September he told the US news site Axios: “I expect more talks with Iran [this week]. They want to make a deal, but it is not the deal that I want to make.”

On Wednesday 30 September Iran’s foreign minister, Abbas Araghchi, presented a US counterproposal for a seven-day ceasefire to Iran’s president, Masoud Pezeshkian, at a cabinet meeting, according to a US local-news report that cites Qatari mediators, meaning officials from Qatar passing messages between the sides. The proposal’s terms on Hormuz have not been made public.

Context & background. On 22 September US envoys Steve Witkoff (Trump’s special envoy for the Middle East) and Jared Kushner (a senior White House adviser) met Araghchi for about three hours at the United Nations in New York, the first direct talks since June. Iran’s asking price then was $24 billion of Iranian money frozen abroad, released up front, plus an end to Saudi Arabia’s blockade of Houthi-held Yemen. Now the demands centre on the US naval blockade and Iranian oil sales, and the ceasefire has a length, seven days, and a geography, Lebanon included.

The details. Axios reports that Iran puts Hormuz and the blockade first while the Trump administration wants Iranian nuclear concessions first. That is the disagreement. Trump also posted an image renaming the waterway the “Trump Strait”, according to the Associated Press. Trump denied reports that the US had offered sanctions relief in exchange for nuclear concessions, calling them a “hoax”, and one report on the exchange says “neither side is willing to make significant concessions”. Two further reports have one source each: a US television network’s live coverage says US Secretary of State (the US foreign minister) Marco Rubio reportedly asked Iran’s United Nations delegation to leave early, which Iran denied, saying its departure was scheduled; and the Associated Press notes that Pezeshkian’s most recent meeting with Iran’s supreme leader lasted seven hours, without saying what was decided.

Why it still matters. Both governments have now put terms on paper, which moves the dispute from whether to talk to what comes first; Iran’s answer to the counterproposal will show how firm each position is. Open questions: the counterproposal’s text, whether the talks Trump said he expected this week take place, and whether the attacks on ships (next card) are connected. A public rejection and a private counteroffer can run together, as they did this week.

Hormuz shipping27 Sep – 1 Oct

Three tankers are hit in Hormuz by unidentified projectiles as reports of how much oil is moving disagree

Three ships, an oil tanker, a liquefied natural gas tanker and a third vessel, were hit by “unknown projectiles” in the Strait of Hormuz on Wednesday 30 September, shortly after Iran received the US counterproposal, according to reports citing the United Kingdom Maritime Trade Operations centre, a UK navy-run shipping-safety monitor. Nobody has claimed responsibility. A ship-tracking website reports that a Kuwaiti tanker, the Al Funtas, was struck on 28 September and that a seafarer died aboard another vessel, the Cape Dao, on 23 September.

Reports of how much oil is leaving the Gulf disagree. US president Donald Trump said the largest oil volume since the war began passed through the strait over the weekend, over 20 million barrels, and a US defence official told Axios 22 million barrels were exported on Friday night. PortWatch, a ship-tracking project of the International Monetary Fund, counted one ship passing through on 27 September, against about 85 a day before the war. Brent crude, the global oil price benchmark, stood near $98 a barrel on 30 September and about $100 on 1 October.

Context & background. Earlier in September Reuters (a news agency) counted four ships passing through on one day and Windward (a ship-tracking company) twelve on the next, and PortWatch logged a single ship on 20 September. This week adds a third kind of number, barrels exported, from US officials. Saudi Arabia’s East–West pipeline, restarted on 22 September, carries oil across the kingdom to the Red Sea, so oil can leave the region without passing through the strait.

The details. The measures are not the same. PortWatch counts only ships that broadcast their location, and the tracking site reports that the struck vessels were moving through the strait with their location beacons switched off, which would leave some traffic out of the count. A US television network’s live coverage put exports at roughly 21.8 million barrels against 23.3 million before the war, without a clear time period. The tracking site also cites a trade publication saying Iran’s “grip on strait traffic is loosening”, meaning Iran’s ability to restrict who passes, and CNBC reporting that crude oil exports have recovered to pre-war levels while refined fuels such as diesel remain constrained and depend on US military protection. It adds that insurance for ships entering the area, called war-risk insurance, costs about 40 times normal and that one supertanker passage can cost about $10 million in premiums, the fees paid for it; that figure has one source. The site also dates the three-tanker strike to 1 October, while other reports say 30 September.

Why it still matters. Each side wants to show it controls the strait: if Washington can show oil flowing under its protection, Iran’s hold over the strait looks weaker, and continued attacks on tankers would weaken that claim. Because the figures differ so widely, none of them should be read as the true state of the strait. A count that includes ships with their beacons off, or a named attacker, would settle part of it.

These statements come while the two governments are exchanging ceasefire proposals (first card of this section). No ceasefire has been declared, and no new round of Iranian missile launches at Gulf states has been reported.

Sanctions & Trade — Russia answers Western sanctions with seizures

1 item
Retaliation18–30 Sep

Russia seizes Nestlé, Auchan and Metro operations and signals more to come for European firms

Russia’s president, Vladimir Putin, signed a decree on 18 September putting the Russian assets of Nestlé, the Swiss food group, and Auchan, the French supermarket chain, under a state-appointed manager, L.E.V. Management, a company with no public profile, using a 2023 decree that covers assets from countries Russia calls “unfriendly”, the Moscow Times reported. Dmitry Peskov, spokesman for the Russian president’s office (the Kremlin), said the targets were “European companies from unfriendly countries” involved “in the most active manner in military actions against our country”. Modern Diplomacy, a news site, reports that the German wholesaler Metro was also placed under temporary management in September.

The seizures answer Western sanctions, the economic penalties imposed on Russia over the war. On 30 September the Institute for the Study of War, a Washington research group that tracks the war daily, reported that the Russian government is signalling it intends to seize more assets of European companies and keep them as a lever to push European states to drop sanctions. Nestlé said it would take “all necessary steps to protect its rights”; Auchan declined to comment.

Context & background. The Graham Act, signed the same day as the decree, is the main new US pressure tool: it lets the president put tariffs of up to 100% on countries that buy Russian energy. No use of its tariff power has been reported, so the live sanctions development is Russia’s countermove.

The details. The Moscow Times calls the Nestlé and Auchan action the most significant seizure since Danone and Carlsberg, two European food and drink companies whose Russian assets were taken in 2023, and cites a 2025 estimate that Russia has seized more than $50 billion of private property since 2022. Nestlé’s Russian business covers coffee, pet care and infant formula and generated about $2.4 billion in sales in 2021; its shares fell 2.4% on the news. Modern Diplomacy quotes officials calling the moves “asymmetric measures”, meaning a response in a different form from the sanctions they answer, prompted by “the EU’s active support of hostilities against Russia”, and says Peskov noted the decisions could in theory be reversed, citing “the lack of dialogue from the EU”. The same report says Russia has put 135 firms tied to foreign companies, mostly European, under temporary administration, against 22 Western firms reported before; that count comes from that outlet alone and the two numbers may not measure the same thing. It also says no major US company has been targeted.

Why it still matters. The seizures are presented as a reply to the European Union’s freeze of about €210 billion in Russian state assets, which turns private European property into a bargaining chip in the same dispute. It differs from the US approach, where the main tool is a tariff whose maximum has so far been used sparingly. Open questions: how many more firms are on any list, whether Nestlé or Auchan go to court, and whether Peskov’s remark is a real offer or a negotiating position.

Drones and deniable pressure on Europe — Poland tightens patrols; a drone falls in Moldova

1 item
Airspace incidents17–30 Sep

Poland adds air patrols and a Russian drone crashes in Moldova, while NATO reportedly sees Russia turning more cautious

After alleged Russian incursions into its airspace, Poland is stepping up air patrols. Its defence minister, Władysław Kosiniak-Kamysz, said on 25 September: “We are increasing the activity of our air forces, including F-16 aircraft [fighter jets] and helicopters”, after preventive patrols began on 17 September, Al Jazeera reported. Prime Minister Donald Tusk said: “According to intelligence, the Russian plan includes hybrid drone and missile strikes against countries supporting Ukraine, including Poland”, meaning a mix of military and deniable attacks short of open war.

On the morning of 30 September a likely Russian drone crashed near Hirbovat, a village in Moldova, the Institute for the Study of War, a Washington research group that tracks the war daily, reports. Moldova is not a member of NATO (North Atlantic Treaty Organisation), the Western military alliance. A separate report on 28 September says NATO has observed Russia showing “increased caution” over drone flights near the borders of Poland and the Baltic states (Estonia, Latvia and Lithuania), which it attributes to stronger air defences; it names no official.

Context & background. Poland’s step-up follows a Russian military helicopter’s 42-second entry into Polish airspace on 23 September near Kaliningrad, a Russian territory between Poland and Lithuania, and the shooting-down of a drone over Lithuania on 15 September that Lithuania’s president called a Russian Gerbera-2, a cheap decoy drone; another account called its origin unconfirmed.

The details. The two signals pull against each other. Polish leaders speak of planned hybrid strikes, which implies more incidents, while the 28 September report says Russia is being more cautious. That report gives no comparative incident figures and names no NATO official; its one number is Lithuania’s six responses between 21 and 27 September. It also says Russian military aircraft flying to and from Kaliningrad do so without pre-planned flight schedules and in some cases with their location beacons switched off. The Moldova crash is described as “likely” Russian by the Institute for the Study of War; no statement from Moldova’s government or analysis of the wreckage is available.

Why it still matters. A drone landing in Moldova puts the same pattern outside the alliance, where NATO’s pledge to defend its members does not apply and the response options differ. It is the accumulation of small incidents, rather than any one, that Poland’s government is reacting to. An incident with casualties or serious damage, a confirmed attribution of the Moldova drone, or incident data that backs or refutes the “more cautious” claim would change this.

lightbulbEditor’s view

Proposals are being exchanged while the fighting and the pressure continue

1. Energy is now the contested ground in Ukraine. Russia’s heaviest strike on the power system in months came eight days after Ukraine’s president Volodymyr Zelensky offered to stop attacks on energy sites if Russia did the same, and two days after US president Donald Trump asked Ukraine to ease refinery strikes. The only public movement toward a pause is Trump’s account of a request; neither Ukraine nor Russia has confirmed it.

2. The US–Iran gap is about order, not about whether to talk. Iran wants the strait and the US blockade dealt with first, the US wants Iran’s nuclear concessions first, and both sides have now put ceasefire proposals on the table. A US president who says he expects talks this week and also doubts that peace is possible “ever” leaves both readings open.

3. The numbers on Hormuz have become evidence in the dispute. US officials cite barrels exported, trackers count ships, and ships are reportedly sailing with their location beacons off. Three tankers were hit by attackers nobody has identified. No single figure here settles the state of the strait.

4. Whether Iran’s halt in missile and drone attacks still holds is unconfirmed. No new Iranian launches and no declared ceasefire have been reported, but the latest day-count available ends on 23 September. It remains an open question, not a fact.

5. Russia’s answer to sanctions is to seize European-owned businesses, not to negotiate. Taking over the Russian operations of Nestlé, Auchan and Metro links private European property to the dispute over Russian state assets frozen in the European Union. The new US tariff power has not been reported used.

6. The border between NATO and Russia sends mixed signals. Poland is adding patrols on intelligence that Russia is planning mixed military and deniable strikes, while one report says Russia is turning more cautious. A drone in Moldova shows the pressure also falls on a country with no NATO protection.

What to watch: Iran’s reply to the US counterproposal and whether talks take place; whether Russia keeps up strikes at this scale and whether Ukraine publicly changes its refinery strikes; whether the list of seized European businesses grows; and whether the new US tariff power is used against a named buyer of Russian energy.
