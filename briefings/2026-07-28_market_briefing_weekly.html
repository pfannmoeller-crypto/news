Markets & Macro · Weekly

The AI-capex reckoning went global this week: a Chinese chip IPO detonated into South Korea's worst trading day in decades, even as a fragile US-Iran pause dragged oil back from $101 into the mid-$80s — with the Fed and four megacap earnings still to come.

Last week's edition closed on Alphabet and Tesla's punished earnings and Brent above $100. This week opened with genuine relief: the US paused its strikes on Iran late Friday, Tehran reciprocated, and Oman-mediated talks on Hormuz shipping access made enough progress that oil fell hard — WTI down roughly 8% on Monday alone. That relief did not extend to chips. China's ChangXin Memory Technologies (CXMT) staged one of the biggest IPO debuts on record Monday, and the resulting fear of Chinese memory competition, layered on an already-nervous market, hammered SanDisk, Micron and Western Digital Monday before detonating Tuesday into a genuine shock: South Korea's Kospi fell nearly 11%, its worst session since March, triggering a market-wide circuit breaker as Samsung and SK Hynix posted their steepest single-day losses in over a decade. Nvidia fell 5% Monday on renewed "circular financing" concerns tied to a reported $250B OpenAI backstop, letting Apple retake the title of world's most valuable company. All of this lands in the exact middle of the two events this briefing has been building toward for a month: the Fed's rate decision Wednesday, and Wednesday-Thursday's earnings from Microsoft, Meta, Apple and Amazon — the second, much larger round of the "spending versus returns" test that Alphabet and Tesla just failed.

SemiconductorsMon 27 – Tue 28 Jul

A Chinese chip IPO detonates into Korea's worst trading day in decades: Kospi −10.84%, circuit breaker triggered, Samsung and SK Hynix post their steepest falls in over a decade

China's ChangXin Memory Technologies (CXMT) debuted on Shanghai's STAR Market Monday and soared more than 500%, closing its first day as mainland China's most valuable company at roughly $540B market cap on an $8.6–9.8B raise. CXMT is only the world's fourth-largest DRAM maker (8% share, versus Samsung's 36%, SK Hynix's 29% and Micron's 24%), but reports that Apple is testing its DRAM chips revived fears that Chinese memory could reach top-tier customers sooner than assumed. US memory names sold off hard Monday — SanDisk −12%, Western Digital −7%, SK Hynix's ADR −6%, Micron −5%, the Roundhill Memory ETF −4% — and the story escalated overnight into a genuine shock: South Korea's Kospi fell 10.84%, its worst session since a record March decline, forcing the Korea Exchange to activate a market-wide circuit breaker for 20 minutes. Samsung closed 13.4% lower (its worst day in nearly two decades) and SK Hynix fell roughly 14.7% — the two companies together account for close to half the index. The rout spread further: Japan's Nikkei and Taiwan's benchmark each fell almost 4%, and the MSCI Asia Pacific Index is now down more than 10% from its 22 June peak, entering correction territory.

Context & background: This is not the first CXMT scare this briefing's sourcing has tracked — a mid-July article referenced in this week's research already showed SK Hynix and SanDisk sinking 7% and Micron falling 5% on 16 July as CXMT readied its IPO, and the memory sector had separately reversed a Tuesday rally the week before on a Korea-linked selloff. What changed this week is scale: the IPO itself, not just anticipation of it, landed, and it landed spectacularly — a 500%+ first-day pop is an extreme outcome even by 2026's standards for AI-adjacent listings, and it forced markets to price CXMT as a real competitor rather than a future possibility. The deeper backdrop is a memory sector that had rallied 200–500% year-to-date on acute DRAM/NAND shortage pricing, which left every incumbent name priced for a supply picture that new Chinese capacity, even years away from mattering commercially, complicates.

The details: CXMT remains commercially constrained — it is on the Pentagon's list of firms with alleged military ties, some US lawmakers have signaled interest in restricting purchases of its chips, and it still lacks the advanced lithography tools (under US export control) to match Samsung, SK Hynix or Micron's cutting-edge process nodes. Analysts quoted in Monday's coverage explicitly said CXMT is unlikely to ease the near-term memory shortage that has driven 2026's pricing boom. None of that mattered to Tuesday's price action, which read as a valuation reset rather than a fundamentals reset: the Roundhill Memory ETF's concentration (Samsung, SK Hynix and Micron are 72% of its assets) mechanically amplified the swing, and SK Hynix's own Q2 earnings — due after Tuesday's US close — added a second layer of pre-earnings de-risking on top of the competition scare. Two counterweights existed going into the rout: South Korea unveiled a $950B AI initiative involving Samsung and SK Group over the weekend, and Morgan Stanley and Mizuho had both characterized the recent memory weakness as a buying opportunity rather than a downturn's start.

Context: This closes a loop this briefing has followed for three weeks: a memory rally built on a single bullish price call, a reversal built on Korea-linked contagion, and now a third act in which the contagion channel itself — the tight mechanical linkage between US memory names and Korean chipmakers via shared index and ETF exposure — became the story rather than a side effect of it. It also broadens the "AI spending versus returns" argument this briefing tracked through Alphabet and Tesla into a new dimension: the question is no longer only whether hyperscaler capex will pay off, but whether the supply side of the AI buildout (memory, in particular) is about to face genuine new competition from China years earlier than priced. A circuit-breaker-triggering, near-11% single-day index move in a G20 economy is a systemically significant event by any measure, not a routine sector rotation.

Watch-Items: SK Hynix's Q2 report, due after Tuesday's US close, for whether management addresses the CXMT threat directly. Whether Wednesday sees follow-through selling in Asia or a technical bounce given how oversold the circuit-breaker move left the Kospi. Whether US memory names (SanDisk, Micron, Western Digital) stabilize or extend Monday's losses once Tuesday's Asia session is digested. And whether Chinese chipmaking-tool export-control policy sees any US legislative response given CXMT's Pentagon-list status.

NvidiaMon 27 Jul

Nvidia falls 5% on renewed circular-financing worries — and Apple retakes the world's-most-valuable-company crown

Nvidia shares fell nearly 5% to $196.51 Monday, their steepest drop since February, on reports the company is in talks to backstop up to $250B in funding for OpenAI to lease computing capacity tied to a US data-center project — an arrangement that revives "circular financing" concerns, where a chip supplier is also a major financial backer of its own biggest customer. Chinese competition compounded the move: the same CXMT IPO rattling memory stocks (see above) also raised concerns about China's progress on mass-producing chipmaking equipment more broadly. The pullback let Apple overtake Nvidia as the world's most valuable public company for the second time this month — a swing this briefing has now tracked twice, both times on the same logic: the market currently rewards distribution and monetization discipline over raw AI infrastructure exposure.

Big Techon deck Wed 29 – Thu 30 Jul

The market's second, bigger AI-spending test arrives this week: Microsoft and Meta report Wednesday, Apple and Amazon Thursday

Having just watched Alphabet and Tesla get punished for beats-plus-heavy-capex, the market faces the same test at greater scale this week. Microsoft and Meta report Wednesday 29 July — the same day as the Fed's rate decision — and Apple and Amazon report Thursday 30 July. Consensus estimates call for Meta revenue of $60.22B (EPS $7.14), Microsoft revenue of $87.61B (EPS $4.23), and Apple revenue of $108.86B (EPS $1.89). The capex backdrop is the real story: Google, Amazon, Microsoft and Meta together plan roughly $725B in 2026 capital expenditure, up 77% from 2025's $410B, with Microsoft's trailing-twelve-month spend of ~$97.2B expected to climb to $118.9B (+22%) and Meta's ~$75.8B expected to reach $135.2B (+79%). Fortune's framing of the setup: earnings "slam into a market in revolt over AI spending."

Context & background: This briefing's Alphabet/Tesla story last week called their earnings "the cleanest possible resolution" so far of whether AI capex reads as demand evidence or a return-on-investment red flag, and the market chose the skeptical reading even against solid headline beats. This week's four reports are a strictly larger version of the same test: combined, Microsoft, Meta, Apple and Amazon represent a bigger share of both index weight and AI capex than Alphabet and Tesla did alone, and they arrive with the market already unsettled by Monday's Nvidia drop and Tuesday's Kospi crash — both AI-capex-adjacent stories in their own right.

The details: The capex numbers are genuinely extraordinary in scale: a 77% year-over-year jump to $725B combined is one of the largest single-year increases in corporate capital spending across any sector in memory, and the two steepest individual trajectories — Meta's planned 79% increase and Microsoft's 22% increase off an already-large base — are precisely the kind of guidance that cost Alphabet 7% last week. Unlike Alphabet, though, Apple has spent the past two weeks being explicitly rewarded by the market for comparatively frugal AI spending (partnering rather than building), which sets up a real divergence risk within this week's four reports: Amazon and Microsoft's infrastructure-heavy models face the harshest version of last week's bar, while Apple's asset-light approach may again be read more favorably regardless of its underlying numbers.

Context: The scheduling itself is a genuine risk-concentration event: Wednesday alone carries both the Fed's rate decision and two megacap earnings reports, meaning a single trading session could move on three largely independent catalysts simultaneously. If Microsoft or Meta deliver an Alphabet-style "beat but raise capex" outcome on the same day the Fed delivers a hawkish surprise, the combination could produce outsized moves purely from stacking, independent of how either signal would trade in isolation. This is the single most information-dense 48-hour window this briefing has flagged in over a month of tracking the AI-capex debate.

Watch-Items: Whether Microsoft and Meta face the same "beat isn't enough" reaction Alphabet did, or get more benefit of the doubt given different capex-to-revenue ratios. Whether Apple's Thursday report extends its two-week "spend less, win more" pattern. Amazon's AWS growth rate specifically, as the cloud unit most directly comparable to Google Cloud's beat that didn't save Alphabet. The Fed meets with hike odds rising, not falling, even as oil's retreat should argue the other way

The FOMC's two-day meeting runs Tuesday 28 through Wednesday 29 July, with the rate decision due at 2:00pm ET Wednesday and Chair Kevin Warsh's press conference thirty minutes later. This is a non-SEP meeting — no updated economic projections or dot plot — leaving the statement language and press conference as the market's only fresh signal. Consensus still expects a hold at 3.50–3.75%, but Polymarket's hike odds have risen since last week, now clustering in the 20–27% range (with the hold priced around 73–78%), up from 18.9%/80% a week ago — a shift that happened despite, not because of, Monday's sharp oil-price retreat on the Iran pause. The apparent tension: falling energy prices should ease the inflation case for tightening, yet elevated PCE inflation readings and the equity market's own AI-driven volatility appear to be keeping a meaningful minority of positioned money leaning toward a hike.

Context & background: Last week's edition flagged an unusually wide 15–20 point gap between Polymarket (80% hold) and CME FedWatch (~64% hold) heading into this meeting, and read that divergence itself as a signal of genuine uncertainty. Chair Warsh's reduced reliance on forward guidance, flagged last week, remains the structural wrinkle: without a dot plot at this meeting, markets have fewer Fed-supplied anchors than usual precisely when they most want one.

Context: A Fed that raises hike odds into a week when its own preferred inflation input (energy) is falling is a Fed whose reaction function markets currently cannot pin down — which is itself a market-moving fact independent of Wednesday's actual outcome. Combined with Wednesday's Microsoft/Meta earnings landing the same day, this sets up one of the more genuinely two-sided single trading sessions this briefing has flagged all year: a hold-plus-dovish-tone outcome paired with soft Big Tech guidance would read very differently than a hold-plus-hawkish-tone outcome paired with reassuring capex commentary, and current positioning looks unprepared for either extreme.

Iran · HormuzFri 25 Jul – ongoing

The US and Iran pause strikes for the first time in nearly two weeks, and oil gives back almost its entire post-Houthi spike

After roughly two weeks of nightly strikes, the US paused its bombing campaign against Iran late Friday 25 July; Tehran reciprocated, and both sides held the pause for a second straight day through the weekend. President Trump said Iran "asked very nicely" to stop and meet, while warning the US would resume attacks absent a new ceasefire deal; Iran's foreign ministry publicly denied "negotiations," even as its officials confirmed Oman-mediated technical talks on Hormuz shipping access were making "progress" — centered on a compromise where Iran would run vessel transit through the strait with fewer restrictions on ships. The market reaction was immediate and large: WTI fell roughly 8% Monday to around $82, and Brent eased from Thursday's $101.10 peak into the mid-$80s — unwinding nearly all of the prior week's Houthi-strike-driven spike within a matter of sessions.

Regulationwk of 24–28 Jul

CLARITY Act stalls on the Senate floor as an August 10 recess deadline closes in on its 2026 window

The crypto-market-structure bill has not advanced since clearing the Senate Banking Committee in May: it remains parked on the Senate legislative calendar with no floor vote scheduled and no cloture motion filed. Three interlocking disputes are blocking the seven to nine Democratic votes needed to clear the 60-vote filibuster threshold, with an ethics provision — barring sitting presidents and other officials from issuing or holding crypto tokens until 2029 — drawing particular Democratic objections over enforcement by the Justice Department. With the Senate's August 10 recess approaching, the window for a 2026 signature is narrowing fast; last week's 32.5% Polymarket odds of passage this year reflect that growing doubt.

lightbulbEditor's view

Two fronts of the same AI-spending anxiety converged this week — and the Fed and Big Tech haven't even reported yet

1. The AI-capex reckoning stopped being a US story this week. A single Chinese IPO turned into a nearly 11% single-day crash on South Korea's Kospi, with Samsung and SK Hynix posting their worst days in over a decade. That is a materially larger, faster shock than anything Alphabet's or Tesla's earnings produced last week, and it happened in a market this briefing wasn't tracking as the primary risk.

2. Genuine geopolitical relief and genuine market anxiety arrived in the same 72 hours. The US-Iran pause and Oman-mediated Hormuz progress are real de-escalation, and oil's roughly 8% Monday drop reflects that. But it did nothing to stop Tuesday's chip-sector shock, and Polymarket's Fed hike odds actually rose this week despite the falling oil price — a sign that inflation risk premium is now being priced from multiple, only loosely correlated sources.

3. Apple's "spend less, win more" pattern held for a third straight week. Nvidia's circular-financing scare handed Apple the world's-most-valuable-company title again. Whether that logic survives Apple's own earnings Thursday, against Amazon's and Microsoft's much heavier infrastructure spend reporting the same 48 hours, is one of this week's cleanest open questions.

4. Wednesday is now one of the most concentrated risk days this briefing has flagged all year. The Fed's decision, a press conference from a chair who has deliberately reduced forward guidance, and Microsoft and Meta's earnings all land the same session — on top of markets still digesting Tuesday's Kospi crash. Three largely independent catalysts stacking on one day raises the odds of an outsized move regardless of which way any single one breaks.

5. Bitcoin's month-long decoupling from equity risk-off cracked this week. It shrugged off oil spiking past $100 last week; it did not shrug off Tuesday's chip-driven Asia rout. That is a small but real data point that crypto's insulation from broader risk sentiment has limits.

6. The CLARITY Act's 2026 window is closing on the calendar, not on the substance. With no floor vote scheduled and the Senate's August 10 recess approaching, the bill's fate this year now depends on whether an ethics-provision dispute can be resolved in roughly two weeks — a legislative, not market, clock that crypto-policy watchers should track closely.

The wildcard heading into the second half of this week: whether Wednesday's Fed decision and Microsoft/Meta earnings calm the market that Tuesday's Kospi crash rattled, or compound it. This edition's read is that positioning across three continents is now thin and jumpy enough that either outcome — calm or compounding — could plausibly follow, and Thursday's Apple and Amazon reports will likely be read through whichever lens Wednesday sets.
