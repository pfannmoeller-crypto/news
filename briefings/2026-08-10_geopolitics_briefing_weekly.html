Geopolitics · 3–10 August 2026

The Strait of Hormuz stays effectively shut as Iran and Oman edge toward a deal that would formalise Tehran's grip on the waterway, the US Senate delivers final 86-12 passage of the Graham Act, a single day of cross-border strikes leaves 27 dead on both sides of the Russia-Ukraine front, and a drone that exploded in Bulgaria turns out — unusually — to likely be Ukrainian rather than Russian in origin.

The 2 August edition closed on a warm-but-thin Zelensky-Trump meeting, a Russian cruise missile crater in Poland, an Iranian missile-and-retaliation exchange with US forces, and a still-tentative Ukraine-Iran de-escalation over a Caspian Sea strike. None of those threads resolved cleanly this week. Instead, the most concrete movement came from Hormuz, where a temporary Iran-Oman arrangement is close enough that a Eurasia Group analyst described the US as likely to accept Iranian control "without explicitly acknowledging" it; from the Senate, which passed the Graham Act's final vote by the same 86-12 margin as its procedural vote ten days earlier; and from an odd, narrative-breaking hybrid incident in which the drone that crossed into Bulgaria appears to have come from Ukraine's own arsenal, not Russia's.

Ukraine / Russia — a 27-dead day on both sides of the border, and a Kyiv-visit timeline that keeps rewriting itself

2 items
Air war3 Aug

At least 27 killed as Russia and Ukraine trade heavy attacks — for once, the toll runs nearly even on both sides

Russian and Ukrainian local officials reported a combined death toll of at least 27 on 3 August as both sides exchanged drone and missile fire: 16 killed inside Russia (seven, including three children, when intercepted-drone debris fell on the Krasnodar resort village of Arkhipo-Osipovka; four in occupied Crimea; four in Belgorod's border settlements; five in a Moscow-region drone strike, per TASS) and 11 in Ukraine (eight, including two children, in an overnight attack on Zaporizhzhia; three, including an emergency responder and his eight-year-old son, at a petrol station in the Kryvyi Rih district of Dnipropetrovsk Oblast). Ukrainian drones also struck Wildberries warehouses in Vladimir Oblast and near St Petersburg, extending the economic-pressure campaign this briefing has tracked since July.

Context & background. Prior editions of this briefing have recorded Ukrainian civilian tolls from Russian strikes in detail but rarely a comparably sized Russian civilian toll on the same day — the 30 July and 1 August barrages killed eight and nine people respectively, all in Ukraine. 3 August is the first day this briefing has logged in which the reported death tolls on each side are close to symmetric (16 versus 11), which is itself informative about how the war's civilian cost is currently distributed.

The details. The mechanism behind Russia's larger figure matters: the seven deaths in Arkhipo-Osipovka, per Krasnodar governor Veniamin Kondratyev, came from debris of an intercepted drone falling on the village rather than a direct hit — meaning even successful Russian air defence is now generating civilian casualties inside Russia, a dynamic distinct from Ukraine's own toll, which came from strikes that were not intercepted. Al Jazeera's Kyiv correspondent Audrey MacAlpine reported that Zelensky continues to justify the Wildberries campaign by citing independent watchdog findings that the retailer sells "dual-use items" — used military helmets, protective gear, fibre-optic cable for drones — alongside consumer goods, a framing Kyiv has maintained since the campaign's expansion in July. Trump's Patriot-license reversal, reported last week, was referenced again in this day's coverage without any new movement, suggesting it remains the unresolved sticking point of the Washington relationship.

Context. A day with comparable death tolls on both sides does not indicate rough military parity — it more likely reflects the maturing of Ukraine's own deep-strike and drone campaign against Russian territory, which this briefing has tracked since the Wildberries and refinery strikes began, now generating Russian civilian casualties as an unintended by-product of intercepted ordnance rather than as a stated Ukrainian objective.

Watch-Items. (1) Whether Russian officials use the Arkhipo-Osipovka deaths to justify further strikes on Ukrainian cities. (2) Any Ukrainian statement addressing debris-related Russian civilian casualties specifically. (3) Further Wildberries strikes and whether the campaign's damage estimate (last put at roughly 10% of the network) is updated. (4) Confirmed final casualty figures, which regional officials were still revising as of this writing.

Confidence: high (regional governors and Al Jazeera reporting on record from both sides; precise final tolls still provisional)
Diplomacy4–9 Aug

The Witkoff/Kushner Kyiv visit gets a specific date — Independence Day, 24 August — then an entirely different one, "next week," from Russian state media

RFE/RL reported on 30 July, citing two people familiar with the planning, that envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner are expected to make their first-ever visit to Kyiv in the second half of August, potentially timed to Ukraine's 35th Independence Day on 24 August; Ukraine's ambassador to Washington, Olha Stefanishyna, confirmed the possibility was discussed but stressed arrangements remained unfinished. Zelensky said on 4 August that Ukraine is "ready for all formats" of talks and expects the envoys to "be active" in August. Then, on 9 August, Russian state agency TASS reported — per Eurasia Business News — that Witkoff and Kushner "may" visit both Kyiv and Moscow as soon as the following week, a materially earlier and differently sequenced timeline than the Independence Day plan reported ten days earlier.

Context & background. Last week's edition flagged this trip's timing as already disputed across outlets — "within the next two weeks" versus "the second half of August." This week narrows one version to a specific date (24 August) while simultaneously producing a second, earlier and Kremlin-sourced version ("next week," from roughly 9–16 August) that has not been corroborated by any Ukrainian or US outlet found in this briefing's research.

The details. The Independence Day framing has real logic behind it: if the visit happens on or around 24 August, it would let the envoys attend Ukraine's 35th-anniversary events while also holding the first direct consultations either has had with Kyiv's political and military leadership — as opposed to Witkoff's roughly eight prior trips to Moscow. The competing TASS-sourced report is harder to weigh: it comes from Russian state media relaying unnamed sourcing, has not been matched by Axios, RFE/RL, Kyiv Post or Ukrainska Pravda reporting in the same window, and could reflect either a genuine acceleration of planning or a Kremlin-side signal not yet confirmed on the Ukrainian or American side. Substantively, both the Independence Day and Zelensky statements describe the same goal as last week — reviving trilateral talks and assessing Ukraine's air-defence needs — with a parallel push for a Moscow-proposed "air ceasefire," a narrower step than a full ceasefire, still on the table per US and Ukrainian officials.

Context. A visit that has now been reported on three different timelines across three weeks (within two weeks; second half of August/Independence Day; next week) without ever actually happening is worth reading as a pattern in its own right: it suggests either genuine last-mile scheduling friction, or that "the envoys are about to visit Kyiv" functions as a recurring diplomatic signal independent of whether the trip is imminent.

Watch-Items. (1) Whether the TASS "next week" report is confirmed or denied by Kyiv or Washington. (2) Whether the visit, if it happens, precedes or follows a Moscow leg. (3) Any concrete air-ceasefire proposal emerging from the visit. (4) Whether the visit actually occurs by 24 August as the Independence Day framing implies.

Confidence: low-medium (Ukrainian-sourced timeline corroborated across multiple outlets; the more recent, earlier TASS timeline is single-sourced to Russian state media and unconfirmed by any other party)

Iran / Hormuz — the strait stays shut, but a deal that hands Tehran de facto control is close

3 items
Diplomacy5–8 Aug

Iran and Oman near a Hormuz deal that would give Tehran control of inbound traffic — Iran still won't fully reopen the strait

Iran and Oman are closing in on a temporary arrangement to manage the Strait of Hormuz that, per Reuters sourcing relayed by Fortune and the Wall Street Journal, would give Iran control over inbound shipping traffic — described by one source as a concession "already made." Iran is demanding a 5–7% fee on the value of cargo transiting the strait; Oman has floated roughly 3%. Iran's national security chief said on 8 August that traffic "won't return to normal" unless the US ends the war, withdraws forces and pays compensation, and Tehran has separately warned Gulf neighbours that any new US strike would trigger retaliation against regional energy infrastructure. A US official told the Washington Post any temporary routes must carry "no approvals or permissions and no tolls or charges" — a position Washington maintains even though it is not itself part of the Oman-Iran talks.

Fortune · emerging deal recognises Iran's control Al Jazeera · Hormuz deal "close," latest positions
Read moreexpand_more

Context & background. Last week's edition described the Hormuz talks only at the level of two competing proposals — a regional consortium or a transit-fee model — with Iran's Ghalibaf insisting the strait would "never" revert to its prewar status. This week's reporting confirms which model is actually winning: not a multilateral consortium, but direct Iranian control over inbound traffic, with Oman as intermediary rather than co-administrator.

The details. The unresolved details are consequential: whether Iran's authority extends to outbound traffic as well (Iranian officials have publicly indicated they want it to, and want the power to bar US or Israeli-linked vessels specifically); and the fee structure, where Iran's 5–7% ask versus Oman's roughly 3% counter-offer is not a small gap at Gulf trade volumes. Eurasia Group's Gregory Brew captured the likely US posture bluntly, predicting Washington would accept Iranian control "without explicitly acknowledging" it — a formulation that lets the US maintain its public "no tolls, no permissions" line while the practical reality on the water moves the other way. The arrangement being discussed is explicitly temporary, meant to unstick a longer-term ceasefire negotiation rather than resolve it; Iran has tied full normalisation to the naval blockade being lifted and its frozen assets being released, conditions the US has not agreed to. Gulf states are separately racing to build pipeline capacity that bypasses Hormuz entirely, which — as Fortune's reporting notes — would erode Iran's negotiating leverage over time even if this deal cements it in the near term.

Context. A deal that gives Iran de facto control while allowing Washington to avoid saying so out loud is a strategic climbdown dressed as a technical fix — consistent with reporting (cited last week and again this week) that US munitions stocks and appetite for renewed strikes are both running low, which strengthens Tehran's hand regardless of the diplomatic language used to describe the outcome.

Watch-Items. (1) Whether a fee figure between 3% and 7% is actually agreed. (2) Whether Iran's control extends to outbound as well as inbound traffic. (3) Any US statement that walks back the "no tolls" position in substance even if not in name. (4) Progress on Gulf-state pipeline capacity that would reduce Hormuz's strategic weight over the medium term.

Confidence: medium-high (Reuters/WSJ sourcing relayed by Fortune and Al Jazeera; exact fee percentage and scope of Iranian control remain unresolved)
Shipping4–10 Aug

The strait stays effectively closed to commercial shipping — a damaged tanker still smoking near Khasab, and another targeted on 10 August

Traffic through Hormuz remained severely disrupted through the window: satellite imagery of a tanker struck near Khasab, Oman on 4 August showed continued fire damage, with a 7 August follow-up showing the vessel stationary and disabled though no longer smoking. At least one oil tanker was targeted while attempting to transit the strait early on 10 August, per Lloyd's List Intelligence. Roughly ten product tankers made the transit in the preceding week, up from six the week before — still a fraction of pre-war volumes, and consistent with what independent trackers describe as an effectively closed waterway.

Sanctions & Trade — the Graham Act clears the Senate for good, and the House becomes the real test

1 item
Legislation7 Aug

Senate delivers final 86-12 passage of the Graham Act — same margin as the procedural vote, now it's the House's turn

The Senate passed the Lindsey O. Graham Sanctioning Russia and Iran Act of 2026 in its final vote on Friday, 7 August, by 86 votes to 11 — a slightly different no-count than the 86-12 procedural vote ten days earlier, with reporting split between outlets citing 11 and 12 dissenting senators. The bill now moves to the House, which returns from summer recess on 31 August, meaning September remains the earliest realistic date for the bill to reach Trump's desk. No new provisions were reported beyond what this briefing detailed last week: tariffs up to 100% on the top five buyers or facilitators of Russian oil and gas, sanctions on Putin's inner circle and Russian financial institutions, and an extension of Iran sanctions through 2031.

Read moreexpand_more

Context & background. Last week's edition covered the bill's 28 July procedural motion-to-proceed vote, also 86-12, and detailed its IEEPA-proof structural design at length. This week's development is narrower but important: the procedural vote has now converted into an actual final passage vote, meaning the bill is a real, though still incomplete, legislative product rather than a motion to debate one.

The details. The near-identical margin across both votes (86-12 procedural, 86-11/86-12 final, depending on outlet) suggests the coalition behind the bill was locked in well before the final vote and did not shift materially in the intervening ten days — a sign of durable bipartisan support rather than a fragile majority that might erode under lobbying pressure from affected buyer countries. The practical bottleneck now shifts entirely to the House, which was already out of session for the whole of this reporting window and does not return until 31 August; nothing in this week's reporting indicates House Speaker Johnson has scheduled floor time or resolved the tariff-authority-delegation objections flagged by Senate Democrats Welch and Warnock last week.

Context. A bill that has now cleared the Senate twice with the same lopsided margin, but still cannot become law before the House returns, illustrates a structural feature of this Congress rather than anything specific to Ukraine policy: momentum in one chamber does not translate to speed when the other chamber is on recess, regardless of how bipartisan the underlying support is.

Watch-Items. (1) Whether the House schedules a vote promptly after returning 31 August. (2) Any pre-emptive move by China or India, the bill's two largest implied targets, ahead of enactment. (3) Whether the discrepancy between reported "11" and "12" no-votes is resolved by an official roll call. (4) Trump's explicit public position, which remains inferred rather than stated.

Confidence: high (multiple outlets confirm final Senate passage; exact dissenting-vote count varies by source and is flagged above)

Hybrid warfare / Europe — a drone explodes in Bulgaria, and this time the trail points to Kyiv, not Moscow

1 item
Reversal8 Aug

A drone explodes in Bulgaria near a gas pipeline — but it looks Ukrainian, not Russian, breaking the pattern this briefing has tracked all summer

An unidentified drone crossed into Bulgarian airspace from the north at 8:10am local time on 8 August and exploded roughly 100 metres inside Bulgarian territory, in a sunflower field near the former Kardam border crossing with Romania — about 1,000 metres from a compressor station on the Trans-Balkan gas pipeline. No one was injured and no infrastructure was damaged. Bulgaria's defence ministry said initial analysis points to a "Maya" decoy drone, a type widely used by Ukraine's armed forces, carrying a substantial explosive payload. Ukraine's foreign ministry said its forces did not intentionally direct any asset toward Bulgaria and promised to explain the circumstances.

Washington Post · drone likely from Ukraine explodes in Bulgaria Al Jazeera · Bulgaria, Germany drone incidents raise security fears
Read moreexpand_more

Context & background. Every hybrid-warfare incident this briefing has logged since July — Romania's three drone incursions in three days, the Kh-101 cruise missile that crashed in Poland — has been attributed to Russia, part of what analysts tracking the pattern describe as an accelerating, deliberate campaign to test NATO's airspace. This incident breaks that pattern: the preliminary evidence points the other way, to a Ukrainian munition landing on NATO territory by accident rather than a Russian one landing there by design.

The details. The technical identification matters here: a "Maya" decoy drone is a low-cost Ukrainian system typically used to saturate and confuse Russian air defences rather than to strike targets directly, which is consistent with this being a stray or malfunctioning asset rather than an intentional strike on Bulgarian territory. The location — a sunflower field 1,000 metres from a gas-pipeline compressor station, not the station itself — and the absence of any casualties or damage both support the "stray" reading over a deliberate one. Al Jazeera's framing groups this incident with a separate drone sighting in Germany the same day, suggesting European security services are treating 8 August as a cluster of airspace concerns rather than two unrelated events, even though the Bulgarian incident's likely Ukrainian origin makes it a different category of concern than a Russian one.

Context. If the Ukrainian-origin finding holds, it complicates the clean narrative this briefing has carried for weeks — that NATO airspace violations are a one-directional Russian pressure campaign. A stray Ukrainian decoy landing in a NATO member state is a genuine accident risk in its own right, distinct from Moscow's "boiling frog" strategy, and worth tracking as its own category rather than folding into the Russian-incursion count.

Watch-Items. (1) Whether Ukraine's promised explanation confirms the Maya-drone identification. (2) Whether Bulgaria or NATO responds differently to a Ukrainian-origin incident than to the Russian-attributed ones. (3) Details on the separate Germany drone sighting reported the same day. (4) Whether this incident affects Bulgaria's posture on continued transit or support for Ukraine's war effort.

Confidence: medium-high (Bulgarian defence ministry and Ukrainian foreign ministry both on record; final forensic attribution not yet complete)
