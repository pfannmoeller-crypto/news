TESLAProduct line6–8 Nov 2026

Tesla will stop making the Cybertruck around mid-2027 to free factory space for the Cybercab

On the evening of 6 November Elon Musk wrote on X that Tesla will wind down Cybertruck production at its Texas factory around the middle of next year "to make room for increased Cybercab production". The Cybercab is Tesla's two-seat driverless taxi, which has no steering wheel or pedals.

A day later he added that a more efficient Cybercab battery is coming and that the car will also be offered in "liquid silver".

Context & background.The Cybertruck, Tesla's angular steel pickup, was unveiled in 2019 and reached customers four years later. It has been the best-selling electric pickup in the US but sold far below what Tesla had planned, mostly because the price ended up higher than first promised. Musk's argument now is about people and floor space: "there are a very limited number of smart, dedicated people", and they cannot be spread across a complex Cybertruck production line and the effort to scale the Cybercab. Tesla has told investors it wants Cybercabs in customers' hands by the end of 2026.

The details.The Cybercab's battery capacity is to shrink from 48 to 40 kilowatt-hours (a kilowatt-hour is the energy a 1,000-watt device uses in an hour), which means fewer battery cells per car and so a lower cost. Teslarati reports that paid Cybercab rides began in Austin on 3 September with 45 cars on Texas's list of registered vehicles; by late October the state listed 573 Cybercabs next to 631 of the Model Y robotaxis. The previous edition cited 463 Cybercab registrations from a 26 October report, so the two figures come from different dates and sources and are not a clean comparison. On 7 November a new version of the Robotaxi app began showing the waiting time and fare for a Cybercab and a Model Y side by side, but so far only in Austin. Tesla said on its quarterly earnings call that Cybercab rides would start in Dallas in November; no start has been reported. All figures here come from one Tesla-focused news site relaying Musk's posts and state records; Tesla has published no formal statement and no production numbers.

Why it still matters.It is the clearest sign yet that Tesla treats the robotaxi, not the pickup, as its factory's future: a model with a loyal following is being retired to protect a vehicle that is still being built in small numbers. If Cybercab volume does not grow quickly, Tesla will have given up a product line with real sales for one without them. The Cybertruck decision is also one more narrowing of a car range that now leans heavily on the Model Y.

Watch items.whether Tesla confirms the date in a filing or at its 3 December annual meeting; the first Cybercab rides in Dallas; any official Cybercab production figure.

SPACEXLaunch countdownTarget Fri 13 Nov 2026

Starship's Flight 15 is now aimed at 13 November, with an attempt to catch the upper stage as well as the booster

A SpaceX launch executive, Kiko Dontchev, gave Friday 13 November as the target on stage at a conference on 5 November, according to the journalist Eric Berger of Ars Technica. On 6 November Musk confirmed on X that SpaceX will try to catch both the Super Heavy booster (the rocket's first stage) and the Starship upper stage (the ship that carries the payload) with the launch tower's mechanical arms. No ship has ever been caught; if both work it would be the first time every part of an orbital rocket comes back intact.

This replaces the earlier tentative 8 November date mentioned in the previous edition. The US aviation regulator's Flight 15 licence had not been listed as of 5 November.

Context & background.Starship is the fully reusable rocket SpaceX is building for its satellites, the Moon and Mars. The booster has been caught by the tower three times (on Flights 5, 7 and 8) and some have been reflown. The upper stage is harder: it returns from orbital speed, so it has to survive far greater heat, flip upright and fire its engines to settle between the arms. The previous Starship flight, Flight 14 on 28 September, was the first to reach orbit and released 26 Starlink satellites of the third generation, despite an engine shutting down early that Musk blamed on software.

The details.Flight 15 uses Booster 22 and Ship 42. Booster 22 fired all 33 of its engines in a full-length test on 25 October; Ship 42 ran one engine on 26 October and all six for 60 seconds the next day. Per Teslarati, the ship is to reach an orbit about 275 kilometres up, release 26 Starlink satellites, circle the Earth about 14 times over more than 22 hours, and only then decide whether to come back for the catch. Backup splashdown zones exist in the Gulf of Mexico (booster) and the Pacific (ship). Musk put the chance of a successful ship catch at "more likely than not" on the October earnings call. SpaceX's president, Gwynne Shotwell, said in October she expects the licence to cover a return to the launch site for both stages; a ship coming back over land is new, because earlier flights ended over open ocean. Sources differ on small details: earlier public schedules showed 8 and 12 November, and SpaceX has not yet published a mission page.

Why it still matters.A caught ship is what would let SpaceX reuse the whole vehicle quickly, which its plan of launching thousands of satellites and orbital data centres depends on (see the next card). A failure would not end the programme, since Musk says further flights are scheduled, but the date is a regulator-dependent target, not a commitment.

Watch items.the regulator's licence; any slip in the date; whether the ship survives re-entry; Flight 16, the flight after this one, which Musk describes as the step where a caught ship would be flown again.

SPACEXComputing business6–8 Nov 2026

Musk says SpaceX is already earning back its data-centre costs fast, while Anthropic's contract can be cancelled on 90 days' notice

Speaking at an investment conference on 6 November, Musk said SpaceX is "getting 50 billion a year for 35 billion dollar investments" in computing capacity, can bring a data centre online in eight or nine months against two and a half years for rivals, and is heading for revenue at an annual rate above $100 billion in December.

On 8 November a venture investor argued on a podcast that the contract with the AI company Anthropic lets SpaceX raise its prices to two to three times the current level. Musk replied that it cuts both ways: "If compute becomes plentiful and cheap, SpaceX revenue will drop."

Context & background.SpaceX absorbed the AI company xAI in February and now rents the computing power of its Memphis data centres, called Colossus, to outside AI laboratories before using it for its own Grok chatbot. Reuters reported on 5 November that this renting helped SpaceX post its first profit as a public company. The previous edition covered the quarter's results and the loans taken to buy chips; this card concerns only what was said since.

The details.The Anthropic terms (about $45 billion in total, cancellable by either side on 90 days' notice) come from SpaceX's stock-market listing papers in May, as reported by Teslarati; similar exit terms reportedly apply to deals with Google and Reflection AI. The sums Musk quoted, the $100 billion December rate and his "best guess" of $500 billion by December 2027 are his own forecasts, not audited results, and were not checked against a filing. Reuters says SpaceX aims to lift capacity from 1.7 gigawatts at the end of September to as much as 3 gigawatts by year-end and 13 gigawatts next year; the research firm SemiAnalysis counts more than 9 gigawatts planned or in development. Deutsche Bank analysts warned last week that Texas capacity could face delays. Shares rose more than 2 percent in overnight trading on Sunday, according to Teslarati citing Stocktwits.

Why it still matters.Much of the case for SpaceX's value rests on the claim that computing capacity is scarce and that its customers cannot leave. A 90-day exit clause shows the other side of that: the contract protects neither party if capacity gets cheap or the price of electricity and permits moves against the builder (see the Politics card).

Watch items.the next SpaceX filing on how much of its revenue is contracted; any change in terms with Anthropic; Texas data-centre permits.

Musk's companies have lost about $420 billion in value since the election, and Texas's new government threatens his data centres

The previous edition reported that Democrats won Congress and the two big Texas races Musk backed. The follow-up: Reuters reports that SpaceX shares fell 5.5 percent and Tesla 5.1 percent on the day after, and CNBC, as relayed by a Malaysian news agency, says the two companies together have lost more than $420 billion since 3 November.

Reuters adds that Texas's incoming governor, Gina Hinojosa, wants a halt on new data centres, and that the next chair of the House Oversight Committee, Robert Garcia, told Republicans and their allies to "lawyer up".

Context & background.Musk was the largest known donor of the election cycle, giving more than $445 million according to a Federal Election Commission count cited by the Cable News Network (CNN), or about $446 million by The Verge's count, including $47 million from his America political action committee against Democrat James Talarico, who won the Senate race with 55 percent. Talarico's campaign used the slogan "Texas is not for sale". Reuters says Musk deleted his election-day post urging followers to vote Republican.

The details.Hinojosa had sued to obtain emails between Musk and her predecessor, Greg Abbott, and has opposed a possible sale of a beachfront state park near SpaceX's Starbase site. Democratic lawmakers have already questioned Musk over Starlink limits in Ukraine (see the previous edition), his government-efficiency work and federal contracts. The $420 billion figure and the report that Musk's wealth fell below $800 billion from $1 trillion in June come only from the Bernama item, itself a relay of a Russian state news agency quoting CNBC; CNBC's own report was not opened, and market moves also reflect a wider fall in technology shares. Musk's public reaction was a joke: "Data centers in space looking pretty appealing right now."

Why it still matters.Texas hosts Tesla's main factory, SpaceX's launch site and the planned chipmaking venture between Tesla and SpaceX, so state-level hostility reaches the businesses more directly than Washington does. The bigger exposure is the computing business: slower permits would delay the capacity SpaceX has promised customers.

Watch items.House Oversight requests for documents; a Texas data-centre moratorium proposal; Starbase park sale.

edit_noteEditor's view

The next fortnight is decided by one rocket test and one state

1. 13 November is a test of SpaceX's reuse claim. A successful ship catch would support the cost assumptions behind the plan to put computing capacity in orbit; a miss would leave them as promises. Treat the date as uncertain until the licence is public.

2. Tesla is concentrating on the Cybercab. Giving up a product with sales for one in small production is a bet, and the only hard numbers so far are state registration counts.

3. Texas is now a business risk, not just a base. The permits for data centres and a park sale sit with a government Musk spent against; the contract with Anthropic shows how quickly the arithmetic can change.

4. The sourcing is thin. Two of four cards rest on one Tesla-focused site quoting Musk's posts, and the $420 billion loss figure travels through a relay. Nothing here comes from a company filing, which is the first thing to look for next week.

A short week: four stories, the rest quiet.
