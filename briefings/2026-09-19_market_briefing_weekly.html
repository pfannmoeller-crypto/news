Fed hikes rates for the first time since 2023 as a crypto bill collapses in the Senate

The Federal Reserve, the US central bank, raised its benchmark interest rate on Wednesday for the first time in three years, and its own forecasters expect at least one more increase before the year is out. Two days earlier, a Senate procedural vote on the CLARITY Act — a bill meant to set clear federal rules for crypto markets — fell well short of the votes needed to advance, and one analyst called the Senate's work on legislation setting the rules for crypto trading effectively dead for the year. Bitcoin dropped sharply on the news, then clawed back most of the loss by Friday. Stocks wobbled on the rate increase but rallied hard on Thursday as a separate worry — a broader debate over whether artificial-intelligence development is moving too fast, which had hit chip-making stocks — eased. Oil drifted back toward $100 a barrel as fears about supply disruption in the Gulf region cooled, and gold held near its highs even after the rate move that would normally work against it (higher interest rates usually pull money out of gold, which pays no interest).

Wall Street shrugs off a Fed rate increase, rallying hard on Thursday before a mixed Friday close

US stocks absorbed the Federal Reserve's Wednesday rate increase and moved on: the S&P 500 and Nasdaq Composite rallied roughly 1.1% and 1.7% respectively on Thursday as investors decided the increase, well telegraphed in advance, was no longer news. Chip-making stocks led the charge back, with AMD and Intel jumping 6.4% and 7.7% on Thursday alone as an earlier sell-off tied to a debate over the pace of artificial-intelligence development eased (that debate itself belongs to this edition's technology coverage; the price impact on chip stocks is the equities-side read). Friday's close was more mixed, with the Dow Jones lagging the broader market for the week.

Context & background: The prior edition closed with equities having already fallen for three straight sessions as an above-forecast inflation reading and a widening Gulf oil-supply shock pushed the odds of a rate increase above 90%. That increase was fully expected by the time it arrived on Wednesday, which is a large part of why the market's reaction skipped past it so quickly.

The details: Thursday's rally was broad but led by growth and technology names; the sector tracking those stocks advanced 2.2% on the day, according to one recap, while chip-making names AMD and Intel posted some of their sharpest single-day gains of the year. Two forces were at work simultaneously: falling oil prices reduced worries about further inflation pressure, and the 10-year Treasury yield (the interest the US government pays on its 10-year bonds; when it falls, bonds compete less with stocks) eased back a few hundredths of a percentage point from its highest level since 2007, making stocks look relatively more attractive again. Friday's session was calmer and more mixed — the S&P 500 and Nasdaq Composite each edged higher while the Dow Jones slipped, leaving the Dow down 1.7% for the week even as the Nasdaq finished up 0.7%.

Why it still matters: A market that shrugs off its first rate increase in three years within a day is telling a specific story: the move had been so thoroughly expected that it carried little new information. The bigger open question, taken up in the macro section below, is whether the Federal Reserve's own forecast of a second increase before year-end gets the same muted reception when the October decision arrives.

Federal ReserveWed 16 Sep

The Federal Reserve raises interest rates for the first time since 2023, and signals it isn't finished

On Wednesday, the Federal Reserve's policymakers voted unanimously — 12 to 0 — to raise the central bank's benchmark interest rate by 0.25 percentage points, to a range of 3.75% to 4.00%. It is the first increase since July 2023, reversing nearly three years in which the central bank had been holding rates steady or cutting them. Fed Chair Kevin Warsh said "the plain fact is that inflation is too high and has been for too long," and the central bank's own economic forecasts point to at least one more increase before the year is out.

Context & background: The prior edition closed with the market already expecting this move — a widely watched interest-rate futures tracker (a tool that reads bets on future interest rates) had already put the probability of a September increase above 90%, and a Reuters poll of economists put 85% of them in the same camp, both driven by an August inflation reading that came in hotter than forecast and a widening Gulf oil shock. Wednesday's decision matched that expectation almost exactly.

The details: Chair Warsh pointed to three separate factors behind rising Treasury yields (the interest the US government pays on its bonds) going into the decision: underlying economic strength, increased competition for capital from major technology firms building out artificial-intelligence infrastructure, and geopolitical tensions (a reference to the Gulf oil situation covered in the energy section below). He also noted the labor market remains healthy, with unemployment around 4.1%. The central bank's own quarterly economic forecasts show policymakers expecting one further 0.25 percentage point increase before the end of 2026, with the rate then holding roughly steady through 2027. As of this snapshot, Polymarket prices a 55.5% chance of another increase specifically at the 28 October meeting (odds board, above) — a meaningfully higher probability than a plain reading of "one more hike sometime this year" would suggest, since it implies traders think the Federal Reserve may not wait until December.

Why it still matters: This is the first sustained tightening move in three years, and it reverses the market's expectation at the start of 2026 that rates would keep falling. Whether the Federal Reserve manages to stop at one more increase or ends up moving faster than its own forecast suggests depends heavily on whether the energy-driven inflation pressure covered below keeps easing or flares up again.

Why it still matters: With comprehensive crypto market-structure legislation now effectively off the table in the Senate for 2026, the industry's next legislative opening shifts to next year, while in the meantime bitcoin's price is being driven more by the same rate-and-oil story moving every other asset class this week than by anything crypto-specific.

lightbulbEditor's view

The Federal Reserve finally moved — markets absorbed it in a day, crypto didn't

1. A well-telegraphed rate increase carries little punch. Wednesday's move ended nearly three years without a rate increase, yet stocks rallied hard the very next session. When investors have already spent weeks expecting a move, the day it actually happens becomes almost a formality.

2. The Federal Reserve says it isn't done, and the market believes it. The central bank's own forecast points to a second increase this year, and Polymarket already prices better-than-even odds (55.5%) of that happening specifically at the very next meeting in October — a more aggressive read than "sometime before December."

3. Crypto took a real hit that equities didn't. The same week that stocks shrugged off a rate increase, bitcoin fell harder than smaller tokens on the CLARITY Act's failure — a reminder that crypto's swings are still driven as much by Washington as by the same macro forces moving everything else.

4. Europe's underperformance is now a three-week pattern. The German index has lagged a recovering Wall Street for three straight editions running, hitting a two-month low this week on a mix of options-expiry volatility and lingering energy-price nerves.

5. Oil's retreat is doing quiet work on the inflation story. Both benchmarks eased for a third straight session as Gulf supply fears cooled — see this edition's Geopolitics coverage for the Hormuz and Saudi detail — which, if it holds, is exactly the kind of development that would let the Federal Reserve stop at one more increase rather than several.

6. Chip stocks' Thursday rebound is a technology story wearing a markets hat. AMD and Intel's sharp gains were the price echo of an easing debate over the pace of artificial-intelligence development — see this edition's Frontier Tech coverage for the substance of that argument.

Next edition should open by checking whether the Gulf oil retreat holds or reverses, whether Polymarket's 55.5% odds of an October increase keep climbing as that meeting approaches, and whether bitcoin's recovery from this week's crypto-bill shock continues once the Federal Reserve's next signals arrive.
