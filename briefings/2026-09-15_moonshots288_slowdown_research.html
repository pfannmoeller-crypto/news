The argument in brief

A panel, not an author, and the disagreement is the substance. Four theses stated as strongly as their proponents state them — including the ones that contradict each other.

  • Something changed inside the labs, and it is visible from outside. In five days: a pacing essay from OpenAI's chief scientist, an urgency post from its CEO, a resignation, and public agreement from the alignment lead at the lab the researcher left. The signals come from inside the buildings — an immune response, Diamandis argues, not sleepwalking off a cliff.
  • A one-in-ten chance of extinction is not an acceptable business decision. Blundin needs no estimate of his own: insiders publish figures between 0.1% and 20%, and the decision is irreversible and made on everyone's behalf — so track large GPU clusters the way fissile material is tracked.
  • The absence of evidence in the sky is evidence about the risk. Wissner-Gross's counter is not that alignment is easy, but that a universe where superintelligence reliably ends its creators should have left visible artefacts over billions of years. We see none — a prior, he says, not a guarantee.
  • The people asking to slow down built the accelerator. A lab that raised billions and named AGI as its mission cannot claim surprise when AGI approaches. Sincere or not, no mechanism exists to act on the alarm — and the weeks that produced the pacing essays produced two frontier launches.

The argument, walked through

In the order built. Verdict scale: 5/5 primary source · 4/5 two independent sources · 3/5 single credible source · 2/5 aggregator only.

The resignation, and whether to take it seriously 9–11 Sep 2026

The episode opens on a screenshot of a single post, read out directly.

"neither company is acting responsibly in the race towards self-improving superintelligence" — Jacob Coxon, read on air
CheckTrue C 4/5 — The researcher is Jacob Coxon, 27, who resigned from Anthropic around 9 Sep 2026 after pretraining work at both labs. Quote confirmed near-verbatim, as is the second charge: the labs are "racing straight to self-improving superintelligence and gambling with our lives". He added that OpenAI staff "have not deeply internalized the civilizational stakes", while Anthropic understands them but is locked into the race.

Wissner-Gross reaches for the base rate — Coxon had been at Anthropic under three months — and files it under a known genre.

"So I heavily discount any particular blaze of glory rage quit couched as virtue signaling." — Wissner-Gross

Then, in the same breath and before anyone challenges him, he qualifies himself: "That said, 100 plus million views on this, the whole story smells wrong." He goes on to ask seriously whether it is a foreign influence operation. The hedge is volunteered, not extracted, and is the more interesting half of his contribution. Mostaque takes the opposite view: "this is like the Tom Hanks moment of COVID".

What makes the resignation hard to dismiss is the reply from inside the same company — though on this same call, it did not go unanswered.

"I personally think it is greater than 10% within the next decade." — Evan Hubinger, read on air
CheckTrue C 5/5 — Confirmed almost verbatim in Hubinger's X reply to Coxon, 9 Sep 2026: "I personally think it is >10% within the next decade… we do not yet have a plan to solve alignment for superintelligence and are not clearly on track to." Both halves check out. The qualifier matters and the panel keeps it: this is his personal estimate as alignment science lead, not Anthropic's official position.
Other viewsThe reply is the strongest corroboration available, from inside the company Coxon left. But Wissner-Gross rejects it immediately, on air, in two moves — first the incentive, then the number itself: "It's a self-licking ice cream cone, hardly surprising that an alignment lead thinks alignment is valuable. So this 10% P-Dume, again, I discount." That rebuttal does not settle the question — the same "self-licking" charge could be levelled at any domain expert's own risk estimate — but it means the reply was contested on the call, not merely by omission afterward. Nor is the reply isolated: Dario Amodei's "We Must Pace the Frontier" (12 Sep 2026) and a "Pacing the Frontier" letter past 1,000 signatures. Against: Alex Karp argues unilateral Western restraint cedes ground to actors who will not reciprocate; Axios notes the executives signing pacing letters mostly could not unilaterally stop their own companies, so the letters cost them nothing. And the plainest fact: the same labs shipped frontier releases (Mythos 5.1, Astra) in the weeks they called for pacing.

The load-bearing item is c2, not c1. A resignation is one person's judgement; public agreement from the lab's own alignment lead is a datum about the institution — and that datum was itself disputed on air, not left standing unchallenged.

Four P(doom) numbers on one panel 11 Sep 2026

Within twenty minutes the call produces four incompatible estimates and no one moves. Ismail is at the floor:

"My personal P-Doom is about 0.1%, in my opinion." — Ismail (attribution contextual)

Mostaque revises downward mid-conversation — "I had a PDM of 50%. It's down to 20%" (ASR; he means P(doom)) — while insisting that lab staff who say 10% privately believe it is higher. Wissner-Gross discounts the 10% on the grounds below. Blundin refuses the framing entirely — but in the same unbroken turn, seconds earlier, he had dismissed Coxon's motives too, and unlike Wissner-Gross, without a hedge: "So they actually self-destructed themselves. And now they're very, very dangerous people."

"To me, a 1% 0.1% or 20% P-Dume is totally unacceptable." — Blundin (ASR: P-doom)

His proposal follows: treat the hardware as controlled material. "But now you have something that's just as dangerous as a pound of plutonium" — so you must know exactly where it is and what it is doing.

Other viewsWhere these numbers sit. The AI Impacts 2023 survey of 2,778 published AI researchers: median near 5%, mean 16.2%. Metaculus near 5% for extinction by 2100, AI contributing ~3 points. Safety-focused communities cluster at a median 20–30%. Extremes: LeCun near zero, Yudkowsky and Yampolskiy near-certain. So Ismail's 0.1% sits at the extreme low end with LeCun, while Mostaque's 20% — presented on air as striking — is near the safety-specialist median, not an outlier. On compute governance a version already exists: the EU AI Act's 1025 FLOP threshold, US EO 14110's 1026. The standing critique of the plutonium analogy: fissile material is scarce, single-purpose and excludable; compute is dual-use and widely distributed, with distributed training on consumer hardware the concrete evasion route.

No claim here needs checking; the numbers are the panellists' own. What is checkable is where they sit — and the pod's "extreme" figure turns out to be the mainstream one.

The Fermi argument against high P(doom) 11 Sep 2026

His reason for discounting the 10% is cosmological, not technical: in a universe where superintelligence reliably ends civilisations, Earth would long since have been reached by von Neumann probes and the Milky Way would show signs of having been consumed. It does not. Diamandis pushes back — "I'm not sure I buy that as a good enough safety net" — and the answer is an immediate downgrade:

"It's not a safety net. It's an inductive prior. It's not a strategy for safety." — Wissner-Gross
Other viewsThree objections. Sandberg, Drexler and Ord, "Dissolving the Fermi Paradox" (2018): model uncertainty in the Drake parameters properly rather than plugging in point estimates and "we are alone, or very rare" becomes highly probable — severing the inference, since an empty sky is then expected whatever AI does. Hanson's Great Filter cuts the other way: if the filter lies ahead rather than behind, silence is evidence for danger. And the argument assumes a misaligned superintelligence expands outward; one optimising a local objective need not, and leaves no signature — against a detection window of ~70–100 years set beside billions of years of galactic history.

He concedes the point under pressure and restates the claim at its defensible strength. As an inductive prior it is legitimate; as a reason to discount a specific engineering risk estimate it does very little work.

Pachocki's essay, and what the labs actually asked for 6 Sep 2026

The panel treats the essay as context rather than text, summarising it as OpenAI's chief scientist "asking for a voluntary slowdown". Wissner-Gross does not contest the substance — "I do take Sam at his word" — but argues the more interesting fact is the normalcy overhang: the gap between how deep into the singularity the mathematics has gone and how ordinary the street still looks.

CheckMostly true C 4/5 — The essay is "An Alien Mind", published by OpenAI on Sunday 6 Sep 2026, not Saturday. More substantively, it goes further than the panel's summary: Pachocki wants voluntary pacing to become commonplace and wants Preparedness-Framework / RSP-style commitments to become legally binding, enforced by third-party auditors or governments.

The bluntest line on the call rejects the premise that the labs may claim surprise. You raise billions, recruit the best people alive, name AGI as the mission — "the whole thing is in the pitch deck" — then raise your hand in alarm when it arrives. The conclusion drawn is not caution:

"I don't think we should slow down. I don't think we can." — Diamandis (attribution contextual; the transcript has no speaker labels)

The reasoning: no mechanism for regulating this exists, and the honest position is comfort with uncertainty rather than performed alarm. The same speaker does offer a constructive version — incentive structures that reward slowing down — before dismissing the alarm as theatre.

The panel's summary understates Pachocki — the rarer direction of error: a group arguing lab alarm is overblown made the lab's ask sound softer than it was.

Stepping back

The error direction in this run. Eight checks: three true, two mostly true, two misleading, one wrong figure. The pattern is worth stating plainly — every headline quote checks out. Coxon, Hubinger, Altman, Brown: the panel reads posts off the screen and reads them accurately. All four failures sit in the second tier, where someone is recalling rather than reading — a study credited to a person who does not exist (c6), a P(doom) figure attached to the wrong statement and event (c7), an overstated "first ever" (c8), and a summary making Pachocki sound less demanding than he was (c4). That last runs against the panel's own interest, which argues against simple thesis-favourable bias here. One episode is not a base rate.

The messenger

A weekly news panel reading from live feeds, not investigative reporting — which explains the error distribution: quotes on screen are accurate, recalled attributions are not. Diamandis promotes his own ventures in-show, and the episode opens with a co-host's exit announcement (Vestmark's acquisition by Envestnet) before the safety segment. Mostaque runs an AI company and revises his own P(doom) live on air. The compiler disclosure belongs here rather than in a footer: this piece was written with an Anthropic model, and Anthropic is the employer Coxon left, the employer of the alignment lead in c2, and the subject of c8. The format rewards speed, and speed is where these errors live.

What survives

  • The resignation and the reply. Both quoted accurately, and the reply from Anthropic's own alignment lead is the stronger — read c2 before deciding what to make of c1.
  • The cost-collapse numbers. c5 is the cleanest check here: two verbatim-confirmed comparisons, and the derived 25,000× figure follows from them.
