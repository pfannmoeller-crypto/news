An AI sell-off erases the election rally; strong jobs data keeps rate rises alive

This edition follows the 5 November one by four days, so it covers only what is new. Thursday brought the one event that mattered: a sell-off led by AI-related shares that undid the whole post-election rise in a single session. Friday was calmer. The US jobs report, due that morning, came in far above forecasts, and the Bank of England, whose decision was still pending last time, raised its rate. Oil gave back part of its fall. There was no Federal Reserve meeting, so the central-bank story is data, not decisions. Gold and the German share index are left out because we found no reliable closing figure for the week.

US equities · AI sharesThu 5 – Fri 6 Nov 2026

The S&P 500 falls 4.4% in a day as AI-related shares collapse, then recovers a sliver on Friday

On Thursday 5 November the S&P 500 dropped 4.4%, its worst day since April 2025 and enough to erase all of Wednesday's election-day gain, which had been its best in three months. The Nasdaq Composite fell 5.5% and ended 11.9% below its record from last month, which counts as a correction (a fall of more than 10% from a recent high). The Associated Press tied the losses to shares of companies building AI data centres: Nvidia lost 9.3% and Advanced Micro Devices 17.3%. Every sector of the S&P 500 ended lower according to one aggregator, and the Associated Press reported that morning gains in most shares disappeared during the day.

Friday was mixed: AI shares bounced and the Nasdaq gained 1.1%, but the Dow slipped 0.5%. For the week the S&P 500 finished 2.7% lower and the Nasdaq 3.4% lower.

Context & background: Last week's edition ended on a relief rally: oil had fallen and the Dow had its best day in 19 months. Thursday reversed it. The Associated Press gave three explanations, none of them confirmed by a company or regulator. First, AI share prices had risen so far that critics call them a bubble. Second, building more data centres has become expensive because borrowing costs rose. Third, Tuesday's Democratic victories raised the possibility that governments could force pauses in data-centre construction, because voters of all parties are wary of AI and of the higher power bills that data centres cause. Any slowdown would mean fewer chip purchases, and AI spending is driving much of US economic growth.

The details: Because the largest AI chip companies are now among the largest companies, their moves carry the index: the Associated Press notes that a 1% move in Nvidia affects the S&P 500 more than a 1% move in almost any other stock. Nvidia is now down 2.6% for the year after gains of roughly 39%, 171% and 239% in the last three years. CoreWeave, which rents out access to AI chips, fell 9.8%, and Vernova (a power-equipment maker spun out of General Electric), which helps power data centres, 7.5%. Among the few winners, AppLovin (a marketing-technology firm) rose 31% on better results and a share-repurchase programme, and Tapestry (owner of the Coach brand) rose 7.1%. Abroad, South Korea's Kospi index fell 5.3% and Japan's Nikkei 225 fell 1.8%. Some sources add other causes, such as Alphabet's capital-spending outlook of $175–185 billion and a weak forecast from the chip maker Qualcomm, but those come from blogs and aggregators and we did not verify them. The size of AMD's fall also differs by source, from about 8% to 17.3%; we use the Associated Press figure.

Why it still matters: The S&P 500 is now about 5.7% below its record, and TheStreet Pro calls this the worst week since April 2025. Two things decide whether Thursday was a one-off. The first is Tuesday's US consumer price index (a monthly measure of inflation): the forecast is a rise of 0.3% in the headline figure and 0.2% excluding food and energy, after 0.6% and 0.3% in September. The second is Nvidia's results, due later this month (the exact date is not confirmed in our sources). Companies are also allowed to buy back their own shares again, which can support prices: Citadel Securities estimates that about 85% of the index by weight is eligible this week. One caution from the same TheStreet Pro piece: the weekly investor-sentiment surveys closed before Thursday and do not yet show the damage.

Federal Reserve · US jobsFri 6 Nov 2026

US employers add 165,000 jobs against 62,000 expected, and a December rate rise looks even more likely

The US labour department's October report, published Friday 6 November, came in well above forecasts. Employers added 165,000 jobs where economists expected 62,000, and earlier months were revised up by a combined 84,000. The unemployment rate fell to 4.1% from 4.2%, and average hourly pay rose 0.4% in the month. Bloomberg reported that rate-futures pricing (contracts that bet on where the Federal Reserve's rate will be) for a rise at the 8–9 December meeting went from 67% to 74% after the release. Shares barely reacted: the Associated Press said yields on US government bonds (Treasuries) held firmer.

Context & background: Job growth had been weak for most of the past year, averaging 48,000 a month over the previous twelve months. August was revised up from 133,000 to 186,000 and September from 29,000 to 60,000, which lifts the three-month average to 137,000, against a break-even pace (the growth needed to absorb new workers) of about 50,000 according to a Bloomberg estimate. The Federal Reserve has held at 3.75%–4.00% while inflation runs well above its 2% target, so a labour market that is not weakening removes the main argument for waiting.

The details: The gain was led by professional and business services (+70,000, the most since July 2022), health care (+41,600) and transport and warehousing (+24,800); factories added 19,000 for a fifth straight monthly gain. Leisure and hospitality lost 13,000, and federal employment fell to 2.677 million, the lowest since May 1966. A separate survey of households showed 463,000 more people employed and the share of adults working or looking for work rising to 61.9%. The soft spots: the number of people working part-time only because they cannot find full-time work rose by 339,000, and the typical time out of work lengthened to 11.9 weeks, the longest since November 2021, the pattern of few layoffs but slow rehiring. Pay rose 3.1% over the year, but after inflation (September's consumer price index was 3.7%) total weekly pay of private workers grew only about 0.7%. Outside views split: Fitch's Olu Sonola called it "strength on strength", while Natixis's Christopher Hodge said the data are noisy and show little inflation pressure from labour.

Why it still matters: The next hard data are Tuesday 10 November's consumer price index, the last before the December meeting, and around 18 November the minutes (the written record) of the 28 October meeting. TheStreet Pro argues that only a clearly lower inflation reading could stop a December rise. The caveat is that the jobs figure is a single month from a survey that is revised often, and that the 74% rate-futures figure is Bloomberg's via TheStreet Pro; we could not check it directly. Official tables were not opened: the headline figures come from TheStreet Pro's summary of the release.

Bank of EnglandThu 5 Nov 2026

The Bank of England raises its rate to 4%, its first rise in three years, as energy prices push inflation toward 5%

The Bank of England's rate-setting committee voted 8–1 on Thursday 5 November to raise Bank Rate by 0.25 percentage points, from 3.75% to 4%. One member wanted a larger rise of 0.5 percentage points. The Bank said the war in the Middle East is in its ninth month and energy prices keep climbing, and it now expects UK consumer-price inflation, 3.5% in September, to reach around 5% by the middle of next year before falling back. Governor Andrew Bailey said that "we've seen encouraging news in the past 48 hours", but that ending the conflict is the best thing that could happen.

Context & background: Last week's edition listed this decision as pending. It follows the pattern of the central banks that moved earlier: the Bank of Canada raised its rate on 28 October, the European Central Bank held at 2.50% on 29 October while euro-area inflation reached 4%, and the Federal Reserve is leaning toward a December rise. In all of them the cause is the same: energy prices lifted by the Iran war.

The details: The Bank's own summary says there is "limited evidence" so far of energy costs spreading into other prices and wages, but that "the degree of inflationary pressure in the pipeline is now sufficient to warrant an increase". It added that activity data have been stronger than expected, which supports "modestly tighter" policy, and that policy "is not on a pre-set path". One aggregator reported the vote as 9–0; the Bank's own report says 8–1, and we use the Bank's. The Associated Press added that London's main share index slipped 0.4% on the day. The Bank also published its quarterly projections; we read the summary and overview, not the full forecast tables.

Why it still matters: The Bank tied its next steps to the Iran war: if oil falls on a deal, the case for further rises weakens, and if it does not, a rate near 4% may not be the end. The date of the next meeting is not in the sources we read. A smaller point for UK households: the Bank expects direct energy effects to show up in utility bills, so inflation is projected to rise even after this increase.

Brent crude rebounds 3.9% to $98.29 after new tanker attacks, but is still far below last week's level

Brent crude, the standard international oil price, rose $3.67 to $98.29 per barrel on Thursday 5 November, winning back part of Wednesday's drop of nearly 9%. The US benchmark, West Texas Intermediate, closed at $90.52, up $2.40. The Associated Press said uncertainty about the Iran war, and about when the global energy industry can return to normal, still reigns. We did not find a settlement price for Friday 6 November. Reports on that day describe a sharp fall on the week, but they give differing sizes, roughly 12% to 15%, so we show none.

Context & background: Oil has been the main reason inflation stayed high this autumn. Last edition ended with Brent at $94.62 after a one-day fall on hopes that the political shift in Washington would hasten the end of the Iran war. That hope has now met facts on the water. The Strait of Hormuz, the narrow passage for much of the world's oil, is covered in our Geopolitics briefing.

The details: The United Kingdom Maritime Trade Operations (a British shipping-safety body) reported incidents involving five or more vessels in the Gulf since 1 November, close to the pace of October, when 35 vessels were hit, the highest monthly total of the war. Iran's Revolutionary Guard said two tankers and a tugboat caught fire after using routes it calls unauthorised. Saudi Arabia issued alerts for Yanbu, its Red Sea export hub, while Yemen's Houthis (an Iran-backed armed group) continued attacks on the kingdom; one claimed strike on an airport at Dammam had not been confirmed. Separately, Ukrainian drone attacks hit Russian refineries, including Ryazan (reported by The Nation, citing Reuters, as an apparent hit not yet confirmed). Eurasia Group, a risk consultancy, cut its probability of renewed US strikes on Iran from 65% to 60%, doubled the chance of a long standoff from 15% to 30%, and halved the chance of a deal from 20% to 10%, saying an escalation could take Brent to $110–$125. Citi, the bank, warned that with the election over the White House could turn its attention to foreign policy and intensify the conflict.

Why it still matters: The $9 swing in two days shows how much of the price is hope. If talks fail, the escalation range quoted by Eurasia is $12–$27 above Thursday's level; if a deal appears, the price has room to fall. Our sources do not give Friday's close, so the weekly change stays unconfirmed. Everything here is a reading of diplomacy, not of changes in physical supply.

lightbulbEditor's view

The election did not break the market; the slump in AI shares did, and it has not been repaired

1. One day undid a week of relief. The Associated Press offers three reasons for Thursday, one of them political. All three are readings, not announcements, and the fact that Nvidia and the rest of the group fell hardest suggests the cause was AI valuations, with the election the trigger rather than the reason.

2. Good news on jobs was bad news for rates. A labour market that adds 165,000 jobs removes the argument for waiting. That makes a December rise likely even as shares fall, which is the uncomfortable combination for investors: the central bank has little reason to help.

3. Central banks are tightening for one reason. The Bank of England, the Bank of Canada and, probably, the Federal Reserve are responding to energy-driven inflation, not to strong demand. That ties all of this week's decisions back to oil and the Strait of Hormuz; see the Geopolitics briefing for the war itself.

4. This is a short edition by design. Gold, the German share index and oil's Friday settlement are missing because we could not confirm them. Tuesday's consumer price index is the next test, and the 17–18 November period brings Nvidia's results and the Federal Reserve meeting minutes.

Four days after the last edition, the main change is that the question moved from "how much will the election matter" to "how much of the AI share rally was resting on spending that may slow".
