LEADUS TAX POLICY23 Jul
New US federal guidance lets pet food manufacturers deduct 100% of new factory costs in year one instead of over 39 years
The IRS and Treasury Department issued Notice 2026-16, interim guidance on a new "qualified production property" (QPP) deduction under Section 168(n) of the tax code, created by Section 70307 of the One, Big, Beautiful Bill Act. Manufacturers — including pet food and treat producers — can now deduct 100% of the adjusted basis of qualifying new-build or upgraded factory space in the year it is placed in service, rather than depreciating it over the standard 39-year schedule. The American Pet Products Association highlighted the guidance to members. It applies to construction that began after 19 January 2025 and before 1 January 2029, with property placed in service by 1 January 2031.
chevron_rightRead more
Context & background

QPP is a narrower, more generous cousin of existing tools like Section 179 expensing and standard bonus depreciation — both of which manufacturers can already combine with QPP where eligible. Notice 2026-16 does not create the deduction (that was already law via the One, Big, Beautiful Bill Act's Section 70307); it is the interim guidance clarifying how manufacturers actually claim it, which is why petfood trade press only picked it up now, roughly eighteen months after the underlying law's construction-start eligibility date. The American Pet Products Association surfaced the notice to its membership specifically because pet food and treat manufacturing qualifies as an eligible "production activity."

The details

To qualify, property must be nonresidential real property used as an integral part of a "qualified production activity" defined as manufacturing, production or refining that results in a "substantial transformation" into a finished product; it must be placed in service in the US or a US territory and be "originally used" by the taxpayer claiming the deduction. Quality testing, oversight, and receiving/storage of raw materials used in production can count toward eligible space if they occur within the same facility as the production activity itself. Excluded: office space, administrative areas, R&D and software-engineering space, lodging, parking, sales space, and — notably — space used to store finished products (as opposed to raw materials or production inputs). Standard depreciation rules otherwise apply; property under the alternative depreciation system does not qualify.

Context

This lands in the middle of the capacity-building wave this briefing has tracked across three prior editions — Nestlé Purina's three simultaneous 2026 plant commitments, Vafo Group's Poland investment, MHP's Croatia plant — all substantially outside the US and therefore outside this specific deduction's reach, but a lower after-tax cost of new US factory construction is exactly the kind of policy lever that could pull the next wave of announcements toward domestic sites specifically. It also runs directly against the capacity-outpaces-demand caution flagged elsewhere in petfood trade press this year: a tax incentive that makes new US capacity cheaper to build does not, by itself, guarantee that capacity is needed.

(1) Whether a manufacturer publicly cites the QPP deduction as a factor in announcing a new US plant. (2) Whether the interim guidance changes materially once final regulations are issued. (3) Whether the capacity-glut caution this briefing has previously flagged sharpens or fades as the incentive takes effect.

LEADINSECT PROTEIN20 Jul
European insect-meal trade body positions the sector as a fishmeal substitute as a "Super El Niño" threatens Peru's anchovy fisheries
The International Platform of Insects for Food and Feed (IPIFF) — the European trade association for the insect-protein industry — is publicly calling for government incentives to help insect meal scale up and help fill a potential global fishmeal shortage as El Niño risk builds around Peru's anchovy fisheries. Per Feed Strategy's reporting, European insect-meal production has grown roughly fourfold, from about 10,000 metric tons in 2022 to a projected 40,000 metric tons in 2026, but IPIFF argues that closing a fishmeal gap at scale will require policy support rather than industry investment alone.
chevron_rightRead more
Context & background

This briefing has tracked Peru's anchovy and fishmeal squeeze since its first edition and does not restate those figures here — see the 22 and 25 July editions for the quota cut and IFFO production data. What is new this cycle is not the shortage itself but a European industry association explicitly stepping forward to position insect meal as part of the answer, in a public statement rather than this briefing's own inference. IPIFF is the recognised EU-level voice for insect-protein producers including black-soldier-fly players this briefing has separately tracked (Innovafeed, Bioflytech, NRGene Canada/FairGene).

The details

Confirmed via Feed Strategy's published article summary: European insect-meal production estimated at roughly 10,000 metric tons in 2022, rising to a projected 40,000 metric tons in 2026 — a roughly fourfold increase in four years. IPIFF frames this growth as evidence the sector could respond to a marine-ingredient shortfall, but says faster scaling depends on government incentives and policy support; the article's full text sits behind Feed Strategy's paywall beyond the opening paragraph and metadata summary used here, so the specific policy asks IPIFF is requesting are not independently confirmed by this briefing beyond what that summary states. Not disclosed in the available material: which governments IPIFF is petitioning, or what specific incentive mechanisms it is proposing.

Context

This is the first time in this briefing's tracking that the marine-ingredient-tightness thread and the insect-protein-capacity thread have been linked by an industry body itself, rather than by this briefing's own editorial juxtaposition (as in the 28 July edition's EU-Mercosur/India item). It is also a reminder that insect meal's largest current market is aquaculture and general animal feed, not petfood specifically — any petfood-formulation impact would be downstream of broader feed-sector substitution, not a direct petfood story yet.

LEADPREMIUMIZATION / US2 Jul (earnings call 1 Jul)
General Mills leans further into cat food and fresh packaging for fiscal 2027, even as its wider business had a rough year
General Mills' North America Pet segment reported fourth-quarter net sales of $702.4 million (+4% year-on-year) and full-year net sales of $2.6 billion (+6%), on its 1 July earnings call — pet now accounts for roughly 14% of the company's total net sales. For fiscal 2027, the company plans a first national marketing campaign for Tiki Cat, a wet-food gravy extension for Blue Buffalo's Blue Tastefuls line, continued investment in the Life Protection Formula dog line, and further expansion of its "Love Made Fresh" fresh dog food line — which saw roughly 80% Q4 retail-sales acceleration behind a new stand-up resealable pouch format and better in-store execution.
chevron_rightRead more
Context & background

General Mills entered petfood through its 2018 acquisition of Blue Buffalo and has since added further pet brands, including Whitebridge Pet Brands (already covered in an earlier edition of this briefing; this item does not restate that acquisition's terms). Chief operating officer Dana McNabb and chairman/CEO Jeff Harmening laid out the fiscal-2027 plan on the company's 1 July earnings call, reported by Pet Food Processing on 2 July.

The details

Q4: pet segment net sales $702.4 million versus $675.2 million a year earlier (+4%); cat food net sales up double-digits, dog food up low-single-digits, treats down low-single-digits; organic pet net sales down 3% for the quarter; segment operating profit $160 million (+14%), aided by favorable pricing/mix and lower input costs, partly offset by higher SG&A and media spend. Full year: pet net sales $2.6 billion (+6%), aided in part by lapping the Whitebridge acquisition; pet now roughly 14% of total company net sales; full-year segment operating profit roughly flat at $499 million. Company-wide, General Mills' full-year operating profit fell 73% to $885.8 million on weaker consumer sentiment across its broader (non-pet) portfolio, and the company is targeting $3 billion in cumulative cost savings through fiscal 2030 ($750 million of that in fiscal 2027 alone) via its Holistic Margin Management program and a broader transformation initiative including supply-chain redesign. FY27 company-wide organic sales guidance is a range of -1.5% to +0.5%.

Context

This is a different flavor of "why capital is moving toward premium petfood" than the PE buy-and-build stories this briefing usually surfaces under this section: a large strategic consumer-goods company is deliberately concentrating growth investment (national marketing, a wet-food line extension, a packaging-led fresh-food relaunch) inside its best-performing division while its base business struggles company-wide — pet as an internal capital-allocation lever rather than an external acquisition target. The "Love Made Fresh" pouch relaunch is also this cycle's closest thing to a packaging-format story, following the pattern of the Drools/Tetra Pak retort-pouch item in the 28 July edition — a brand-led packaging change rather than a packaging-producer capacity investment, so it belongs here and not in the still-open packaging-producer gap this briefing has flagged in every prior edition.

Watch-Items

(1) Whether Tiki Cat's national campaign and the Blue Tastefuls gravy extension sustain double-digit cat-segment growth into fiscal 2027. (2) Whether "Love Made Fresh" retail-sales acceleration holds once the initial pouch-relaunch effect fades. (3) Whether General Mills' FY27 organic-sales guidance band (-1.5% to +0.5%) proves conservative given pet's relative strength.

Analysis

Four reads from the thinnest cycle in this series

  1. The thinness is itself the finding. Three consecutive July editions harvested nearly everything discoverable on this niche beat inside an eleven-day span. A fourth edition only five days after the third was always going to come up short unless the underlying news flow accelerated — it did not. A near-weekly cadence on a beat this narrow will keep producing editions like this one; a slower cadence, or a wider window per edition, would likely read as more substantial without inventing anything.
  2. Two policy stories, not deal stories, carried this edition. For the first time in this series, the Production investments item is a tax-code change rather than a plant announcement, and the Raw-material item is a trade association's public positioning statement rather than a supply shock. Both are legitimate landscape intelligence — QPP genuinely lowers the after-tax cost of new US petfood capacity, and IPIFF's statement genuinely links two threads this briefing has tracked separately — but neither is a "something got built" or "something got bought" story, which is worth naming rather than dressing up as one.
  3. General Mills shows a second path into premium petfood investment, alongside the PE buy-and-build pattern this briefing usually tracks. A strategic consumer-goods company redirecting marketing and packaging investment toward its strongest division while its base business struggles is a different mechanism from a PE sponsor buying a platform — both are visibly moving capital toward the same premiumization trend at the same time, from opposite directions.
  4. The MPM/Partners Group date catch is worth generalizing, not just reporting. A search summary presented a real, well-documented 2025 transaction as if it happened in 2026; only checking the acquirer's own press release caught it. On a beat this narrow, where the same handful of trade outlets get re-indexed and re-summarized repeatedly, that kind of silent misdating is a real risk for every edition in this series, not just this one.

Fourth-edition note: three items across four sections, with PE & M&A explicitly empty, is the smallest haul this series has produced. That is reported plainly rather than padded with older material already covered three times over the past eleven days.
