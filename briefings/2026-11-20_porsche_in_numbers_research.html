Porsche in Numbers: 78 Years in 50 Charts

How many cars Porsche builds, which ones, where they go, and how the cars themselves have changed since 1948 — shown in charts, with as few tables as possible.

Key takeaways

  • Scale: from 317 cars (1950) to a record 320,221 (2023); 279,449 in 2025 and falling in 2026.
  • Mix: about 59% of deliveries are SUVs, 25% sports cars, 16% saloons. In 2001 it was 100% sports cars.
  • The 911: more than 1.3 million built, record deliveries three years running, and its share of the business has risen from 12% (2019) to 18.5% (2025) and 24% (9M 2026).
  • Technology: the base 911 went from 130 to 394 PS, from 9.0 to 4.1 seconds, and from 1,080 to 1,520 kg. The most powerful Porsche is now an electric SUV with 1,156 PS.
  • Price: adjusted for inflation a base 911 costs about 2.4 times what it did in 1964, but most of that jump happened before 1989.
  • Regions and money: China is down 56% from its 2021 peak; operating margin fell from 18% (2023) to 1.1% (2025).

1 · How many Porsches: from 317 cars to 320,000

Porsche built 317 cars by the end of 1950. It needed until July 1996 to build its first million. The record year so far is 2023 with 320,221 deliveries; 2025 ended at 279,449, and the first nine months of 2026 are another 16% lower.

The series hides the company's near-death. Sales peaked around 1986, fell to about 14,000 in 1993, and only the Boxster (1996) and then the Cayenne (2002) lifted volume to a different scale.

Which models carry the volume

Until 2002 Porsche sold two sports cars. Today two SUVs make up about 59% of deliveries, and the 911 — the car the brand is known for — about 18%.

The 911 is growing while the rest shrinks

The 911 set delivery records three years running (2023–2025). Because every other line is falling, its share of the business has almost doubled since 2019.

2 · The portfolio: from one car to 85 versions

For its first 27 years Porsche built rear-engine sports cars only. The 1970s added front-engine cars to survive the oil-crisis era, the 1990s cut everything back to the 911 and a cheaper Boxster, and from 2002 the company added one new segment roughly every five years.

The range has widened inside each line too. In 1965 there was one 911. In 2015 a count found 21 versionsC2; today the German configurator lists 22, and the Cayenne has even more.

3 · The 911 in depth

More than a million 911s had been built by May 2017. Adding deliveries since then (about 336,000 for 2018–2025) puts the running total near 1.35 million — our estimate, since Porsche has not published a figure for the current 992 generation.

Output per year roughly doubled with the switch to water cooling and shared parts with the Boxster in 1998. The 991 is the most-built generation; the air-cooled cars that collectors prize were made in far smaller numbers.

Coupé, Cabriolet or Targa

About one 911 in three is a Cabriolet, a share that has been stable for two generations. The Targa fell from a quarter of sales in the 1980s to 3–4%. No body split was found for the 991 and 992.

Manual, all-wheel drive, Turbo

Hard take rates are rare; Porsche publishes almost none. What exists: fewer than one in ten 991s was ordered with a manual, but the GT3 is the opposite, especially in the US. Porsche says about half of Carrera S buyers choose all-wheel driveC5. The 997 Turbo was about 9% of 997 productionC2.

4 · Technical data over six decades

The base 911 has three times the power of the 1964 car and weighs about 40% more. Each PS now moves under 4 kg instead of more than 8.

Acceleration has more than halved. Top speed rose quickly until the late 1990s and has been flat near 290 km/h for the base car since 2012 — the gains moved to acceleration and lap times.

Across the range

The power record has passed from sports cars to a hybrid supercar and now to electric cars: the most powerful Porsche you can order in 2026 is an SUV.

5 · What a 911 costs

In today's money the first 911 cost about €55,000 and the 1974 car about €46,000. By 1989 the real price had more than doubled to about €109,000 — and since then it has risen only moderately, to about €131,000 for the 2024 car. The current list price is €137,800.

Measured per unit of power, a 911 is cheaper than in 1989: the price jump of the 1980s bought a much more complex car, and since then power has grown faster than the real price.

6 · Where the cars go and where they are built

China was Porsche's largest single market from 2015 to 2023. It peaked at 95,671 cars in 2021 and had fallen by 56% by 2025; the first nine months of 2026 brought 21,493. North America has been stable at about 86,000.

7 · Electric, hybrid, combustion

About a third of Porsches delivered in 2025 had a plug. The Taycan, the first electric Porsche, peaked in 2021 and has lost 60% of its volume since; the electric Macan now carries the battery-electric share.

8 · The money

For a decade Porsche earned 15–18 cents of operating profit on every euro of revenue, among the highest margins in the car industry. In 2025 that collapsed to 1.1%: China, US tariffs, and the cost of reversing parts of the electric strategy. Since January 2026 the chief executive is Michael Leiters, who succeeded Oliver Blume.

It is the second deep crisis. In the first, sales fell by more than two thirds between 1986 and 1993 and the company posted losses; lean production then halved the hours needed to build a 911.

9 · Totals, rarities and records

Counted over its whole life, the Cayenne has probably overtaken the 911 as the most-built Porsche — in 23 years against 61. The Macan reached a million in eleven.

10 · What the numbers cannot show

Take rates are mostly unpublished. Porsche does not report how many buyers choose a manual, all-wheel drive, a Cabriolet or a Turbo. We found body splits for three 911 generations, a few manual-gearbox statements, and nothing reliable on colours, options spend or Sport Turismo shares.

Early years are thin. Annual company totals before 1994 could not be verified beyond a few anchor points. Launch prices for the 1978, 1984, 1998 and 2016 911 are missing, so the price line skips those generations.

Sources disagree on 911 generation totals. Porsche itself publishes different counts depending on where it draws the line between generations (for example 63,762 or 74,008 for the 964). We used the most widely quoted set.

Technical data mix measurement standards. Weights follow DIN where we could find it; some recent Wikipedia figures include a driver. Acceleration figures are factory claims with the fastest standard gearbox.

Sales, deliveries and production are three different numbers. We label which is used. Fiscal years ran August to July until 2010.

Two different debates sit behind the charts. One is whether a company that sells three SUVs and saloons for every sports car is still a sports-car maker — the mix says no, the profit and brand logic of the 911 says yes. The other is the electric strategy: Porsche planned for more than 80% electric by 2030, has since extended combustion and hybrid models, and booked about €2.4bn for the realignment in 2025; the status of the electric 718 is reported differently by different outlets.
