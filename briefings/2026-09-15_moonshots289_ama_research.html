The AMA where almost nothing was checkable

Peter Diamandis, Dave Blundin, Salim Ismail and Alexander Wissner-Gross spent ninety minutes answering questions from a Zoom room of roughly three hundred listeners. No guest, no news peg — mostly career advice, education advice and speculation. A fact-check of an episode like this is largely a report on how little there is to fact-check, and where the few hard numbers came from.

The argument in brief

Three positions the panel would sign without hedging, stated at their strongest and before any checking.

Reasoning models have outgrown the word "prediction"

Calling a large language model an autocomplete engine was fair for a 2021 system trained purely to predict the next token. It is not fair for a model reinforcement-fine-tuned against reasoning problems with verifiable answers. To keep using the term is to assert that such a system cannot produce anything genuinely new — an assertion that is now empirically awkward.

The binding risk in AI policy is too much rule-making, not too little

Regulators move on a schedule set by incidents; capability moves on a schedule set by compute. A federal regime that locks in safety constraints now would freeze an industry already self-correcting through competitive pressure, while a rival state without those constraints keeps compounding. And the cost of delay is not abstract: people die at roughly a hundred and fifty thousand a day, and the most plausible near-term tool against that number is a much more capable model.

Education should start from a problem, not a curriculum

The supply-side model — train as a doctor, a lawyer, an engineer, then find demand for that credential — assumes the demand side is legible years ahead. It no longer is. Let a child pick a problem they care about and reverse-engineer the skills from there, with an AI tutor that compresses a classroom day into an hour. The reference point is not a policy paper but a novel: the adaptive primer in Neal Stephenson's The Diamond Age.

The argument, walked through

In the order built. Verdict scale: 5/5 primary source · 4/5 two independent sources · 3/5 single credible source · 2/5 aggregator only. ASR errors in proper nouns are corrected silently; the list is in section 3.

1 · "Stop calling them prediction machines" 00:09

A caller said she treats LLMs as prediction machines and is more interested in "the emergent latent space, ahas, off the beaten path kind of sparking creativity." Wissner-Gross took the bait deliberately.

"I don't think it's accurate to characterize them as prediction machines or stochastic parrots" — Alexander Wissner-Gross (attribution contextual — this passage carries no speaker label in the source transcript)

Reasoning models have been fine-tuned so heavily against reasoning challenges, he argued, that the old term is now "tantamount to saying basically that they're incapable of novelty or insight, which is most definitively not true at this point." That is a position, not a fact, and carries no verdict — but the people who coined the phrase have not conceded it.

Other viewsEmily Bender, co-author of the 2021 "Stochastic Parrots" paper, has not moved: her 2025 book with Alex Hanna argues it remains fundamentally confused to apply human-cognition vocabulary to current LLMs, reasoning models included. Melanie Mitchell and co-authors (2025) hold the middle — capabilities plainly exceed parroting, but that is no licence to treat these systems as autonomous reasoners in safety-critical settings. Kempt and Lavie, "Simulated Reasoning is Reasoning" (arXiv, January 2026), argue directly for the side attributed above to Wissner-Gross (contextual, per the note on speaker labels) while conceding the brittleness that comes from a lack of grounding.

The term is contested, not retired — and the strongest form of each side concedes something to the other.

The same exchange produced the episode's one neuroscience number. Diamandis, on what a brain–computer interface would add: "we have 100 trillion synaptic connections, 100 billion neurons that were limited by."

CheckWrong figure C 5/5 neurons · 2/5 synapses — Herculano-Houzel's isotropic-fractionator counts put the human brain at roughly 86 billion neurons, alongside some 85 billion non-neuronal cells; the on-air figure is about 14% high. The companion "100 trillion synapses" number is a separate, widely repeated estimate for which no primary source was located — unverified rather than wrong. ↑ Scorecard

2 · "Over-regulation is the bigger risk" 00:25

A caller with a background in tech regulation at Google and X asked how a US federal official should frame AI risk assessments. Wissner-Gross put the framing upstream of the method: "the risk at this point is over-regulation, not under-regulation."

"I am deathly concerned that we will overregulate our way into losing a superintelligence race with China." — Alexander Wissner-Gross

He paired it immediately with the humanitarian case, which does most of the work:

"more than 150,000 people on earth die every day" — Alexander Wissner-Gross

"And probably the single best antidote to that will be superintelligence for solving medicine." Pressed on non-regulatory incident response — what a government should do after a breach — he did not soften: "I'd say hands off," because the industry is already advancing through "defensive co-scaling" and well-meant safety interventions risk "hamstringing our ability to solve honest to goodness important problems." At its strongest this is the administration's own position: emergency rule-making hands a compounding advantage to a state that will not reciprocate.

CheckMostly true, outdated C 2/5 — current trackers put 2025–26 global mortality at roughly 63 million a year, or 165,000–173,000 a day: 10–15% above the figure used, which reflects an older ~55m/year baseline. Note the direction — the number understates current mortality and is deployed to argue for acceleration, so the error works against the speaker's own case. The hedge "more than" is his, and it does real work: with it, the sentence is not false. Low confidence — only aggregators; no primary UN or WHO figure located. ↑ Scorecard
Other viewsMax Tegmark argues the inverse: his high estimate of catastrophe is explicitly conditional on no regulation, which makes "regulation is the risk" a restatement of the thing he thinks produces the risk. Dario Amodei is the more awkward counter-example, being an industry insider rather than an outside critic: his June 2026 regulatory agenda — FAA-style oversight of frontier systems, support for displaced workers, a global democratic AI coalition — rejects the safety-versus-competitiveness framing outright, and in September 2026 he said the industry had for too long lied about the risks. On Wissner-Gross's side sits the administration itself, making the same competitiveness argument.

The disagreement is not about whether China is racing, but about whether an unregulated US frontier is a faster runner or a less reliable one.

Diamandis then layered career advice on top: declare a narrow expertise and become the government's go-to on it. He cited having watched David Sacks "from the All-In podcast" — the transcript's ASR renders the surname "Sachs" — "get dragged into the White House, and unbelievably effective in that role for a year," then a second example — "our friend of the pod, Alvin Graylin, declared himself the top expert on China AI race. And now he got dragged in for the United Nations meetings on September 24th as a top advisor to the White House." The advice is sound enough. The Sacks tenure checks out; the second example slips.

CheckTrue, with context C 4/5 — Sacks was appointed AI and crypto czar in January 2025 and stepped down on 26 March 2026, a calendar tenure of roughly fourteen months — so "for a year" is not overstated; if anything it undersells it. The detail the phrase leaves invisible is that the post was structured as a special-government-employee role capped at 130 days of counted service, a chargeable-service ceiling that measures something different from how long he held the job. He moved on to co-chair the President's Council of Advisors on Science and Technology (PCAST). Whether he was "unbelievably effective" is opinion and carries no verdict. ↑ Scorecard
CheckMisleading C 4/5 — no evidence of a formal White House role was found. Graylin is Global VP at HTC, a Senior Fellow at the Asia Society Policy Institute and a Digital Fellow at Stanford HAI, and advises governments and multilateral bodies broadly. That is a real advisory profile; it is not a documented post in a US administration, and "top advisor to the White House" reads as one. ↑ Scorecard

3 · The advice layer, and why it is not checkable 00:04–01:12

Most of the ninety minutes is this: an Australian worried his country is behind, an engineer in Indiana asking what the playbook is for people with mortgages, a parent asking whether curricula still matter. Ismail told the first that emerging markets can leapfrog because they have less to unlearn, and added the one piece of advice with a mechanism attached: "create deliberate slack in the system," ten or twenty per cent of your time unscheduled, because "if you wait till you have spare time, you'll never catch up."

Blundin took the Indiana caller: build things with the models at night, watch for the franchise and data-collection playbooks the labs are about to hand out, and then — "the other thing you can do is try to network with" — a line about the company you keep.

"Drew Houston, the Dropbox founder, said you become the average of the five people you spend the most time with" — Dave Blundin

"It's just a fact. Don't fight it." Diamandis calls back to it in the sign-off — "my favorite time of the week is spending time with my moonshot mates, you know, the average of five people" — and nobody on the panel questions the name attached to it.

CheckWrong attribution C 4/5 — the line is overwhelmingly attributed to the motivational speaker Jim Rohn, and its earliest documented print appearance is a 2005 chapter epigraph crediting Rohn in Canfield and Switzer's The Success Principles; Quote Investigator traces the idea circulating earlier still. No documented origin with Drew Houston exists. The advice may or may not survive scrutiny — "it's just a fact" is doing more work than the evidence supports — but the byline is simply the wrong one. ↑ Scorecard
"Go to the demand side, tell the kids to get excited about a problem" — Salim Ismail, on education

The education argument: we teach as we did 150 years ago; a child with an AI absorbs in an hour what a classroom day delivers; that mismatch breaks the system; so build from the problem, not the syllabus. Asked whether children should have a sovereign agent from age five, Diamandis answered with fiction rather than policy — "Read Neal Stephenson's book, Diamond Age." Blundin supplied the counterweight from inside the panel: the curriculum may become irrelevant, but "the kids need each other and they need school more than ever before."

Other viewsNamed critics run the other way on credentials. A Lumina Foundation analysis reported by US News in February 2026 argues a degree becomes more valuable in the AI era, because the labour-market signal matters more once entry-level tasks are automated. Rubin Pillay takes the reform-not-obsolescence line: what AI exposes as outdated is the execution work inside a doctorate — literature review, drafting, analysis — not doctoral training as such.

Blundin's advice to a civil engineer with thirty years of project records was the most concrete thing said all episode, and also the least verifiable: labs "all saying we want to write five, 10, $15 million checks to buy corporations that have data outright," so package the archive, price it yourself, and above all show up — "just go to SF, find that person who's doing the acquisitions" and meet at the coffee shop outside their building. The anecdote used to justify it is where the job title went stale.

CheckWrong — outdated C 5/5 — Blundin calls Kevin Weil "a chief product officer at OpenAI"; Diamandis confirms "CPO at OpenAI." Weil held that role from mid-2024, later moved to a research position founding OpenAI for Science, and left OpenAI entirely on 17 April 2026 — five months before this episode aired. Confirmed by his own departure post. The anecdote itself (hired, heard nothing, told by Altman to simply show up) is not in dispute; only the present tense is. ↑ Scorecard

A Louisiana caller — a deep-sea ROV specialist retrained into data-centre cooling — supplied the other number.

"we got $250 billion that's dropped in here now" — caller, on Louisiana data-centre investment
CheckWrong figure C 4/5 — the one confirmed mega-project, Meta's Richland Parish campus, had reached more than $50 billion by July 2026, up from $10bn at announcement in 2024. No reporting aggregates Louisiana data-centre commitments anywhere near $250bn. His underlying point — cheap natural gas is pulling hyperscalers into the state — is well supported; the figure is roughly five times the documented total. He is a caller, not a panellist, and nobody corrected him. ↑ Scorecard

The one place where numbers are read out deliberately rather than recalled is the sponsor segment. Fountain Life's chief medical officer says CT angiography with AI analytics is "finding that 88% of people coming in have detectable coronary disease," and that "23% of those individuals had soft plaque." The 88% is true to the company's own PR (C 3/5) — but that describes roughly eight thousand self-selected, paying longevity-clinic members, pre-sorted for age, affluence and health anxiety; without the caveat it reads as a finding about people in general. The 23% appears in no published material, including Fountain Life's own. The segment also states that "50% of people die of heart attacks with no warning signs" — a term conflation: the literature supports roughly that figure for sudden cardiac death, a distinct condition from myocardial infarction (C 3/5), and that is the term the same speaker uses correctly moments earlier, describing her own husband's death.

Almost none of the advice layer is falsifiable, and a research piece should say so rather than manufacture a scorecard out of it.

Stepping back

The error direction in this run. Seven scorecard-checkable claims in eighty-six minutes, and five are wrong or overstated: a job title five months stale, a neuron count 14% high, a White House role that does not appear to exist, a quotation hung on the wrong man, an investment figure roughly five times the documented total. Two break the pattern. The daily-mortality figure understates current data and is used to argue for acceleration — the error cuts against the speaker's own case. And David Sacks's "for a year" checks out — his calendar tenure runs roughly fourteen months — though the phrase leaves invisible the 130-day cap on his counted service. Neither looks like directional bias. The honest reading is staleness and round-number recall under live conditions, not a uniform pattern of overstatement. One episode is not a base rate, and an AMA is the weakest possible sample for this kind of test.

The messenger

The format is an unscripted live AMA — about twenty callers in sequence, the host noting eighty then ninety-seven raised hands and asking his panel to be brief. Figures in that setting are recalled, not read, which is exactly the condition that produces stale job titles and rounded numbers, and makes this a weaker fact-checking target than the news episodes in the same batch. Diamandis moderates while also running XPRIZE and Fountain Life, and the Fountain Life segment is a sponsor read presenting company-internal data from a self-selected paying cohort as a general finding. Compiler disclosure: written with an Anthropic model, a competitor to OpenAI, whose departed executive is the subject of c1.

What could not be substantiated

  • The 23% soft-plaque figure — absent from all published material, including the company's own.
  • The 100 trillion synapse count — widely repeated, no primary source located.
  • OpenExO's stated 50,000 members — no published count found.
  • That frontier labs write "five, 10, $15 million checks" for data companies at that granularity. The broader trend of premium-paying AI acquisitions is documented; that range is not.

A method note

The failure worth recording here is not that an AI digest fabricated material. It is that an automated verification step falsely accused a correct summary of fabrication, and two true items were struck from the brief on the strength of that accusation.

The mechanism was mundane. The search pattern demanded a long run of same-line context from a transcript whose lines are short, so it could not match; and one surname was searched in the correct spelling while the ASR had rendered it with a different letter — "Sachs" for Sacks. Two null results, read as proof of invention. Both lines are in the source, at roughly sixteen and twenty-seven minutes. They are the checks numbered c6 and c7 above, and they came within one step of not appearing at all — caught only because the next agent in the chain went back to the transcript instead of accepting the verdict handed to it.

A false charge of fabrication is the harder error to see: it removes true material silently, while looking like diligence. That bears on the same question as the original warning — how much weight a reader should give AI-assisted research, this piece included. Every quotation above was individually grep-verified against the transcript for that reason.

What survives

  • The daily-mortality argument, strengthened. The real figure is higher than the one quoted, which makes the humanitarian case for faster medical AI stronger — whatever one concludes about the regulatory inference drawn from it.
  • The reasoning-models position is defensible and live. Kempt and Lavie argue the same case in print — though the authors of the phrase Wissner-Gross wants retired have not retired it.
  • "Deliberate slack," and physically showing up. Neither is falsifiable, but both are specific, mechanism-bearing and cheap to test on yourself.
  • The 88% coronary-disease figure is real — in the population it was measured in.

What to discount

  • Names, titles and dollar figures recalled live. Five of seven checkable items were stale, misattributed or inflated; treat named roles, tenures and quotation bylines in this format as needing a date stamp and a source.
  • The sponsor figures as general health findings. The 88% describes paying clinic members; the 23% has no published basis; the "no warning signs" figure belongs to sudden cardiac death, not heart attack.
  • Caller claims that go uncorrected. The $250bn figure came from an audience member and passed unchallenged; silence is not verification.
  • The implied consensus on education. Named researchers argue the credential is becoming more valuable — a position no one on the call represented.
