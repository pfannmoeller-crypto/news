Geopolitics · 25–28 July 2026 (3-day window)

Zelensky flies to Washington for a second Trump meeting this month while Ukraine's drone campaign against Russian retail reaches Moscow's own streets, Iran logs its first quiet night since strikes resumed, and Romania downs its third drone in three days.

Three days ago the frame was a half-resolved Ukrainian cabinet crisis, a rejected Iranian ceasefire offer, and NATO's first drone shootdown of the war. Some of that has moved and some has simply continued: Kyiv and Washington's diplomatic coordination is now visible in Zelensky's travel schedule rather than just anonymous official quotes; Ukraine's strike campaign against Russia's civilian retail backbone, which this briefing first flagged last week as a new target category, widened sharply and reached the capital itself; Tehran and Washington traded a rare quiet night alongside continued, unresolved argument over who controls transit through the Strait of Hormuz; and Romania's single shootdown became three in three days, with the drone's origin now confirmed. Meanwhile, Moscow's crackdown on potential election challengers intensified ahead of September's parliamentary vote, and the Pentagon's casualty-count dispute evolved into a new bureaucratic category rather than resolving.

Ukraine / Russia — Zelensky returns to Washington, the drone war on Russian retail reaches Moscow, and the pre-election crackdown intensifies

4 items
Diplomacy28 Jul

Zelensky heads to Washington for a second Trump meeting this month, ahead of Senator Graham's funeral

President Zelensky arrived in Washington on 28 July for what officials describe as his second sit-down with Trump this month, held at the White House ahead of Senator Lindsey Graham's funeral at Washington National Cathedral; Zelensky is also due at the Capitol that evening to meet with senators. Graham's final act as a public official was a visit to Kyiv where he helped secure agreement on a package of sanctions targeting countries that purchase Russian oil, gas and other exports — giving the funeral a direct, if solemn, link to this week's broader sanctions story. The visit follows a 22 July call in which Zelensky and Trump envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner discussed "how to intensify diplomacy and bring peace closer"; Zelensky has said he wants US-Ukraine-Russia trilateral talks in July rather than the fall timeline officials have floated, though as of this writing Witkoff and Kushner have no confirmed plans to visit Kyiv and are instead expected in Russia in the coming days.

Context & background. Last week's edition reported the "new peace push" as a set of anonymous-official quotes and an expected but unconfirmed Witkoff/Kushner Moscow trip. This week supplies the concrete calendar: a confirmed Zelensky-Trump meeting, a specific venue (the Graham funeral), and Zelensky's own public framing of the timeline he wants (July, not fall) versus the timeline officials have floated (fall 2026) — the clearest evidence yet of where Kyiv and Washington's positions genuinely diverge within an otherwise aligned diplomatic push.

The details. That Witkoff and Kushner are travelling to Moscow without a corresponding Kyiv stop is structurally significant: it means the active shuttle diplomacy at this moment runs through Washington (for Ukraine's input) and Moscow (for Russia's), rather than through Kyiv directly — a sequencing that gives the US envoys maximum flexibility to test Russian positions without over-committing Ukraine to specific terms in advance. The Graham-funeral venue itself is doing real diplomatic work: it is a low-formality, unavoidably scheduled event that lets Trump and Zelensky meet without the collapse risk of a dedicated summit, echoing last week's edition's read of the same tactic. Graham's own legacy item — the sanctions-on-buyers bill tied to his final Kyiv visit — quietly links this personnel story to the EU's 21st package and the broader sanctions-pressure campaign covered elsewhere in this briefing.

Context. The gap between Zelensky's stated preference (trilateral talks this month) and the fall timeline reportedly favoured by US officials is worth tracking as a genuine, not cosmetic, disagreement — it suggests Kyiv sees urgency (plausibly tied to its own Patriot shortage and the static front line) that Washington's envoys do not yet share, or are not yet ready to act on. Until Witkoff and Kushner's Moscow trip actually happens and produces a readout, this remains a diplomacy-in-motion story rather than a diplomacy-with-results one.

Watch-Items. (1) The substantive readout from the Tuesday White House meeting. (2) Confirmation of dates and outcome for the Witkoff/Kushner Moscow trip. (3) Whether Zelensky's Capitol meetings produce any new Congressional aid or sanctions commitments tied to Graham's legacy legislation. (4) Any narrowing of the July-vs-fall timeline gap.

Front line25–27 Jul

A Russian wedge opens between Pokrovsk and Kostiantynivka, while barrages kill civilians in Kyiv and occupied Horlivka

Russian forces made further gains between the Donetsk Oblast towns of Pokrovsk and Kostiantynivka, according to Ukrainian military spokesperson Viktor Tregubov on 27 July, who described the Pokrovsk direction as still "the key vector" of Russia's offensive and the sector where Moscow has concentrated most of its troops. Overnight into 26 July, Russian ballistic missiles struck Kyiv, killing two people and wounding at least three, while Russian-occupied Horlivka's Russian-appointed mayor said a Ukrainian drone strike killed four civilians there the same night; Russia's Defence Ministry separately claimed it downed 133 Ukrainian drones over Russian territory, the Black Sea and Crimea.

Read moreexpand_more

Context & background. Last week's edition described the front line as static at roughly 1.6 km²/day of Russian gains, framed as generalised incremental movement rather than any specific breakthrough shape. This week's reporting is qualitatively different: for the first time this briefing has a named "wedge" forming between two specific towns, rather than a diffuse rate of advance.

The details. Tregubov's framing — that Russia has concentrated most of its troops on this single direction — implies a shift from broad-front pressure toward a narrower breakthrough attempt, which would be consistent with an army trying to force a decisive local result rather than grind uniformly. Russia's claimed 133-drone intercept figure, if accurate, indicates Ukraine's own strike tempo against Russian territory remains high even as this section covers Russian territorial gains — the ground and air dimensions of the war are moving in different directions simultaneously. The Horlivka and Kyiv casualty reports come from opposite sides of the front (a Russian-appointed official reporting Ukrainian-caused deaths, Ukrainian officials reporting Russian-caused deaths), a reminder that both sides' self-reported civilian tolls in this war require the same caution applied throughout this briefing.

Context. A confirmed wedge between two towns is the classic precursor to an encirclement threat if it widens further, which is why this briefing treats it as a genuine change from pure stasis rather than routine attritional movement — though independent ISW-level confirmation was not available to this briefing at time of writing and should be treated as the next checkpoint.

Watch-Items. (1) Independent (ISW or similar) confirmation of the wedge's scale. (2) Whether Kostiantynivka itself comes under direct pressure. (3) Barrage-size trend versus the 19 July record and the 23-24 July 180-drone night. (4) Any shift in Russian troop concentration away from Pokrovsk if the wedge stalls.

Confidence: medium (single Ukrainian military spokesperson quote; Russian MoD drone-intercept claim unverified)
Economic warfare24–28 Jul

Ukraine's drone war on Russian retail widens sharply — Wildberries hit in five regions, then Moscow itself

The Wildberries targeting campaign this briefing first flagged last week escalated dramatically: Ukrainian drones struck logistics centres near Moscow, St Petersburg, Krasnodar, Stavropol and Yekaterinburg, damaging an estimated 10% of the roughly 200-facility network of Russia's dominant e-commerce platform — often compared to Amazon — according to founder Tatyana Kim. At least six people were killed in the initial wave on 24 July, and on the morning of 28 July drones reached Moscow itself, with Mayor Sergei Sobyanin reporting more than ten intercepted over the capital. "We are, quite justly, bringing the war back home — to Russia," President Zelensky said.

Forbes · Wildberries campaign, network-damage estimate Moscow Times · at least 6 dead, 24 Jul Censor.NET · Moscow-region strikes reach the capital
Read moreexpand_more

Context & background. Last week's edition flagged the initial ten-warehouse wave as dual-purpose — degrading logistics while straining air defence and hitting civilian convenience — and treated it as a new but still-contained target category. This week confirms it was the opening of a sustained campaign: from roughly ten warehouses to at least seven distinct facility locations across five regions, and now Moscow's own airspace for the first time.

The details. Wildberries and rival Ozon together account for an estimated 8.5% of Russian GDP and employ more than 5% of the national workforce, having filled the vacuum left by Western retailers since 2022 — Forbes reporting frames the strikes as landing on an already-stressed sector: Russia recorded its first decline in total shop numbers in over two decades in 2025, no major new international retail brand entered the market in the first quarter of 2026, and Moscow shopping-centre vacancy rose to 5.7%. The 10% network-damage figure and the six confirmed deaths are the two hardest data points; both come from company and Russian-media sourcing rather than independent verification, and should be read as company- and state-adjacent estimates rather than confirmed totals. Zelensky's own quote — that Ukraine is "bringing the war back home" — confirms this is now acknowledged doctrine rather than incidental spillover from military-logistics targeting.

Context. Deliberately targeting a retail platform millions of ordinary Russians depend on for daily deliveries, rather than purely military or energy infrastructure, is a doctrine shift worth tracking in its own right: it converts Russia's existing sanctions-driven retail fragility into a second front that doesn't require a single additional Western sanction to bite, and it does so in a way domestic Russian media finds harder to reframe as a purely "military" target than an oil refinery or an air base.

Watch-Items. (1) Whether the 10% network-damage estimate rises in subsequent reporting. (2) Whether Ozon, Wildberries' rival, also becomes a target. (3) Any Kremlin acknowledgment beyond its standing denial that the warehouses serve military logistics. (4) Whether further strikes reach Moscow itself, or Sunday's ten-drone intercept proves a one-off.

Confidence: high (multi-outlet, company founder on record; the 10% damage figure is a single-sourced estimate)
Russia internal17–27 Jul, ongoing

Moscow intensifies its pre-election crackdown as September's Duma vote nears — a Putin critic convicted, a former loyalist arrested

With State Duma elections approaching in September, a Russian court convicted Putin critic Boris Nadezhdin of displaying "extremist symbols" — over a 2023 video that briefly showed a photo of the late opposition figure Alexei Navalny — a conviction that bars him from campaigning. The same day, former pro-Kremlin blogger-turned-critic Ilya Remeslo was arrested in St Petersburg on charges of spreading false information about the military. State-owned polling put Putin's approval at 65.1% as of 12 July, with 23.4% openly disapproving, against the backdrop of the fuel crisis triggered by Ukraine's refinery strikes.

Read moreexpand_more

Context & background. Last week's edition documented the Moscow Exchange's steepest one-day drop since 2022 and a 27-month-low Central Bank sentiment reading as evidence of economic distress. This week's political crackdown is the domestic-politics counterpart to those economic signals — evidence the Kremlin is acting preemptively against any electoral vehicle for dissent rather than waiting to see whether economic pain translates into votes.

The details. Nadezhdin's charge — a brief, years-old image of Navalny — is a deliberately low evidentiary bar that gives authorities wide latitude to disqualify candidates after the fact; Remeslo's case is more pointed still, since he was a pro-war voice until publishing a manifesto titled "Five reasons why I stopped supporting Vladimir Putin," and his prosecution signals that even a previously loyal, Kremlin-aligned media figure is not exempt once he breaks ranks. The 65.1%/23.4% approval split is a continuation of, not a break from, the erosion trend this briefing flagged last week (~66% and falling), suggesting the crackdown is being timed to the trend rather than to any single triggering event.

Context. Pairing an economic squeeze (fuel crisis, rate cuts, rising inflation) with legal action against potential opposition candidates and previously loyal critics is a defensive pattern consistent with a government that expects the economic story to be an electoral liability, and is closing off legitimate channels for it to be expressed rather than betting it can out-argue it in a campaign.

Watch-Items. (1) Further disqualifications or arrests as September approaches. (2) Whether the fuel crisis visibly worsens ahead of the vote. (3) Any measurable further movement in the approval trend. (4) Whether any genuinely independent candidate survives onto the ballot.

Confidence: high (conviction and arrest independently reported; approval figure from state-owned pollster, treat with usual caveats)

Iran / Hormuz — a first quiet night, a Hormuz "new route" dispute, and a casualty-count controversy that evolved rather than ended

5 items
Diplomacy25 Jul

Iran reports no new US strikes for the first time in two weeks — Trump softens his tone, but the Hormuz "new route" dispute persists

Iran reported no new US strikes overnight into 25 July — the first quiet night in roughly two weeks — as Tehran and Washington confirmed they are exchanging mediated proposals, though Iranian officials caution "not much progress" has been made given "fundamental differences" and deep mistrust. Trump softened his tone, saying Iranian officials "would love to make a deal" and that further "massive" strikes "may no longer be necessary," while adding "I don't think they're ready to [make a deal] ... but I'm willing to listen." The unresolved core dispute is the Strait of Hormuz: Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi said the US is trying to force open a new transit route bypassing Iranian-designated coordination, which Tehran says violates the June memorandum of understanding's clause giving it a say over routing; CENTCOM said it had "disabled" a tanker attempting to breach the US blockade the day before.

Al Jazeera · quiet night, Hormuz "new route" dispute
Read moreexpand_more

Context & background. Last week's edition recorded Iran rejecting an Iraq-relayed ceasefire proposal specifically because it failed to resolve Hormuz's final status. This week shows that rejection did not end the exchange: mediated proposals are still moving, even without "much progress," and for the first time since the 14 July blockade restart, an overnight period passed without a new US strike.

The details. The dispute's mechanism is now more precisely defined than last week's more general "Hormuz final status" framing: Iran claims a contractual right, under the June MoU, to set transit routes and be consulted before any new one is opened, while the US disputes that Iran has that authority at all — a disagreement over the MoU's application, not a rejection of the MoU itself. A former Austrian defence attaché quoted by Al Jazeera characterised the US approach as deliberately "hot and cold," alternating strikes and pauses to keep pressure on while leaving room to negotiate — a reading consistent with Trump's own softened but noncommittal language. Separately, Israeli Prime Minister Netanyahu is due to meet Trump at the White House the following week, adding a variable this briefing has not yet been able to assess for its effect on any US-Iran de-escalation.

Context. A single quiet night is a weak signal in isolation, but combined with Trump's rhetorical softening and confirmed message exchanges, it is the clearest indication yet that both sides may be probing for an off-ramp from the blockade-and-strikes cycle that began 14 July — though the Hormuz routing dispute remains exactly the sticking point flagged since the MoU's practical collapse two weeks ago.

Watch-Items. (1) Whether the quiet-night pattern extends beyond a single night. (2) Any specific new proposal addressing Hormuz routing authority. (3) The outcome and substance of Netanyahu's White House meeting. (4) Further CENTCOM blockade-enforcement actions and their effect on the diplomatic track.

Confidence: medium-high (Iranian officials and CENTCOM spokesperson on record with Al Jazeera; "not much progress" is a single-source characterisation)
Casualty transparency27 Jul

The Pentagon's casualty dispute deepens: a new "Overseas Operations" category splits the count rather than resolving it

Last week's Pentagon casualty-count controversy evolved further rather than resolving: on 27 July the Defense Department introduced a new tracking category, "Overseas Operations," backdated to 7 July — the day before the June ceasefire's collapse — effectively splitting the Iran-war casualty count into two administrative buckets instead of restoring the original figures. Combined across both categories, the Pentagon now lists 18 killed and 624 wounded (207 under the new category, 417 under the original "Operation Epic Fury"), while chief spokesman Sean Parnell continued to attribute the earlier 18-to-14 drop to a "temporary data disruption."

Army Times · new "Overseas Operations" category, 27 Jul
Read moreexpand_more

Context & background. Last week's edition documented the initial 18-to-14 revision and the bipartisan "ruse" accusation tied to War Powers Resolution reporting deadlines. This week's reclassification is a materially different move: rather than restoring or further disputing the number, the Pentagon created a new administrative category that changes which incidents count toward which named conflict, without changing the combined total.

The details. The 7 July start date for "Overseas Operations" sits one day before the ceasefire's collapse and the resumption of nightly CENTCOM strikes, and lines up with Trump's 10 July formal notification to Congress that hostilities had resumed — a notification the administration says restarts the 60-day clock under which force can be used without new congressional authorization. Defense Secretary Hegseth had separately argued in April that the ceasefire itself merely paused, rather than reset, that clock, a reading Senator Tim Kaine explicitly disputed on constitutional grounds at the time. Splitting the casualty count into "before" and "after" 7 July categories is consistent with the administration's broader position that the post-ceasefire fighting is a distinct conflict from Operation Epic Fury, even though the combined casualty total — 18 killed, 624 wounded — is unchanged in aggregate from before the controversy began.

Context. Whether this is a legitimate administrative distinction or a way to keep any single named conflict's casualty count below a politically sensitive threshold, the practical effect matches last week's flagged concern: the war's own casualty accounting has become a recurring point of dispute that outlasts any individual data correction. The new category's public page also omits distinguishing detail about what exactly it covers, which is likely to keep the story open rather than close it.

Watch-Items. (1) Whether Congress presses further on the War Powers Resolution question given the new categorisation. (2) Whether the "Overseas Operations" page is filled in with clearer detail. (3) Any further renaming of the conflict's official designation. (4) Whether the combined 18/624 total itself is revised again.

Confidence: high (official DCAS category change and combined totals independently reported across multiple outlets)

Escalation24–26 Jul

Romania intercepts three drones in three consecutive days, confirms Russian origin, and summons Russia's ambassador

Romania intercepted three drones in three consecutive days — Friday 24 July, Saturday 25 July, and an F-16 shootdown over the Black Sea on Sunday 26 July — prompting Bucharest to summon Russia's ambassador and hand him a verbal note demanding one Russian diplomat leave the country within five days. President Nicusor Dan confirmed the Friday drone was a Shahed-type, "used by the Russian Federation in its war of aggression against Ukraine," resolving last week's open question about the origin of NATO's first drone shootdown of the war. Russia's ambassador dismissed the summons as a "propaganda show" intended to deter further Russian strikes on Ukrainian port infrastructure.

Al Jazeera · three drones in three days, envoy summoned Moscow Times · Dan calls breaches "intolerable"
Read moreexpand_more

Context & background. Last week's edition flagged the 24 July shootdown as NATO's first of the war but explicitly left the drone's origin unconfirmed, noting it could be Russian-launched, a captured/redirected Ukrainian drone of the kind Poland had warned about, or something else. This week resolves that specific open question: Romania's own investigation confirmed a Russian-made Shahed, directly validating the Polish/Baltic hybrid-warfare warning from two weeks ago rather than leaving it ambiguous.

The details. The pace — three incursions in three days, against one the week before — indicates either a genuine escalation in Russian drone activity near Romanian and Black Sea airspace, or that Romania's newly demonstrated willingness and legal authority to intercept means previously unreported incursions are now being engaged and publicised rather than tolerated silently; this briefing cannot distinguish between the two from available reporting. NATO spokesperson Colonel Martin O'Donnell's "unacceptable and intolerable" language and the US ambassador to NATO's pledge to "defend every inch" of allied territory both signal the alliance is treating this as a pattern requiring visible collective response rather than an isolated incident. Last week's separate, unattributed explosion aboard the Ukraine-bound coal carrier Christiana B off Romania's coast remains uninvestigated as of this writing, with no update found this window.

Context. Romania's diplomatic response — summoning the ambassador and demanding an expulsion — is a step beyond the shootdowns themselves and marks the most concrete state-to-state confrontation over drone incursions so far in this war, a marker other frontline NATO states (Poland, the Baltics) may look to as precedent if incursions on their own territory continue.

Watch-Items. (1) Whether the named Russian diplomat actually departs within the five-day window, and Moscow's response. (2) Whether other NATO states report similar upticks in the same period. (3) Any resolution of the still-open Christiana B investigation. (4) Confirmed origin of the second and third drones, beyond the first.

Confidence: high (Romanian government and NATO on record; first drone's Russian origin independently confirmed by investigation)
lightbulbEditor's view

Three days, two resolved unknowns: a drone's origin, and what "bringing the war home" actually means

1. Ukraine diplomacy is now running on two separate, unconfirmed channels at once. Zelensky's Washington trip and the still-unconfirmed Witkoff/Kushner Moscow trip are two halves of the same push, but neither Kyiv nor Moscow has been in the same room together yet — and Zelensky's own preference for a July trilateral, against a fall timeline reportedly favoured by US officials, is a real gap worth tracking.

2. "Bringing the war home" is now Ukraine's own stated doctrine, not this briefing's inference. Zelensky's direct quote on the Wildberries campaign confirms deliberately targeting Russian civilian retail infrastructure is policy — and this week it reached Moscow's own airspace for the first time, landing on a retail sector Forbes reporting shows was already structurally weakened.

3. Iran's first quiet night in two weeks is real but thin. It comes with a precisely defined, unresolved sticking point — who controls new transit routes through Hormuz under the June MoU — rather than any sign the underlying dispute is close to resolution.

4. The Pentagon's casualty story didn't end with a correction — it evolved into a new bureaucratic category. The combined 18-killed, 624-wounded total is unchanged, but splitting it into a category that starts one day before the ceasefire's collapse keeps the War Powers Resolution question, and the credibility question, both open.

5. Romania resolved last week's central unknown and escalated its response. Confirming the first drone as a Russian Shahed — and following with two more shootdowns and an ambassador summons within three days — makes this the most concrete NATO-Russia friction point of the war so far, more concrete than it was a week ago.

6. Moscow's pre-election crackdown is the domestic mirror of the economic distress this briefing has tracked for weeks. Convicting Nadezhdin and arresting Remeslo — a former loyalist — reads as a Kremlin closing off dissent channels ahead of September rather than betting the economy improves before voters go to the polls.

Wildcard to watch into next week: whether the Witkoff/Kushner Moscow trip actually happens with a confirmed date and readout, and whether Romania's expulsion demand produces anything beyond Moscow's dismissal.
