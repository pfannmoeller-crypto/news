INSECT PROTEINEUROPE26 Oct 2026 · earlier than window

Finland's Volare finishes a 5,000-tonne insect protein plant for €15 million

Volare, a Finnish company, has completed its first industrial-scale factory in Pori, Finland. It uses black soldier fly larvae to turn food-industry leftovers into protein and oil, and is designed for up to 5,000 tonnes of protein and 2,000 tonnes of oil a year. Tech.eu reported on 26 October that the plant came in on schedule and within its €15 million construction budget, and that Volare raised a further €7.1 million.

Its protein is already used in pet food, fish feed and poultry feed, and a large part of the plant's output is reported as already sold.

Context & background.Insect protein was once expected to become a major pet food and fish feed ingredient, but several European producers have failed while trying to scale up. Tech.eu names the French company Ÿnsect, which raised more than €600 million before judicial liquidation in 2025; the Danish company Enorm, declared bankrupt on 30 October 2025; and the German company FarmInsect, which entered insolvency proceedings in 2026. Volare's plant is a counter-example so far, but it has only just been built and has not yet run at full output.

The details.The plant sits in a former newspaper printing house, which the company says saved at least a year of construction. It is designed to run with four people per shift and entirely on electricity. The company claims lower capital cost, about half the staff and 30% less energy than competitors; these are the company's own statements and were not independently checked. Customers named include Alvar Pet (dog treats), the Vafo Group brand Dagsmark (cat food) and the fish feed maker Skretting, which has an agreement to buy output. A second factory with roughly five times the protein capacity is planned, and the chief executive says the company is focused first on bringing the first plant up to capacity. Sources differ on when full output is reached; one earlier report said 2027.

Why it still matters.It adds a European supply source for a protein that competes with fishmeal and soy. Fishmeal supply is limited, and the company argues insect protein is a needed alternative. That argument is untested at scale: the plant's real running cost, and whether pet food makers will pay for it when fishmeal is cheap, are not public.

PACKAGINGEU RULES2 Nov 2026 · earlier than window

New European Union packaging law forces pet food pouches toward single-material designs

Petfood Industry reported on 2 November that the European Union's Packaging and Packaging Waste Regulation (PPWR) became applicable on 12 August 2026 and requires all packaging on the EU market to be recyclable by 2030. For pet food, that means replacing layered packs made of several materials with single-material ones.

Packaging suppliers quoted in the article say compliance costs are hard to estimate and depend on how much a brand must redesign and whether its filling lines can run the new material.

Context & background.Many wet pet food pouches are made of layers of polyester, aluminium and polypropylene. The layers keep out air and moisture and survive heating inside the pack to sterilise the food, but they are hard to recycle because the layers cannot easily be separated. The regulation pushes producers toward packs built from one plastic, usually polyethylene or polypropylene.

The details.The article, written mainly from interviews with packaging suppliers (Mondi, Coveris, Huhtamaki), says dry food bags can move to single-material structures more easily, while wet food pouches are technically harder because of the heat treatment and barrier needs. Smaller brands are expected to struggle more because they have fewer technical resources. All quotes come from companies that sell packaging, so their view of the effort involved is not neutral. The full article was behind a registration wall after one free view, and we read it once.

Why it still matters.It is a cost and design burden that falls on every pet food seller in the EU, and it favours larger groups and suppliers with ready-made recyclable packs. What is not public is the actual cost per brand, or whether recycling capacity in Europe will be ready by 2030; the article itself says it is still developing.
