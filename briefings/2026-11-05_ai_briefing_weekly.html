RESEARCH2 Nov
A model rewrote 0.016% of a small model's internals and its maths score rose from 8% to 48%

Researchers at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology's Computer Science and Artificial Intelligence Laboratory had OpenAI's Astra edit the stored numbers (the "weights") of a small open model, Qwen3.5-0.8B, directly instead of retraining it. On a standard maths test its accuracy went from 7.9% to 48.1%.

Most of the gain came from curing a fault: the small model kept repeating itself until it ran out of room. The authors call it "very early results", unreviewed and not independently replicated.

0.016%of weights changed (131,072 numbers)

At the table — Moonshots #308, 4 Nov

Wissner-Gross: "The machines will understand the machines." Blundin likened it to the first integrated transistor, but tiny; he cited the same 0.016% and 7.9% to 48.1% figures, which match the reporting. He stressed tests get maxed out quickly, so a jump on one maths test says little about general ability.

SourceslinkVentureBeatlinkAuthors' postlinkexplainx.ai
chevron_rightRead more
Context & background

Editing model weights directly is not new: methods from 2022 and 2023 changed single facts. What is new is a general-purpose model deciding what to change, by reading the weights as text or through a Python environment.

The details

The same team reports Astra identifying four game-playing networks (Breakout, Pong, Pac-Man, Freeway) from weights alone, patching a driving controller in one reply, and building a Breakout network from rules that out-scored one trained conventionally for 500 million steps (517 against 25). It failed to improve a different small model, Gemma 3 270M, which lacks the repetition fault, and could not spot a people-pleasing model from weights alone until given extra measurements. One outlet notes a small discrepancy in the edit count (130,188 against 131,072).

Why it still matters

The result is a repair, not new knowledge: the patch removed an obstacle. But a model that can debug another model is a step on the path to systems that improve themselves. A critic at the campaign group PauseAI called the direction reckless.

●●●○○ C-Score 3/5 · single source team; coverage relies on the authors' own logs; no replication yet

Four takeaways

  1. Self-reported milestones are becoming legal ones. Anthropic puts "over 90% of code by Claude" in a securities filing; Apple and Alphabet put in writing that agents can act beyond their control. Both are claims made under liability, which makes them more credible than a blog post and still not independent.
  2. Models are now being used to edit models. The Massachusetts Institute of Technology result is a repair, not new knowledge, and the authors say so. Whether it compounds is the open question.
  3. The money is tightening and the politics have turned. OpenAI cut its raise, lenders ask more, and Ohio voters restricted data centres 21 times. Alphabet still says 2027 spending will pass $300 billion, so something has to give.
  4. Thin non-AI week. Space, robotics and security each have one or two firm items. Several panel figures (Alphabet's fall, LifeVal, a $115 billion chip takeover) failed to check out and were left out.
