German Rents: The Evidence Behind the Debate

Two camps argue about why rents in German cities are high and what should be done about it. This piece checks sixteen of the claims each side makes, against Destatis, BBSR, ifo, DIW, IW Köln, RWI, empirica and the peer-reviewed international literature — including a number neither camp campaigns on.

The two cases, in brief

Two camps, not one author. Each side's strongest empirical argument is presented here at full strength, before any check runs below — no verdicts in this section.

The case for regulation

The strongest empirical case for keeping and extending rent regulation does not rest on rents simply feeling unfair — it rests on a specific, measurable gap. Melanie Weber-Moritz, president of the Deutscher Mieterbund (Germany's national tenants' association) since June 2025, Marcel Fratzscher, president of DIW Berlin, and Konstantin Kholodilin of DIW each point to the same underlying pattern: the spread between what sitting tenants pay and what new tenants are asked to pay. That spread means the market is pricing newcomers out of cities where the existing housing stock is not, in aggregate, scarce — it is simply held, disproportionately, by people who are not moving. The camp's own numbers are specific: on DIW's SOEP data the average rent burden has been roughly stable since about 2005, but it splits sharply by income — around 36% for the bottom income quintile against about 22% for the top, and about 30% for single-person households. Read from inside this camp, that is the case for treating rent as a distributional question first: the aggregate looks manageable precisely because the burden is concentrated where it is least affordable, which is exactly what an average conceals and what a protective instrument is meant to address.

Fratzscher's sharpest formulation goes further than "there is not enough housing." He argues the crisis is primarily a distribution problem, pointing to under-occupied large flats held by long-tenured, often older households who have every financial incentive to stay put, since moving would mean trading a cheap sitting-tenant rent for a market-rate new-letting rent. On this reading, adding new supply without addressing that lock-in leaves the underlying misallocation of existing space untouched, because the households best placed to free up space have the least reason to do so.

Kholodilin's contribution is methodological rather than rhetorical, and arguably the strongest single piece of evidence the regulation camp has: his own evaluation of the Mietpreisbremse (rent brake) — the kind of study that might be expected to embarrass a market-skeptical economist — found it measurably dampened rent increases in the units it covers. His own assessment, as extracted, is that the law is "better than its reputation." An economist checking his own policy's claims against data and finding they hold up better than its loudest critics assume is a different kind of argument than an advocacy claim, and deserves to be weighed as such before any rebuttal.

The case for supply

The strongest empirical case against a regulation-first policy — made by Oliver Falck, Simon Krause and Pascal Zamorski at ifo, Michael Voigtländer and Christian Oberst at IW Köln, Iris Schöberl of the ZIA (the commercial property industry association) and Reiner Braun of empirica — rests on the same spread Fratzscher cites, read in the opposite direction. If asking rents have risen roughly 50% nationally and roughly 75% in the Top-7 cities since 2013, while sitting-tenant rents rose only about 19% over a comparable period, the mechanism dividing insiders from outsiders is not primarily about who holds a large flat. It is that too little new housing has entered the market to keep pace with demand in the cities people actually want to live in, so every unit that does turn over gets bid up sharply.

The distributional version of this argument is its sharpest form: the bottom income quartile's rent burden at new lettings rose from roughly 38% of income in 2014 to roughly 50% in 2025. That is not a story about badly allocated existing stock — someone signing a new lease today is paying a fundamentally different price than someone who signed one a decade ago, regardless of how efficiently the existing stock happens to be used. And the supply side keeps deteriorating on the numbers this piece checks below: completions have been falling for years and only just started turning upward in permits, with the delivered units still years away. On this reading, treating the shortage primarily as a mis-allocation problem understates how much of the pressure is simply too few homes for too many households competing for the same handful of cities.

Diagnosis: what the numbers say

Completions fell to a 13-year low in 2025 2025

Destatis reports 206,600 completed dwellings in 2025, down 18.0% (−45,400) from 2024 — the lowest completion count since 2012. Multi-family completions fell to 109,800, down 18.9%. The regional split is uneven: East Germany fell 34.3%, West Germany 16.7%, so the East contracted roughly twice as fast. Both camps read this figure the same way: less new supply reaching a market that, on the asking-rent numbers below, is already tightening. The one thing that is not settled is the exact count: empirica's Wohnungsmarktbericht 2026 puts 2025 completions at roughly 190,000, about 16,600 below the Destatis figure. That gap is shown here, not resolved — the definitional difference between the two counts was not established in what was checked.

CheckTrue C 5/5 — Destatis PD26_174 confirms 206,600 completions in 2025 (−18.0%), the lowest since 2012, directly from the official release. empirica's independent estimate of ~190,000 is shown alongside and left unreconciled.

The size of the shortfall is beyond dispute; only its exact count depends on whose ledger you use.

The Bauüberhang (permit overhang): about 453,500 permitted homes are not yet started

At the end of 2025, the Bauüberhang — permitted dwellings not yet completed — stood at 760,700, of which 307,200 were under construction, leaving roughly 453,500 (59.6%) permitted and not started. A year earlier, end-2024, the split was 759,700 total, 330,000 under construction, 429,700 not started (56.6%) — so both the absolute overhang and the not-started share grew year over year. On the numbers alone, this is the single most load-bearing figure in this piece: it says the binding constraint sits somewhere past the permitting stage, not at it. The partition itself is contested: BBSR's own reporting of the 2025 split gives 51% not started against 49% "in some stage of construction," a materially different split from the one the Destatis press figures imply (59.6% not started against a 40.4% under-construction share). Neither figure is corrected against the other here — both are shown as published, because the underlying definitions of "started" were not established in this check.

CheckTrue C 4/5 — Destatis PD26_174 and PD25_183 corroborate the overhang totals across two years, confirming the growth in both the overhang and the not-started share. BBSR's 51/49 split and the Destatis-implied 59.6/40.4 split are shown side by side, unreconciled.

Whichever partition is used, the not-started share is large and rising, not falling — that direction survives both versions of the number.

Permits are turning up sharply in 2026 2026

H1/2026 permits reached 126,300, up 15.1% — the strongest increase since 2016. June 2026 alone recorded 21,600 permits, up 13.8%; multi-family permits for H1 reached 67,000, up 16.9%. Because permits precede completions by roughly two to three years in the German construction pipeline, this does not contradict the 2025 completions collapse above — it is a leading indicator pointing the other way, not a retraction of a lagging one.

CheckTrue C 5/5 — Destatis PD26_291 confirms all three figures directly from the official release.

A turn in permits is not yet a turn in delivered homes — the overhang above is exactly why that gap can persist for years.

The "400,000 homes a year" target no longer formally exists

400,000 annual completions was the Ampel coalition's 2021 target. The 2025 CDU/CSU–SPD coalition agreement sets no annual numeric target at all — it commits only to homes renting for under €15/m². The old target has been neither formally reconfirmed nor formally abandoned; it simply has no successor figure in the current coalition's own text. Any "percentage of target achieved" statistic is therefore arithmetic against a benchmark with no current legal or coalition standing. For orientation only, and labelled as such: measured against the old 400,000 figure, attainment ran 73.8% (2022), 73.6% (2023), 63.0% (2024) and 51.7% (2025). This piece's own first draft used the 400,000 figure as though it were the current government's live benchmark; the fact-check corrected that framing before publication, and the correction is recorded here rather than quietly fixed.

CheckMisleading C 3/5 — The 400,000 figure is real but describes the previous coalition's target, not the current one; treating it as a live benchmark, not the number itself, is the misleading move. Attainment percentages are given for orientation only.

A shortfall against a retired target says less about the current government's policy than the headline percentage implies.

The viability gap: new-build costs exceed what asking rents cover

Construction cost for multi-storey residential buildings has roughly doubled, from about €2,000/m² in 2010 to about €4,500/m² in 2024. Add land, financing and a developer's return, and full development cost runs close to €7,000/m², implying a cost-covering new-build rent of roughly €20–25/m² — against a Top-7 asking rent averaging €17.12/m² (see below). empirica's independent estimate of the West German cost-covering threshold, about €20/m², sits inside that same range. The construction price index corroborates the direction, if not the exact level: +69% cumulative from 2015 to 2025, though the current annual rate has cooled to +3.3%, meaning the gap is not obviously widening further from cost inflation alone right now.

CheckMostly true C 4/5 — ifo Schnelldienst 10/2025 (citing ARGE and Saucken et al. 2025) and empirica agree in order of magnitude on the €20–25/m² threshold; BKI and Destatis PD26_126 corroborate the underlying cost trend independently.

If the arithmetic holds, no rent-side rule changes the fact that many projects do not pencil out at today's asking rents.

Asking rents have pulled away from sitting-tenant rents since 2013

Asking rents rose 50% nationally (€7.08 to €10.68/m²) and 75% in the Top-7 cities (€9.71 to €17.12/m²) between 2013 and 2025; Berlin rose 108% (€7.97 to €16.56/m²), Munich 66% (€13.44 to €22.28/m²). Sitting-tenant rents, by contrast, rose only about 19% over 2013–2024. The resulting gap in 2022 was €2.10/m² nationally (+31%), €3.00/m² in the Top-50 cities (+35%) and €4.48/m² in the Top-7 (+48%) — the gap is largest exactly where demand is tightest. An independent cross-check shows both the size and the limits of this picture: BBSR/IDN ImmoDaten's 2025 figures give Germany €11.11/m² against Value AG's €10.68/m², and Munich €21.01/m² against €22.28/m² — a 4–6% level divergence between the two databases, though the trend direction is identical in both. The sitting-rent series itself rests on the official consumer price index rather than transaction-level data, which is a real limit on how precisely the gap can be measured.

CheckMostly true C 4/5 — Value AG and BBSR/IDN ImmoDaten agree on direction and rough magnitude, with a 4–6% level gap between the two sources shown side by side rather than resolved.

The insider/outsider structure of German rents is real regardless of which database is used; only the exact euro figures shift.

DIW and ifo do not actually contradict each other on rent burden

The two figures are frequently cited against each other, but they answer different questions from different data. DIW (Kholodilin and Baake, using SOEP panel data, 1990–2021) reports a roughly stable average rent burden since about 2005, with a sharply unequal distribution underneath it: the bottom income quintile pays around 36% of income in rent, the top quintile around 22%, and single-person households around 30%. ifo, drawing on Destatis's EVS 2023, reports an average of 27.8% and about 1.5 million households above the 50% burden threshold. These are close, not contradictory, averages from two different surveys — and DIW's actual point was never a different mean, it was always the distribution hiding underneath it. This piece's own working thesis initially framed the two figures as a contradiction; the fact-check corrected that framing before publication. DIW's own hedge on how much of the mild post-2015 decline in average burden the Mietpreisbremse can explain should travel with the figure rather than be dropped, as extracted: "cannot be answered on the basis of the data used here."

"[The extent to which the Mietpreisbremse drove the decline] cannot be answered on the basis of the data used here." — DIW Wochenbericht, as extracted
CheckMisleading C 4/5 — DIW Wochenbericht 41/2024 and the ifo/EVS 2023 figures were read directly; the averages are close, and the claimed contradiction does not survive comparing what each study actually measures.

The interesting number here was never the average — it was always the bottom quintile's 36%.

German housing supply is unusually unresponsive to price — and not because of geography

German housing supply elasticity is estimated at about 0.22 for floor space and 0.25 for units, meaning a 10% price rise brings only about 2.2% more floor space onto the market. That is materially below typical US metro estimates of roughly 0.3–0.5. The striking part is what does not explain the gap: unlike Saiz's US findings, where terrain and coastline predict inelastic supply, German average terrain slope is only 4.87% — topography cannot be doing the work here. That points the constraint toward planning and regulation rather than geography, which also means it is, in principle, reformable rather than fixed.

CheckTrue C 4/5 — RWI Ruhr Economic Papers 23/1003 (Bartik instrument, 2SLS, 2008–2019 panel) is the primary elasticity estimate; Saiz (QJE 2010) is the US benchmark and the source of the geography comparison.

Low elasticity for regulatory rather than geographic reasons is uncomfortable for both camps at once: it blunts price signals but does not excuse permitting delay.

Instruments: price regulation and demand subsidy

The Mietpreisbremse dampens regulated rents, but the spillover to exempt units is larger

Mense, Michelsen and Kholodilin (Journal of Urban Economics 134, 2023), using difference-in-differences and a regression-discontinuity-in-time design across Germany's staggered state introductions, find regulated units renting 1.4–3.3% lower while exempt and new-build units rise 2.7–3.8% higher — the spillover exceeds the dampening. The statutory evaluation DIW/DIW Econ ran for the Bundesministerium der Justiz (2019) finds a similar pattern: −2% to −3% regulated, +2% to +4% exempt. Beneficiaries skew toward middle- and higher-income tenants rather than the intended target group, and compliance is weak — 50–95% of listings still exceed the legally permitted rent, with the dampening effect persisting despite that. Building land prices in Bavaria rose 13–16% more in regulated municipalities than in unregulated ones. On mobility, higher earners move less and hold onto good regulated flats, while lower earners move more within the local market. Quality evidence is genuinely mixed rather than damning: one study finds more fitted kitchens in regulated units (+2.7 percentage points), while the statutory evaluation finds no significant renovation effect, and no negative effect on permit issuance was found for 2015–2016. The law was extended to 31 December 2029 by the Bundestag in June 2025.

CheckTrue C 5/5 — Mense/Michelsen/Kholodilin (JUE 2023) and the DIW/DIW Econ statutory evaluation (BMJV, 2019) are two independent quasi-experimental designs that agree on direction and rough magnitude.

Better than its reputation on compliance-adjusted dampening, and still a machine for shifting cost onto the newly exempt segment.

Berlin's Mietendeckel (rent cap): the cleanest natural experiment, and a contested 2026 sequel 2020–2026

Berlin's Mietendeckel (2020–21, struck down by the Federal Constitutional Court) is Germany's cleanest rent-cap natural experiment. ifo, using entropy balancing against 13 large German comparison cities, finds rent growth roughly 60 percentage points lower in the regulated segment during the cap, 5–9 percentage points higher in the unregulated segment over the same period, and purchase prices about 9 percentage points lower. DIW finds the supply of regulated listings fell by about half, and that the people hit hardest by the resulting harder search were low earners, young families and newcomers — the finding behind DIW president Fratzscher's own headline, as extracted, that the Mietendeckel "harms low earners." DIW is a publicly funded research institute rather than an industry-financed one, but its president is publicly identified with a distinct policy position on housing, and that is worth stating at the point of use with the same directness applied to IW Köln's employer-aligned funding at c16 — a finding from a non-neutral source is not thereby wrong, and in this case it cuts against the instrument DIW's own camp is associated with. A new ifo study, released 14 August 2026 and using 2.3 million rental and 1.1 million sale listings across 14 cities from 2017–2024 plus a newspaper-based policy-uncertainty index, finds Berlin asking rents now running 10–15% above the modelled counterfactual, attributed to investment uncertainty from the regulation-and-expropriation debate rather than to the cap's direct mechanics. That 2026 study is recent and has not yet been independently replicated.

"[The Mietendeckel] harms low earners." — Marcel Fratzscher, DIW, as extracted
CheckTrue C 5/5 (2026 follow-on: C 3/5) — ifo's entropy-balancing design and DIW's listing-volume analysis are independent and directionally consistent for 2020–21 (C 5/5). The August 2026 uncertainty-channel finding rests on one institute's single study to date (C 3/5) and is flagged as such.

The 2020–21 result is about as solid as German rent-policy evidence gets; the 2026 sequel is a live hypothesis, not yet a second data point.

Wohngeld (housing benefit) capitalisation in Germany: an unmeasured, consequential gap

No German study quantifying how much of a Wohngeld increase ends up captured by landlords in higher rents was found, despite a targeted search that included the 2023 "Wohngeld Plus" reform. That is a real gap, not a rounding error — Germany runs a demand-side subsidy without knowing its incidence. International estimates range roughly 40–80% and rise as local supply gets less elastic: Fack (Labour Economics, 2006, using a French natural experiment) finds €1 of benefit raises rent by 78 cents; Susin (Journal of Public Economics, 2002) finds vouchers raised rents about 16%, with a net loss of $2.4bn to low-income households as a group; Collinson and Ganong (AEJ: Policy, 2018) find blanket payment-standard increases are largely absorbed by landlords, while small-area indexing performs better; Eriksen and Ross find no broad level effect but localised increases where supply is inelastic. Given the German elasticity estimate above sits at the low end of the international range, theory predicts capitalisation toward the higher end for Germany — but that is an inference from a different country's elasticity, not a German measurement, and should be read as exactly that.

CheckUnverifiable C 4/5 (international) — Fack, Susin, Collinson/Ganong and Eriksen/Ross are four independent, peer-reviewed international estimates (C 4/5 collectively). No German figure exists to score at all.

An instrument this size should not be running on an untested incidence assumption, whichever direction that assumption points.

Instruments: supply, land, and the models people cite

New construction lowers nearby rents — mostly, and not evenly across price segments

Asquith, Mast and Reed (Review of Economics and Statistics 105(2)) find rents fall 5–7% within 250m of large new buildings in low-income areas, an effect holding for at least three years. Pennington, using building fires as an instrument for where construction ends up happening, finds −1.2% to −2.3% within 500m in San Francisco and a 17.1 percentage-point reduction in displacement risk. Li's New York difference-in-differences finds a 10% increase in local stock associated with roughly a 1% rent decrease. Mast's migration-chain work finds that 100 new market-rate units free up roughly 70 vacancy-equivalents in below-median-income neighbourhoods within five years. The counter-finding deserves equal billing, not a footnote: Damiano and Frenier (Journal of Urban Affairs, 2026, Minneapolis, difference-in-differences) find high-price-segment rents fall about 2% near new construction but low-price-segment rents rise about 4.5%, with no significant effect on the market overall — an amenity or upgrading channel that reverses the filtering story exactly where it is meant to help most. Even Mast's own chain-migration estimates weaken in the cheapest neighbourhoods. All of this evidence is American: transferring it to Germany means transferring it across a Mietpreisbremse the US markets studied do not have, and across permitting timelines that move considerably faster than German ones.

CheckTrue C 5/5 — Asquith/Mast/Reed, Pennington and Li are three independent quasi-experimental US studies pointing the same direction; Damiano/Frenier is an equally credible, independently published counter-finding shown at equal weight, not subordinated.

"Build and it filters down" holds in the studies that find it, and does not hold in at least one that looked specifically at the cheap end — both facts belong in the same sentence.

Auckland's upzoning result is genuinely contested, not settled

Greenaway-McGrevy (Economic Inquiry, 2025), using synthetic control against 51 untreated New Zealand towns, finds three-bedroom rents 22–35% below the synthetic control six years after Auckland's 2016 upzoning reform. A companion paper, Greenaway-McGrevy and Phillips (Journal of Urban Economics, 2023, difference-in-differences), finds about 21,808 additional building permits over five years attributable to the reform. The rebuttal is serious and comes from a reputable venue, not a fringe source: Cameron Murray, in a Motu re-analysis published in Land Use Policy (2025), argues that excluding Special Housing Area permits manufactures an artificial 2016 structural break in the data, that using a linear rather than a cyclical trend extrapolation inflates the permit estimate — cutting it from roughly 22,000 to roughly 4,500 — that Wellington, which did not upzone, shows similar underlying trends anyway, and that demolition substitution means net dwelling growth lags permit growth by more than the headline numbers suggest. The rebuttal is itself recent and has not been independently replicated either — the same caveat attached to the 2026 ifo finding at c10, applied here so that the freshness discount does not land on only one camp's inconvenient study.

CheckOpen C 4/5 — Both the original and rebuttal analyses are peer-reviewed or peer-adjacent and use defensible but different specifications; this is an open methodological dispute, not a case where one side has been shown wrong.

Auckland is cited constantly as a settled pro-upzoning result; on the evidence assembled here it is not settled.

Vienna is a century of accumulated stock and land, not a switch Germany could flip

About 60% of Viennese households live in municipal or limited-profit housing — roughly 220,000 Gemeindewohnungen (municipal flats) plus about 200,000 further subsidised units — built from 1923 onward, financed originally by the Breitner tax, expanded again after 1952, and sitting on land acquired at prices no German city could now reproduce. Current financing keeps the system running rather than building it from scratch: the Wohnbauförderungsbeitrag (housing-subsidy levy) rises from 1% to 1.5% of gross wages on 1 January 2026, worth about €190m a year to the city, and land for subsidised construction is allocated at €240–300/m². The uncomfortable part belongs in the same account: Wiener Wohnen effectively stopped building new municipal housing in 2004; Vienna's building approvals fell from 21,400 in 2019 to 5,772 in 2025, and privately financed new rental units halved in 2025 to 2,087. Access to the system creates its own insider/outsider structure — two years' residence required, income limits tested only at entry and never again. This block rests on the weakest evidence base in the piece: the main German-language assessment, commissioned by the property-industry association BID and carried out by empirica/Reiner Braun, calls the Vienna system, as extracted, "expensive, insecure, litigious, bureaucratic, opaque and unfair," while the main advocate account, by Justin Kadi, is published through a welfare association. Both are named here and both are labelled as interested parties, not neutral arbiters.

"Expensive, insecure, litigious, bureaucratic, opaque and unfair." — empirica/BID assessment of Vienna's system, as extracted
CheckMostly true C 3/5 — The stock and financing figures are corroborated across multiple official Vienna sources; the two evaluative accounts (BID/empirica and Kadi) are each single-source and each interested, which is why this claim scores lower than the diagnosis claims despite solid underlying numbers.

Vienna shows what a hundred years of land banking buys, not what a policy switch would buy in five.

Land value tax: the theory is strong, the measurement is thin

The cleanest natural experiment found is Denmark's 2007 municipal reform, which merged 237 municipalities into 65 and harmonised tax rates, covering 211,209 property transactions. It shows full capitalisation of future tax liability into prices at the moment of announcement, borne by the owner at that point, with later buyers effectively compensated through a lower purchase price. That is a real and useful efficiency result about who bears the tax — but it is not evidence that rents fall, because it says nothing about rental markets directly. Pennsylvania's split-rate experience offers less than is usually claimed: only about 20 municipalities ever adopted split-rate taxation, and the land-value analysis available rests on 7 municipalities over 2012–2018, a period in which none of them newly adopted the policy — meaning there is no counterfactual within the data, a limitation the authors themselves acknowledge. Estonia and Australia, both frequently cited as land-value-tax examples, yielded no identification-grade study in this search.

CheckOpen C 4/5 (Denmark) / C 3/5 (Pennsylvania) — The Danish reform is a strong natural experiment on capitalisation (C 4/5); the Pennsylvania land-value literature has no true counterfactual period in the data used (C 3/5); Estonia and Australia return no score at all.

Land value tax's efficiency case rests on real evidence; its rent-reduction case, in this record, rests mostly on theory.

Social housing: the thinnest evidence base of all sixteen claims

No clean German causal study was found for the fiscal cost per social unit per year, for crowding-out of private construction, or for a head-to-head comparison of object subsidy (subsidising the building) against subject subsidy (Wohngeld, subsidising the tenant). What exists is descriptive or openly interested rather than causal: the Sachverständigenrat's (German Council of Economic Experts) 2024/25 chapter describes the sector and makes recommendations without causal identification, and a 2016 IW Köln analysis — cross-sectional, employer-aligned in its funding — finds only 46% of social-housing households were "genuinely in need" by its own criteria. The stock figures themselves are on firmer ground: about 1,072,266 units remained under binding rent limits at end-2023; in 2025, 27,283 units entered the binding stock while 57,621 left it, a net loss of 30,338; the Sachverständigenrat expects roughly 40,000 units a year to fall out of binding through 2035 against a historical build rate of around 20,000 a year; only 4.5% of 2023 completions were publicly subsidised at all; total spending runs at roughly 0.2% of GDP.

CheckOpen C 3/5 — The stock and flow figures rest on official reporting (higher confidence); the cost-effectiveness and crowding-out questions have no causal German study to score at all, which is the point of this claim.

Germany can say precisely how many social units it is losing and almost nothing about what building more of them would actually cost or displace.

Stepping back

The error direction in this run. Of the sixteen claims checked here, the corrections did not run in one camp's favour. Two corrections landed on this piece's own framing before any source was ever cited against either camp: c4, where the 400,000-homes target was initially treated as a live government benchmark when it is actually a retired one, and c7, where DIW and ifo were initially framed as contradicting each other on rent burden when their averages are, in fact, close. A widely cited pro-supply number turned out to be wrong in the opposite direction from what its critics usually assume: Hsieh and Moretti's famous estimate that US land-use restrictions cost 8.9% of GDP (3.7% under a lower-bound model) was corrected upward, not downward, to roughly 36% and 14% respectively, after Bryan Caplan identified a compounding error in 2023 that the authors themselves confirmed — so anyone still citing "8.9%" today is citing the superseded figure, not an inflated one. And one instrument the regulation camp rarely puts under scrutiny produced a clean null: Vancouver's empty-homes tax cut the number of vacant units by about 21%, but had no significant effect on average rents and none on new construction. One qualification on this callout's own claim of balance, since it would be easy to overstate: of these four items, two are genuinely undirected corrections to this piece's own framing, but the other two — the upward Hsieh-Moretti revision and the Vancouver null — both happen to cut the same way, toward the supply camp's reading. No fact surfaced in this run happened to strengthen a regulation-camp number or null a supply-camp instrument with comparable force. That may be what the evidence looks like, or it may be what this particular set of sixteen questions was able to see. Sixteen claims checked in one piece is not a base rate for either camp; it is a record of what this particular run found.

Where it is contested

Other views Kholodilin sits on both sides of his own evidence: he co-authored the evaluation that found the Mietpreisbremse "better than its reputation" (see c9), and he also co-authored, with Sebastian Kohl, a 27-country, roughly 100-year panel study showing that strict rent regulation drives landlord exit, market segmentation and a higher rate of owner-occupancy over time. His own summary of the tension, as extracted: "the measures that are supposed to protect tenants also mean that there are fewer and fewer of them." The supply camp concedes the filtering mechanism has a floor: Bernhard Faller (Quaestio) and Roland Busch (IRI), writing from inside the supply-aligned camp, put it plainly, as extracted: "the filtering effect often only works up to the middle segment at most" — and empirica, itself broadly supply-aligned, documented the same limits in its own 2020 Sickereffekte (filtering-effects) meta-study. Fratzscher's distribution framing in Section 1 is itself a concession made from inside the regulation camp: it undercuts the pure quantity story that "just build more" solves the problem on its own. The Sachverständigenrat leaves the regulation-versus-supply dichotomy behind altogether when it comes to land policy, recommending Grundsteuer C (a higher property-tax rate specifically on undeveloped building-ready plots), stronger weighting of plot area generally within the property tax, "Gebäudetyp E" (a simplified building standard meant to cut construction cost), and harmonising the sixteen state-level Landesbauordnungen (building codes), which currently differ enough to add cost and delay on their own. There is also an institutional split worth naming on its own: the Sachverständigenrat explicitly advised against extending the Mietpreisbremse beyond 2028, and the legislature extended it to the end of 2029 anyway. And the Bundesbank takes no position on rent regulation at all — its public statements speak only to valuation, where it estimates overvaluation in German residential property has fallen from a range of 25–40% in 2022 to under 15% now — and should not be read into either camp's column.

The messenger

This is a landscape piece with no single author, so what matters is where each figure comes from rather than any one person's track record. The backbone numbers are official statistics: Destatis press releases (completions, permits, the Bauüberhang) and BBSR data. Two institute papers were read directly by the compiler rather than taken from a secondary summary: DIW Wochenbericht 41/2024 and ifo Schnelldienst 10/2025. Peer-reviewed international literature — Journal of Urban Economics, Review of Economics and Statistics, Journal of Public Economics, Labour Economics, AEJ: Policy, Economic Inquiry, Journal of Urban Affairs, Land Use Policy — underpins the instrument-level claims c9–c15. Interested parties appear on both sides and are labelled as such wherever they are cited: the Deutscher Mieterbund and DIW on the regulation side; ZIA, Haus & Grund, IW Köln, the BID-commissioned empirica Vienna study and the GdW (the federal association of housing companies) on the supply side.

What could not be substantiated

  • No German study quantifying Wohngeld capitalisation exists, despite a targeted search (c11).
  • No clean German causal study on social-housing cost-effectiveness or crowding-out of private construction (c16).

What survives

  • The permit overhang and the viability gap as the binding constraint on delivery (c2, c5).
  • The asking/sitting-rent spread as a real insider-outsider structure (c6).
  • The Mietpreisbremse's spillover onto exempt units exceeding its dampening effect on regulated ones (c9).
  • German supply elasticity being low for regulatory rather than geographic reasons (c8).
  • New construction lowering nearby rents, at least in the high-price segment (c12).

What to discount

  • Against the regulation camp: price regulation as an answer to a viability gap it does not close (c5, c9); the Mietendeckel as a template, given the roughly 50% drop in regulated listings it produced (c10); the Vienna model as a switch Germany could flip, rather than a century of accumulated stock and land it cannot easily reproduce (c14).
  • Against the supply camp: "build and it filters down" as a general claim, given the Damiano/Frenier counter-finding and the filtering limit empirica itself documented (c12, and "Where it is contested"); permits as the binding constraint, given roughly 453,500 permitted-and-not-started homes already in the pipeline (c2); Auckland as a settled result — it is an open methodological dispute (c13).
  • Against both: the 400,000-homes target as a current benchmark; it describes a retired coalition's goal, not this one's (c4). Wohngeld as an instrument with a known incidence; no German capitalisation estimate exists (c11).
