xAI settles with Apple as Starship's next launch slips again, to 22 September

This is a normal seven-day edition, 13–19 September. The clearest movement this week sits in court and mission planning rather than in any product launch. On 14 September, X Corp and xAI dismissed their antitrust claim (a lawsuit alleging anti-competitive conduct) against Apple with prejudice — meaning that specific claim cannot be refiled — even as their parallel claim against OpenAI's corporate entities keeps moving toward a January 2027 trial. Separately, Elon Musk said on social media that Starship's next flight is now set for 22 September, a further slip from the 18–19 September window an aviation-planning filing had suggested, and one a SpaceX mission description has since backed. Grok 4.8 entered its final stage of training this week, though Musk's own description of it — "a noticeable improvement" — is markedly more modest than the promise once attached to its predecessor, and xAI's own release notes still show no model newer than August's Grok 4.6. Tesla's vehicle-safety audit into the Cybercab and Musk's political spending both sat exactly where the previous edition left them, with nothing new to report. Neuralink stayed silent for a fourth consecutive edition.

Grok 4.8 enters its final training stage, but Musk's own claim for it keeps shrinking

Musk said on 14 September that the 2.5-trillion-parameter (parameters are the adjustable numbers inside a model; more usually means more capable and costlier to run) Grok 4.8 would finish training that week and move into reinforcement learning (RL) — a further stage of training that adjusts a model's behaviour after its initial run is complete. He described 4.8 only as bringing "a noticeable improvement" over Grok 4.7, the model whose own "3–4 weeks, surpass every current model" promise had already expired without a release in early September. On the same day, one account had Musk describing 4.7 itself as merely "roughly on par with Opus 5.0, not 5.1 — better in some ways, worse in others" (Opus is Anthropic's rival model line), referring to Anthropic's Claude Opus model — a further retreat from the original claim.

Context & background. This continues the thread the previous two editions have tracked: a documented pattern of Musk announcing a release window for Grok 4.7 — "4 weeks" on 24 July, "3–4 weeks" again on 12 August, "10 days" on 2 September — that each passed without a release, followed on 13 September by Musk naming Grok 4.8 as the next model rather than shipping 4.7 at all. This week's development is Musk giving 4.8 an actual training timeline for the first time, alongside his own roadmap through a future Grok 5.

The details. Musk described the sequence as Grok 4.8 (a "noticeable improvement," finishing training and starting reinforcement learning this week), then Grok 4.9 (which he placed in an internal "Astra/Fable" performance class, without defining that class publicly), then Grok 5, which he called potentially "better than anything" and, when asked directly whether 4.8 approaches artificial general intelligence (AGI) — a system with human-like reasoning across most tasks — replied simply "that will be Grok 5." As of 17 September, xAI's own developer release notes still list Grok 4.6 (12 August) as the newest model; the only dated entry since is a voice-transcription tool, not a new flagship model.

Why it still matters. A training-stage update is not a release, and the roadmap Musk described pushes the "beats everything" claim two models further out than where this briefing first found it attached to Grok 4.7. The 4.7-versus-Opus comparison remains sourced to a single account of Musk's own words, not to any xAI benchmark publication.

Watch items. Whether Grok 4.8 actually reaches a public release, under that name or another, and whether xAI's release notes add an entry beyond the 17 September voice tool before the next edition closes.

xAI closes its three-day Grok Bot conference by shipping three new agent products

xAI's Grok Bot Galaxy conference ran 15–17 September at The Howard in San Francisco, pairing a different professional audience with each day — engineering, product management and founders first; sales and support second; marketing and post-sales last — alongside a livestream and a running three-day demonstration in which xAI staff built a working company using its own Grok Bot agents as the workforce. On the final day, xAI introduced three enterprise products: a speech-to-speech interface for agents priced to undercut established voice-AI vendors, a no-code tool for configuring voice agents without an engineer, and a connector that lets an agent operate a company's own internal software. xAI also highlighted an internal procurement agent it says has already saved the company more than $100,000 by tracking vendor spending.

Starship's next flight slips a third time, from 18 September to 22 September

The 18 September target the previous edition reported — itself already a slip from an earlier, satellite-filing-sourced 15 September date — passed without a launch. Musk confirmed on social media that the flight is now set for 22 September, a date a SpaceX mission description, reported the same week, backs. Flight 14 is intended as Starship's first attempt at reaching a genuine Earth orbit rather than the suborbital arcs flown to date: it would circle the planet roughly six times at about 275 kilometers before a Pacific Ocean splashdown near Chile, carrying around 20 newer, higher-capacity Starlink (SpaceX's satellite-internet network) satellites that would be put into active service for the first time on this flight.

Context & background. This is the third dated slip this briefing has tracked for this specific flight: a satellite-deployment filing's 15 September date, then an aviation-planning entry's 18–19 September window, and now a company-described 22 September target. Each prior date passed without a launch.

The details. Unlike Starship's thirteen previous test flights, which flew suborbital arcs that came back down within roughly an hour, Flight 14 is built to insert the upper stage into a real orbit for around ten hours across six passes around Earth, testing the heat shield and re-entry systems under a full orbital return rather than a shorter arc. Booster 21 and Ship 41, the flight's hardware, completed their final qualification tests on 21 and 28 August respectively, already reported in an earlier edition; no new hardware test data emerged this window.

Why it still matters. An actual orbital insertion, rather than a suborbital arc, is the technical milestone SpaceX has been building toward across this entire flight-test campaign; a fourth slip past 22 September would extend a pattern this briefing has now tracked across three consecutive editions.

Watch items. Whether the flight launches near 22 September and whether it reaches its planned orbit, given the added difficulty of a full re-entry over a short arc's return.

Musk's X and xAI drop their Apple antitrust claim (a lawsuit alleging anti-competitive conduct), but keep fighting OpenAI

X Corp and xAI filed a joint motion in a Texas federal court on 14 September to dismiss all of their antitrust claims against Apple, with prejudice — meaning those specific claims cannot be refiled — after telling the court the two sides "have resolved their claims." Neither side disclosed terms, and Apple did not oppose the dismissal. The underlying case, filed last year, had alleged Apple struck an exclusive deal making a rival chatbot the only one built into the iPhone, then pushed Musk's own X and Grok apps down its app-store rankings. Claims against OpenAI's corporate entities were specifically excluded from the dismissal and continue, with a trial already scheduled for 11 January 2027.

Context & background. X Corp and xAI sued Apple and OpenAI together, arguing that a 2024 agreement making one chatbot the iPhone's only built-in AI assistant, combined with App Store treatment that disadvantaged X and Grok, amounted to anticompetitive conduct favoring one AI provider over its rivals.

The details. The dismissal covers only Apple; the filing specifically preserves every claim against OpenAI's corporate entities, which remain defendants with a trial already scheduled for January 2027. Neither company's public statement disclosed whether money changed hands or whether Apple made any change to how it treats competing AI apps on the iPhone going forward.

Why it still matters. Musk is now pursuing a narrower legal fight than the one he opened: dropping the claim against the platform that could have forced App Store changes benefiting every AI competitor, while continuing to press only the claim against a direct model rival. Separately, and without movement this window, his own suit against OpenAI's Sam Altman remains dormant on a different appeal, to the Ninth Circuit, following his May 2026 trial loss.

edit_noteEditor's view

A quieter week that mostly resolved threads already open, rather than opening new ones

1. Legal fronts narrowed rather than widened. Dropping the claim against Apple, with prejudice, while keeping the OpenAI claim alive toward a January 2027 trial, and leaving both the Minnesota appeal and the Altman suit dormant this week, is the closest thing to a pattern in this edition: Musk narrowed which court fight he is actively pushing, rather than settling all of them at once.

2. Grok 4.8's ambitions kept shrinking even as its training moved forward. Musk's own language went from "surpass every current model" for Grok 4.7 in July, to "a noticeable improvement" for Grok 4.8 in September — a steadily more modest claim attached to a steadily later date, one xAI's own release notes have not yet backed with an actual release.

3. Two measures of xAI's momentum keep diverging. Its next flagship model remains unshipped, with its own documentation showing no movement beyond a voice tool, while its agent product, Grok Bot, shipped three new features at a dedicated conference this week — the split this briefing first named last edition widened rather than resolved.

4. Starship's slip is now company-sourced, not just regulator-sourced. The move from an aviation-planning entry to a date Musk confirmed himself, and a SpaceX mission description backs, makes 22 September a firmer target than 18 September ever was — firm enough to be worth watching rather than assumed to hold, given this flight's history of slipping.

5. Two threads sat exactly still. Tesla's Cybercab audit and Musk's political spending both carried over unchanged from the previous edition, with no findings, no new figures and no timeline attached to either.

6. A genuinely quiet company stayed quiet, for a fourth time. Neuralink produced no new dated development this window either — stated here plainly, as instructed, rather than padded into a card that would not hold up.

The next edition should carry: whether Starship actually flies near 22 September and reaches its planned orbit; any findings from the safety regulator's Cybercab audit; whether the 8th Circuit rules on xAI's injunction bid; and whether Grok 4.8 ships under that name, or slips the way 4.7 did.
