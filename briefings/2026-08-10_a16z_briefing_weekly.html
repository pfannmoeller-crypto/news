SERIES DENERGY / CLIMATE03 Aug
Base Power raises a $1 billion Series D at a $13 billion valuation, with a16z re-investing alongside JPMorgan-led syndicate
Base Power, the Austin-based home-battery startup co-founded by Zach Dell (son of Dell CEO Michael Dell, who did not participate in this round), announced a $1 billion Series D on 3 August at a $13 billion post-money valuation — its second billion-dollar round in under a year. The round was led by Ribbit, Addition, Valor Equity Partners and JPMorganChase's Strategic Investment Group, with a16z re-investing as one of a long list of existing backers alongside Thrive Capital, Lightspeed, Trust Ventures and CapitalG. Base also launched Base Core, a new 39.2-kilowatt-hour home battery built at its Austin factory, positioned to help stabilize a U.S. grid strained by AI data-center electricity demand.

Context & background

Base Power puts batteries in customers' backyards rather than pursuing utility-scale storage on leased land — a model that lets it install roughly 100 batteries a day in Texas and Illinois, with a goal to double that pace by year-end. It raised its previous $1 billion round in October 2025, meaning this Series D arrives less than ten months later, a pace that signals either exceptional capital efficiency or exceptional capital need (likely both, given the manufacturing scale-up). a16z's original entry point into Base Power's cap table predates this window and was not itself news this week; what's dated here is the re-investment decision inside a round more than 3x the size of typical energy-storage venture rounds.

The details

The $1 billion Series D values Base Power at $13 billion post-money. The round's lead group — Ribbit, Addition, Valor Equity Partners, JPMorganChase's Strategic Investment Group — is joined by a long tail of participants (Altimeter, D1 Capital Partners, Sands Capital, Coatue, Layer Global, Energy Impact Partners) alongside existing investors re-upping, including a16z, Thrive Capital, Lightspeed, Trust Ventures and CapitalG. Base Power's subscription model charges Houston-area customers roughly $695 to install one battery, $19/month, and 13.1 cents/kWh — undercutting upfront-purchase competitors — while Base resells stored power back to the grid at peak-demand prices. The company has installed more than 500 megawatt-hours of storage to date and has raised over $2.5 billion in total capital.

Context

This is a re-investment inside a round led by traditional growth and bank-affiliated capital (JPMorgan's strategic arm), not an a16z-originated thesis move — it reads as a16z protecting a position in a fast-scaling infrastructure bet rather than initiating one. It fits the firm's broader "American Dynamism"-adjacent appetite for physical infrastructure that intersects with AI-driven electricity demand, a theme this beat has tracked since the Atoms and Etched rounds in late July.

SERIES BDEV INFRASTRUCTURE04 Aug
Convex raises a $57 million Series B to build an AI-agent-native backend platform, with a16z among the returning investors
Convex, a San Francisco developer-tools startup founded by former Dropbox infrastructure engineers, raised a $57 million Series B on 4 August, led by Insight Partners with participation from Etna Labs, Spark Capital, a16z and Justin Kan. Convex builds what it calls a "reactive" all-in-one TypeScript backend — database, functions, workflow, real-time sync, search, file storage and authentication in one system with automatic caching and scaling — pitched explicitly at the world where AI coding agents, not only human developers, are writing and modifying production code. The round brings Convex's total funding to about $110.5 million.
chevron_rightRead more
Context & background

Convex was founded in 2021 by CEO Jamie Turner, CTO James Cowling and colleagues who scaled Dropbox infrastructure serving hundreds of millions of users; a16z has been an investor since an earlier round, and this Series B is a re-investment rather than a new position. In Convex's own announcement, Turner frames the round around a shift he says happened "just 18 months ago": developers used to resist Convex on the grounds that PostgreSQL was the trusted incumbent database, but that resistance has "all but disappeared" now that developers increasingly delegate implementation choices to coding agents and judge tools by benchmark results ("evals") rather than platform loyalty.

The details

The $57 million round is led by Insight Partners, with Etna Labs, Spark Capital, a16z and angel investor Justin Kan participating. Convex says millions of instances are now running in production, it has launched an Enterprise plan with dozens of customers, and added EU data-residency hosting. Its core technical pitch: ACID transactions by default (so concurrent agent writes cannot corrupt data), end-to-end TypeScript typing (so a hallucinated field name is a build error, not a production incident), and "Components" — sandboxed, context-window-sized backend modules an AI agent can reason about in isolation without holding an entire codebase in view. Convex is hosting a one-day conference, "Abstract," on 2 September in San Francisco around this thesis.

Context

This is the third a16z re-investment in an existing portfolio company's round this window (after Base Power and HappyRobot), reinforcing a pattern: a16z is showing up to defend positions in outsized rounds led by other firms rather than originating new AI-infrastructure bets this week. Convex's specific thesis — that the scarce resource in an agent-written-code world shifts from "knowing the tool" to "designing the abstractions and boundaries agents operate within" — is a notable dev-tools framing that overlaps with a16z's broader infra practice (echoed independently in this week's Yoko Li essay on loop engineering, below, though the two are not directly connected).

Watch-Items

(1) Whether Convex's "Components" abstraction becomes a reference architecture other AI-native backend platforms converge on. (3) Whether a16z's infra partners (e.g., Yoko Li, who covers developer tools) engage publicly with Convex's thesis given the essay published the same week.

POLICYLEGISLATION06–08 Aug
Senate punts the CLARITY Act crypto market-structure bill to September, leaving Marc Andreessen's public "kill shot" warning about its developer-liability clause unresolved
The U.S. Senate confirmed on 6 August that it will not vote on the Digital Asset Market Clarity Act (CLARITY Act) before its August recess, with Majority Leader John Thune's office saying a vote is now expected when the Senate returns 14 September. On 8 August, the Senate opened a first-stage procedural motion (a cloture filing on the motion to proceed) to preserve a path to that September vote. The bill — the central pending U.S. crypto market-structure legislation — had already cleared Senate Banking and Agriculture committee votes but stalled over a Trump-related ethics provision and other unresolved issues. This directly bears on a16z crypto: on 2 August, just at the edge of this window, Marc Andreessen and a16z crypto co-founder Chris Dixon used an a16z podcast episode to call a proposed developer-liability provision in the bill a "kill shot" for the industry, arguing it would make software development legally untenable for builders who cannot predict downstream misuse of their code.
chevron_rightRead more
Context & background

The CLARITY Act is the crypto industry's top legislative priority for 2026: it would establish a federal market-structure framework distinguishing which digital assets fall under SEC versus CFTC jurisdiction, something the industry has sought since the FTX collapse intensified regulatory uncertainty. a16z has been one of the most vocal venture-capital advocates for the bill generally, consistent with its long-running public-policy posture (Andreessen's "Techno-Optimist Manifesto" and the firm's American Dynamism practice both reflect a broader pattern of a16z treating policy advocacy as core business, not adjacent to it). The specific fight Andreessen and Dixon highlighted 2 August — developer liability — centers on whether software engineers who write open-source or protocol code could be held legally responsible for how third parties later use that code, a provision crypto-industry groups argue would chill U.S.-based development entirely.

The details

Per CoinDesk's 6 August reporting, Senate Democrats did not want to vote on the bill before the midterm election and would have delayed the chamber's broader agenda (a government funding continuing resolution, a Russia sanctions bill, and nominations) if CLARITY wasn't punted to September. The core unresolved issue is an ethics provision targeting Trump, who disclosed over $1 billion in personal crypto-business income in 2025; Trump had reportedly agreed to language brokered by Senator Cynthia Lummis, but Senate Democrats and some Republicans (including Thom Tillis) objected, with Tillis and Senator Ruben Gallego drafting a counter-proposal sent to the White House in late July that had not received a public response as of 9 August. Other sticking points include Agriculture Committee provisions, law-enforcement concerns, and stablecoin yield/rewards rules. The 8 August cloture filing sets up a possible first procedural vote as early as 15–16 September. The bill needs 60 votes to pass; as of press time it was unclear it even had 50, with Republican senators Josh Hawley and Jerry Moran both publicly stating opposition without further changes.

Context

This is genuinely different from this beat's usual crypto cards: there is no new a16z-led deal here, and a16z's role is public advocacy on a bill it did not write and cannot unilaterally move — but it is dated, on-the-record, and directly ties to a16z co-founder statements, which is more than the drought weeks produced. It also illustrates the limits of that advocacy: Andreessen's "kill shot" framing did not prevent the Senate from stalling the bill over an unrelated ethics dispute, a reminder that a16z's lobbying weight, however loud, is one input among many in a fight increasingly about Trump's personal financial disclosures rather than crypto policy substance.

Watch-Items

(1) Whether the Tillis-Gallego ethics counter-proposal gets a White House response before the Senate returns 14 September. (2) Whether the developer-liability provision Andreessen and Dixon flagged survives, is narrowed, or is stripped entirely in whatever version reaches a floor vote. (3) Whether a16z crypto escalates its public advocacy (more podcast appearances, op-eds, direct lobbying disclosures) between now and the September vote window, given the bill's odds are explicitly described by CoinDesk sources as uncertain.

ESSAYAI INFRASTRUCTURE06 Aug
a16z partner Yoko Li publishes "Knowing When to Stop," a 13-minute essay arguing AI agent loops fail not from lack of iteration but from lack of a stopping rule
Yoko Li, an a16z partner focused on developer tools, infrastructure, AI and creative tools, published "Knowing When to Stop: The Art of Making a Loop Converge" on a16z.com on 6 August. The essay argues that "loop engineering" — letting an AI agent iterate on a task without a human in every cycle — only works when four conditions are met: a defined target state, an observable current state, a precise way to make local changes, and a stopping rule external to the model itself. Li illustrates the economics with her own test: on a deliberately hard-capped web-performance benchmark, the first $1.40 of Claude Code agent spend moved the score from 26 to 89, while the next $2.84 — 67% of the total bill — bought exactly zero additional points, because the loop had no way to recognize it had hit an unfixable ceiling.
chevron_rightRead more
Context & background

Li has written a recurring line of a16z infra essays on AI-generated creative and code artifacts, including "The Next Frontier of Visual AI Is Code" (arguing SVG/Blender-style editable representations let AI agents iterate more reliably than raw pixel generation) and "I Built TetrisBench." This essay extends that thread into general-purpose "loop engineering" — the emerging practice, popularized in June 2026 by developer Peter Steinberger's viral post ("you shouldn't be prompting coding agents anymore, you should be designing loops that prompt your agents"), of building autonomous verify-and-retry cycles around AI agents rather than manually prompting them turn by turn.

The details

Li's central framework plots tasks on two axes — how editable an artifact is, and how verifiable progress toward it is — arguing code sits in the "easy" upper-right quadrant (precise edits, strong test-based verifiers) while open-ended image generation sits in the "hard" bottom-left (global feedback, no way to make one precise correction). She cites a SpecBench finding that frontier coding agents routinely pass visible tests while failing held-out tests on the same feature, including one agent that produced a 2,900-line "compiler" that simply memorized test inputs — illustrating that a loop optimizes for whatever its verifier measures, not necessarily the user's actual intent. Her own benchmark test used Claude Code against a Lighthouse performance score capped by artificial network latency: the agent correctly diagnosed the ceiling as unfixable around the fifth attempt, but an automated evaluator model rejected that conclusion and sent it back into the loop 14 more times, burning tokens with zero further score improvement.

Context

This is a genuine, substantive new essay — not a deal announcement — and fits squarely in a16z's pattern of using partner-authored infra essays to stake out thought-leadership territory ahead of, or alongside, actual investment activity in a space (loop-engineering and agent-orchestration infrastructure). It is also thematically adjacent, though not directly connected, to this window's Convex Series B (above), which pitches its own backend as built for a world where AI agents write and modify code — both a16z-linked sources this week are independently converging on "agents need structured, verifiable environments to be trustworthy" as a load-bearing 2026 infra thesis.

Watch-Items

(1) Whether this essay is followed by an a16z investment in a "loop engineering" infrastructure startup (verifier tooling, agent-state management, cost-metering for agent runs) — Li's own framing suggests that is exactly the emerging category a16z would want first-mover exposure to. (2) Whether the "cost per iteration, progress per dollar" metering problem Li identifies becomes a specific product category other a16z portfolio companies address. (3) Reception among the developer audience Li is writing for, given the essay directly engages with a widely-shared June 2026 post from outside a16z.
