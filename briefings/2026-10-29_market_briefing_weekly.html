Markets & Macro · Weekly

The Federal Reserve holds as oil tops $116 and AI shares swing

Three forces pulled in different directions. Oil, which feeds into the prices of nearly everything, climbed to its highest level since April on renewed Middle East tension. Investors swung from doubting the huge sums spent on artificial intelligence (AI) to cheering a stock-market filing by Anthropic, the company that makes Claude, and then back to sorting winners from losers among the big technology companies. And the Federal Reserve (the US central bank) left its main interest rate unchanged on 28 October, but two of its voting officials wanted an increase, so the next move is expected to be up. The European Central Bank (the euro area's central bank) announces its own decision on 29 October, the day of publication.

historyWhere things stood on 1 October

  • US shares had slipped through September: the S&P 500 (an index of 500 large US companies) stood at 7,651.54 on Wednesday 30 September, because strong economic data kept government bond yields high.
  • Interest rates: the Federal Reserve had raised its main interest rate on 16 September to a range of 3.75%–4.00%, and Goldman Sachs (a US bank) expected a second rise on 28 October. The ten-year US government bond yield (the yearly interest the government pays to borrow for ten years) was 5.289%, the highest in about two decades.
  • Oil and gold: Brent crude (the standard international oil price, quoted per barrel of about 159 litres) was $98.03 and gold $4,211 an ounce on 30 September.
  • Crypto: bitcoin (a digital currency) was near $83,800, down from an eight-month high near $86,000 on 23 September. The CLARITY Act (a bill setting federal rules for crypto markets) was stalled in the Senate after a failed vote on 15 September.
  • Europe: the European Central Bank had raised its deposit rate (the interest it pays banks that park money with it) to 2.50% and was expected to decide again on 29 October.

US equities15 – 28 Oct 2026

US shares end a rough two weeks about 1% below their record as oil and technology doubts pull both ways

The S&P 500 (an index of 500 large US companies) fell 2% and the Nasdaq Composite (an index dominated by technology shares) 4.5% in the week to 16 October, after the monthly report on what households pay for goods and services, the consumer price index (CPI), came in higher than forecast. A second blow came on Thursday 22 October, when Meta, the owner of Facebook and Instagram, reported results that revived doubts about spending on AI, and a third on Friday 23 October, when a jump in oil pulled the Dow Jones Industrial Average (an index of 30 large US companies) down 1.23%. Anthropic's stock-market filing on 26 October then started a rebound that carried into the Federal Reserve's decision.

Context & background: The S&P 500 closed at 7,651.54 on 30 September and reached its record of 7,818.93 on 6 October, so the damage came after that peak. The September consumer price index (CPI) was published on Wednesday 14 October. Yahoo Finance described both it and the producer price index (the matching measure of what businesses are paid) as higher than forecast, and the headline from CNN (Cable News Network) put annual inflation at 3.7%. The ten-year government bond yield (the yearly interest the government pays to borrow for ten years) briefly reached 5.33% after the consumer price index (CPI) release before easing to about 5.09% by Friday 16 October. The sell-off was concentrated in AI-linked companies: CoreWeave, which rents out AI computing power, fell 12% on Friday 16 October after a fall of more than 15% two days earlier on worries about its debt, and a Portfolio Terminal weekly recap puts the fall in chip shares for the week at 11.41%.

The details: The Dow was the steadiest of the three: up 0.7% in the week to 16 October while the Nasdaq lost 4.5%, because it holds few of the AI companies. Wednesday 21 October was calm (S&P 500 up 0.58%). On Thursday 22 October the Nasdaq fell 2.60% and the S&P 500 1.21% as Meta lost almost 7% (see the earnings section) and Brent crude, the standard international oil price, rose to $112.91. The next day the damage spread beyond technology: the Dow fell 1.23% as Brent finished the day at $116.72, and shares of food and household-goods makers, utilities and healthcare companies fell too. For the week of 19 to 23 October the Dow gained 0.4% while the S&P 500 lost 0.5% and the Nasdaq 0.7%. From Friday 23 to Wednesday 28 October the three indexes then rose 1.6% (S&P 500), 2.3% (Nasdaq) and 0.5% (Dow). The CNBC and Yahoo Finance pages quote slightly different weekly percentages for the week to 16 October, so those two figures are approximate.

Why it still matters: Shares are being pulled by two forces at once. Higher oil feeds directly into the prices households pay, which raises the chance that the Federal Reserve lifts interest rates again in December; falling confidence in AI hits the group of companies that carried the market this year. The rebound since 23 October leaned on one event, Anthropic's filing, plus the Federal Reserve's decision to wait, and both can fade. The tests are whether the S&P 500 can pass its 6 October record, about 1% away, and whether Brent holds above $110.

Federal ReserveWed 28 Oct 2026

The Federal Reserve holds its main rate at 3.75%–4.00%, but two officials wanted a rise and December looks likely

The Federal Reserve (the US central bank) left its main interest rate, the one that sets the cost of borrowing across the economy, unchanged on Wednesday 28 October, as nearly all forecasters expected. The vote was 10 to 2: Lisa Cook, a member of its board, and Beth Hammack, president of its Cleveland regional bank, wanted a quarter-point (0.25 percentage point) increase. Interest-rate futures (contracts through which traders bet on future rates) put the chance of an increase at the next meeting, on 8–9 December, at about 85%. Kevin Warsh, the Federal Reserve's chair, said politics played "no role" even though the US midterm elections (the national vote halfway through a president's term) are days away.

Context & background: The Federal Reserve raised its main rate on 16 September for the first time in three years, unanimously. Since then inflation has not eased: the September consumer price index (CPI), published on 14 October, showed annual inflation of 3.7% according to CNN (Cable News Network) and was higher than forecast according to Yahoo Finance, and it pushed the ten-year government bond yield (the interest the government pays to borrow for ten years) to 5.33% that day. In September, 16 of the Federal Reserve's 18 policymakers had projected at least one more rise in 2026, so a hold with two votes for a rise reads as a pause, not a change of direction. Goldman Sachs, a US bank, had forecast an October rise; it did not happen.

The details: The statement changed in three places: the decision verb moved from "raise" to "maintain", economic growth was upgraded from "solid" to "strong", and the bank said "the accumulation of more information" would guide the next step. The ten-year yield rose about 0.05 percentage points to 5.04% after the statement and closed the day at 5.02%. Mr Warsh, who has avoided signalling future moves, said one of the eight 2027 meetings will focus on long-term strategy without a rate vote. Before the decision, futures gave about a 90.6% chance of a hold, so the outcome itself held no surprise. These details come from press coverage; the Federal Reserve's own statement and the full transcript of the press conference have not been checked.

Why it still matters: With growth called strong and oil near $115, the committee has little reason to cut and a clear route to a December rise. Data due before then decide how firm that is: on Thursday 29 October, the Federal Reserve's preferred inflation gauge, the personal consumption expenditures (PCE) price index, and the third-quarter gross domestic product (GDP) estimate; the monthly jobs report on 6 November; the next consumer price index (CPI) on 10 November; and the record of this meeting's discussion around 18 November.

Oil · Middle East16 – 28 Oct 2026

Brent crude climbs to $116.72, its highest closing price since April and about 19% above 30 September

Brent crude (the standard international oil price, quoted per barrel of about 159 litres) rose 3.5% to $109.55 on Friday 16 October, 2.64% to $112.91 on Thursday 22 October, and 3.1% to $116.72 on Friday 23 October, the highest closing price since April according to CNBC. Reports tie the move to Middle East risk during the war involving the US and Iran: a Houthi attack on Saudi Arabia (the Houthis are an Iran-backed armed group in Yemen) in one account, fear of a new flare-up of the US–Iran conflict in another. By the morning of 28 October Brent was near $115.

Context & background: Brent stood at $98.03 on 30 September, after six falling days driven by US–Iran contacts at the United Nations, and rebounded toward $100 on 1 October. That calm did not last. The 2026 rise in energy prices is linked to the Middle East war, which is also why the European Central Bank has been raising rates this year. The Strait of Hormuz (the narrow sea passage for much of the world's oil) and the US–Iran talks are covered in our separate Geopolitics briefing.

The details: On 16 October Brent was $109.55 and US crude $101.35. By 20 October one data provider showed Brent at $110.48. On Thursday 22 October Brent finished at $112.91, then at $116.72 the next day, which CNBC called its highest closing price since April. On the morning of 28 October Forbes Advisor showed Brent at $115.16 and US crude at $101.76. Providers disagree on the cause: TradingKey names a Houthi attack on Saudi Arabia, while CNBC's posts speak more generally of a possible renewed US–Iran flare-up and attacks near the Red Sea. No single primary source for the trigger has been found, so the cause is unconfirmed. Brent's $116.72 is about 19% above its 30 September level of $98.03.

Why it still matters: Oil is the main reason inflation is not falling, and so a main reason the Federal Reserve and the European Central Bank are leaning toward higher interest rates. On Friday 23 October it moved shares directly: the Dow fell 1.23% on the day oil jumped. Oil above $110 also lifts bond yields and, for now, pushes gold down rather than up.

AI spending · Big Technology22 – 28 Oct 2026

Investors punish Meta and Alphabet for heavy AI spending and reward Microsoft's cloud growth

Meta (owner of Facebook and Instagram) saw its shares fall 6.98% on Thursday 22 October after results showed a quarter of heavy spending on AI equipment that left it burning cash. After the US market closed on Wednesday 28 October, Microsoft reported results above expectations, led by Azure, its cloud-computing business, and its shares rose in trading after the close. Alphabet, Google's parent, reported revenue slightly below forecasts and a bigger cash drain, and its shares fell in the same after-close trading. Oracle, a business-software company, jumped 11% during the day on 28 October after an investor presentation.

Context & background: The question for every large technology company this season is whether spending tens of billions of dollars on AI chips and data centres will earn a return. "Free cash flow" is the cash left after paying for that long-lived equipment. Meta's was negative $2.12 billion on spending of $34.91 billion, and the company narrowed its forecast for 2026 spending to $135–145 billion from $130–145 billion. That reminded investors of the doubts that had already hit AI-linked shares the week before.

The details: Microsoft's profit per share, adjusted to leave out one-off items, was $4.89 against roughly $4.72 expected; its spending on equipment was $39.37 billion ($45.2 billion counting leased equipment) and its cash flow after spending $18.63 billion, down 27% on a year earlier. Alphabet's profit per share was $2.12, down from $2.87, and fell short of expectations even after setting aside an $8.7 billion loss on investments; its Google Cloud revenue was $30.7 billion, up 103% according to Motley Fool (a very large figure that has not been checked against Alphabet's own release), and its spending on equipment and data centres was $51.4 billion, up from $24.0 billion a year earlier. Alphabet forecast $195–205 billion of such spending for 2026 and said 2027 would rise significantly. The sources do not say how far either company's shares moved after the close. Also on Wednesday 28 October, Vertiv, a maker of data-centre power and cooling equipment, rose almost 17% on bullish long-term forecasts, and F5, a network-software company, fell more than 20% on weak forecasts.

Why it still matters: The market now judges each company on its own cloud results instead of buying the whole AI group together. Companies that can show revenue growing as fast as spending are rewarded; those that cannot are punished. How the shares of Microsoft and Alphabet moved on Thursday 29 October is not yet known.

Context & background: Bitcoin slid in late September from an eight-month high near $86,000, and the CLARITY Act (a bill setting federal rules for crypto markets) stalled after a failed Senate vote on 15 September. Since then bitcoin has moved in a narrow band: about $81,400 on 16 October, about $80,700 on 27 October.

The details: The Yahoo Finance report describes several days of money leaving crypto funds that trade on stock exchanges and hold the coins, without giving amounts; on 27 October the largest bitcoin and ether funds rose between 1.06% and 1.32% in value. Glassnode, a crypto data firm, supplies the claim about large holders on Hyperliquid. That is one source describing a narrow group of traders, so it is a signal of mood, not proof of direction. No new Senate action on the CLARITY Act has been found since the failed vote, so it is unchanged, not confirmed dead; the post-election sitting of Congress is the next real chance.

Why it still matters: Bitcoin held up better than technology shares in the week of the Federal Reserve decision, but it has not recovered the $85,000 to $86,000 area of late September. A December interest-rate rise would test it, since higher yields on safe assets compete with a coin that pays nothing. The 6 November jobs report is the next scheduled input.

lightbulbEditor's view

A pause, but the next move is expected to be up

1. The Federal Reserve held, and the crowd called it better than the futures. The bettors who gave 63.5% to a hold beat the interest-rate futures; the two votes for a rise and a roughly 85% futures chance of a December increase say the pause is a wait, not a turn.

2. Oil is the driver to watch. Brent at $116.72 on 23 October, about 19% above 30 September, is what keeps inflation high and December rate talk alive. The cause of the latest jump is reported differently across sources; the Strait of Hormuz is covered in our separate Geopolitics briefing.

3. AI shares now rise or fall one company at a time. Meta fell almost 7% for spending heavily, Microsoft rose on cloud growth, Alphabet fell on cash flow. Anthropic's filing showed demand but is also a possible rival for investors' money.

4. Gold and bitcoin both fell as rate rises looked likelier. Gold touched a three-month low and bitcoin slipped 2.1% in a week, because neither pays interest while rates are rising, and the war did not help gold this time.

5. Some figures are thinner than usual. The DAX (Germany's main share index) and the official September inflation table could not be sourced, so the 3.7% inflation reading rests on one news headline.

6. The next ten days decide a lot. The Federal Reserve's preferred inflation gauge and third-quarter GDP (gross domestic product) on 29 October, the European Central Bank the same day, the jobs report on 6 November, and the consumer price index (CPI) on 10 November all come before the Federal Reserve's 9 December decision.

Next to watch: the European Central Bank's 29 October decision, Thursday's share reaction to Microsoft and Alphabet, whether Brent holds above $110, and whether the S&P 500 passes its 6 October record.
