What they argue

No verdicts yet — this is the argument in the guests' own terms.

Gerlach: an ageing spike burden as the hidden driver

Joachim Gerlach, a former industrial-engineering entrepreneur who now runs a self-funded research alliance from Fuerteventura, argues that spike protein from infection and vaccination persists in cells, exosomes and immune cells long after either event, saturating and inflaming tissue and producing a multisystem disease touching microcirculation, gut, immune function and the nervous system. He says his group maps this burden, sells a finger-prick rapid test for it, and sits deliberately between the two camps: "Wir sind relativ narrativfrei" ("we are relatively free of narrative"). He says the same of both sides, including the vaccine-critical scene, which he calls friends. He puts a brake on his own case twice: "keine Panik, aber realistische Einschätzung der Situation" ("no panic, but a realistic assessment of the situation").

Ehrhorn: what she says she sees in the practice

Ursula Ehrhorn, a Berlin physician in private integrative-medicine practice, describes findings from her own patients: cold hands, changes under capillary microscopy, gut patients who no longer tolerate probiotics, an immune system that "reagiert fast gar nicht" ("barely reacts at all"), and people who look fine on standard labs while feeling unwell. Her grievance: such patients are routinely sent away as psychosomatic — "Das ist ja Gaslighting, was die Kollegen machen" ("that's gaslighting, what our colleagues are doing"). That grievance is well documented and is the strongest thing in the episode.

Ben: pushing back

The host pushes back more than the format usually allows. He names the conflict when Gerlach declines to name his own supplements, asks whether masks now make more sense if the virus is worse than officially stated, says he does not want to sell panic, and takes the rapid test himself on camera. His own result: negative.

Five claims, checked

The five load-bearing claims, in the order made. The rest of the 30 claims checked sit in the scorecard below.

Two thirds have spike

Gerlach: "Ungefähr 66% der Menschen, die getestet wurden, hatten Spike." ("roughly 66% of the people we tested had spike")

The figure comes from the group's own rapid-test survey of people who came to be tested — largely self-referred, often sent by therapists already suspecting a problem — not a population sample, on a test with no published sensitivity or specificity. Gerlach hedges the size himself: "Wir haben wenigstens nur 700 Ergebnisse. Ist noch nicht viel." ("we've only got 700 results so far — not much yet."). Published work sits in patient cohorts, not the general population: Swank et al. (2023) found spike in ~60% of a cohort against 0% of controls; Peluso/Deeks (2024) found antigen in a minority, and their 2025 follow-up found no clinical difference between antigen-positive and -negative patients.

CheckMisleading C 3/5 — self-selected, unpublished, on a test with no published sensitivity or specificity; contrast Swank 2023's ~60% in a patient cohort vs 0% in controls, Peluso/Deeks 2024's minority finding, and the 2025 null result.
CheckUnverifiable C 2/5 — the 9,200 MMD Magdeburg results and the claimed 2025→2026 doubling appear in no publication or on the lab's own pages.
CheckPartly sourced C 3/5 — the €34.90 test is sold to professionals only, no CE/IVDR or validation data shown; the threshold is given on air in two units, ng/ml and pg/ml, a thousandfold apart.

A real phenomenon in patients, a number that cannot carry the weight put on it.

The publication

Gerlach: "Wir haben es ja publiziert, ne?" ("we did publish it, didn't we?")

The paper is real, and it is the group's strongest card: "Spike Protein–Mediated Compound Immunodeficiency Cascade in COVID-19 and Long-COVID", IJSBAR 79(1), 5 July 2026. It is also, in one author list, the whole network named elsewhere in this piece — Gerlach, Jaeger, König, Mavberg, Ehrhorn, Seneff among the co-authors — in a journal outside the main biomedical indexes: its own indexing page lists Google Scholar and WorldCat, shows Scopus as "sent for evaluation", and lists neither PubMed/MEDLINE nor Web of Science.

CheckMostly true, with a caveat C 5/5 — the paper exists as described, by the network's own author list, in a journal not indexed by PubMed/MEDLINE or Web of Science.

Publication is genuine effort, not yet independent confirmation — the two are being presented here as the same thing.

Blood washing

Gerlach on Jaeger's apheresis: "die höchste Erfolgsquote hat bei besonders schweren Fällen" ("has the highest success rate in especially severe cases")

The brake comes from inside the conversation: "das ist nicht das Allheilmittel" ("it is not a cure-all") is said once and immediately echoed — the captions do not settle whether the host or Ehrhorn says it first — and Ehrhorn warns that repeated apheresis sessions can strip out useful antibodies along with whatever the procedure is meant to remove.

CheckMisleading C 4/5 — evidence is observational case series only; no RCT as of Sept 2026. The German S1 guideline advises against apheresis outside studies; the DGfN does not recommend it and warns of serious complications; IGeL-Monitor found no completed study, evidence unclear. Cost €1,300–3,000/session, self-pay. Named critic: Carmen Scheibenbogen, Charité.

The one item in the episode where a listener can lose five figures on evidence that does not yet exist. Physician flag.

Half a million excess deaths

Gerlach: "492 000 Todesfälle mehr als normalerweise zu erwarten gewesen wären" — in his own words, that is, "492,000 more deaths than would normally have been expected."

He hedges that he will not pin the toll on infection versus vaccination, calling it "wahrscheinlich eine Mischung aus all diesen Faktoren" ("probably a mixture of all these factors").

CheckWrong figure C 5/5 — Destatis: 2019 939,520 · 2020 985,572 · 2021 1,023,687 · 2022 1,066,341 · 2023 1,028,206 · 2024 1,007,758 · 2025 1,003,130 deaths. 2021 was 1,023,687, against the "1330 000 oder sowas" ("1,330,000 or thereabouts") given on air. Raw excess 2020–24 against the 2015–19 mean is ~444,000; Destatis' own age-adjusted method gives less still. No method yields 492,000.

The direction of his point survives — something raised German mortality for three years — but the number does not; the biggest single error in the episode is a roughly 300,000-death overstatement inside his own framing.

What to actually do

Gerlach: "bestimmte Nasensprays könnte man zweckentfremden" ("certain nasal sprays could be repurposed")

Alongside the nasal-spray suggestion, the episode names a vitamin D target and argues that the masks actually worn had too coarse a weave and no air exchange — while Gerlach adds that a properly engineered mask, fine fleece with inlet and outlet valves, "hätten bestimmt viel geholfen" ("would certainly have helped a lot"). The three items sit at very different points on the evidence scale.

CheckMostly true, one good trial C 4/5 — azelastine, Phase 2 RCT, Saarland, JAMA Internal Medicine, Sept 2025, n=450: 2.2% vs 6.7% infections, ~67% relative reduction, single centre, authors ask for confirmation. Carrageenan sprays: smaller, older results. Physician flag.
CheckWrong figure C 4/5 — the "≥65 ng/ml" vitamin D target is in no major guideline; Endocrine Society 2024 advises against routine testing and sets no target; IOM/NAM sufficiency is ≥20 ng/ml, caution above 50; VITAL and D-Health show no mortality benefit. Physician flag.
CheckMisleading C 4/5 — the 80 nm virus vs 2,500 nm "mesh" argument treats a filter as a sieve; capture also works by electrostatic attraction, diffusion and impaction, why FFP2/N95 filter ≥95% at the most-penetrating 0.3 µm size. Cochrane 2023 found community evidence too weak to settle it, and its editor-in-chief rejected the "masks don't work" reading.

The cheapest, best-evidenced item they name is also the one they undersell; the two they oversell are free to get wrong and not free to act on.

Where it is contestedCarmen Scheibenbogen (Charité), the named critic of apheresis, runs a competing long-COVID research programme of her own; the DGfN and the AWMF S1 guideline both counsel against apheresis outside studies. Peluso and Deeks, cited above for antigen persistence, published a 2025 follow-up finding no clinical difference between antigen-positive and antigen-negative patients. Antonelli et al. (Lancet, 2022) found lower per-infection long-COVID risk under Omicron than under Delta — the opposite of the episode's framing. A J Med Virol 2024 letter exchange disputes Brogna's gut-bacteria claim. For the strongest form of the guests' own side: Swank 2023, Phetsouphanh 2022, Klein/Iwasaki 2023, and Al-Aly on reinfection risk are real, replicated ground — dismissing every persistence claim would be as wrong as the overstatement.

Stepping back

The error direction in this run. Of 30 scorecard rows: 2 True, 8 Mostly true (four of them narrowed — in vitro only, modelled only, one trial only, or with a caveat), 8 Misleading, 5 wrong on a figure, a date or a superlative, 4 Unverifiable, and 3 Open or partly sourced. The errors run one way — every checkable number came back smaller, older or narrower than stated: the real 1.02 million German deaths in 2021 against a hedged "1,330,000 or thereabouts"; a patient-cohort finding rendered as a population rate; an unvalidated test threshold in two units a thousandfold apart; a 2026 congress moved to 2025, its ~16 speakers becoming 16,000 participants. Meanwhile the phenomena pointed at — antigen persistence in a subset, dismissed patients, added reinfection risk — are largely real. One run is not a base rate.
The safety line. The two things a listener might act on today — paying for apheresis, or treating a rapid-test strip as a reason to start on prescription antihistamines or pregabalin — are the two with the least evidence behind them and the most cost attached. The protocols named on air are described as physician-administered; the risk is a listener shortening that path. The one with an actual randomised trial behind it is a nasal spray.

What could not be substantiated

  • The 9,200 MMD laboratory results and the claimed 2025→2026 doubling of positive rates.
  • The 66% figure treated as a rate for the general population rather than for the self-selected group tested.
  • 2.5 years of nasal spike persistence.
  • HHV-6 reactivation in 60% of long-COVID patients.
  • 16,000–18,000 epigenetic dysregulations "in one go".
  • Three years of lost longevity per Omicron infection.
  • An "AstraZeneca-funded" study said to have found little spike protein.
  • A Washington Post interview with Gerlach.
  • A patent for the senolytic "provocation" method.
  • The disability and insurer-loss figures cited for the United States.

What survives

  • Antigen persistence in a subset of long-COVID patients is real and replicated.
  • The dismissal of long-COVID and post-vac patients as psychosomatic is a documented failure of care.
  • Reinfection adds measurable risk of long-COVID outcomes.
  • The azelastine nasal-spray trial is real, randomised and positive, even if single-centre.
  • The German mortality rise of 2020–2022 was real, even at its correct, smaller size.

What to discount

  • The guests: the 66% figure read as a population rate, the excess-death figure, the vitamin D target, the mask-filtration argument, and Chetty's growing patient count.
  • The host: letting "Zikade" and the thousandfold unit mix-up on the test threshold pass unchallenged.
  • The critics: Scheibenbogen runs a competing long-COVID research programme of her own, and "no evidence yet" is not the same finding as "no phenomenon".
  • The compiler: the absence of a published figure is not proof that it is wrong — several items here are marked Unverifiable, not false.
