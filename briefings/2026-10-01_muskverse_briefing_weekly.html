SpaceX's Starship finally reaches orbit; Tesla's Cybercab deadline passes in silence

Three stories moved this week. Starship, SpaceX's giant reusable rocket, flew its first trip into orbit around Earth after repeated delays and mostly worked, but its upper section was destroyed when it came down in the sea. The date by which Tesla had to give the US road-safety regulator sworn answers about the Cybercab, its driverless taxi with no steering wheel or pedals, came and went with no public word from either side. And SpaceX and xAI, Musk's artificial-intelligence company and maker of the Grok chatbot, are confirmed as one corporate group: SpaceX announced the purchase itself on 2 February. Around those, Tesla arranged new bank financing and moved the reveal of its long-promised Roadster sports car. Musk's political spending, his lawsuits, the tunnelling company and the brain-implant company had nothing new to report.

TESLADeadline passed, no public replydue 30 Sept 2026

Tesla's 30 September deadline to answer its safety regulator on the Cybercab passes with no public confirmation either way

The Cybercab is Tesla's two-seat driverless taxi, built without a steering wheel or foot-operated brake pedal. On 10 September the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration, the US federal road-safety regulator, issued a "special order", a legally binding demand for sworn answers, requiring Tesla to answer 21 questions under oath by 30 September about how it confirmed the car meets US safety rules. In the US, carmakers certify this for themselves ("self-certification") rather than getting approval beforehand. As of the afternoon of Wednesday 30 September, Yahoo Finance reported that neither Tesla nor the regulator had said publicly whether Tesla had filed its answers or asked for more time. No later statement from either side had appeared by 1 October.

Meanwhile the service kept growing: a community tracker of Texas driverless-vehicle registrations counted 126 Cybercabs on 26 September, all in Austin. Correction: an earlier figure here of about 1,000 Cybercabs in Austin was too high; current counts are about 126.

Context & background.The special order is the binding step the regulator took after opening an audit of the Cybercab on 3 September. It was signed by the regulator's chief counsel, Peter Simshauser, and warns that incomplete or false answers can bring civil fines of up to $27,874 per day, to a maximum of roughly $139 million. The regulator's stated objection is that a car without a foot-operated service brake cannot be self-certified to the federal braking standard without a formal exemption (official permission to depart from a rule), the route that Zoox, the driverless-taxi company owned by Amazon, took in July.

The details.Silence is not evidence of non-compliance, since a filing could have been made without announcement. What can be said is narrower: no public notice of an extension, a filing or a missed deadline has appeared, and Yahoo Finance is the only outlet to address the deadline date itself. On fleet size, a community tracker put the Cybercab fleet at 126 on 26 September and the fan-run site Teslarati reported it had doubled from 58 to 125 within a week; neither is an official Tesla figure. The same tracker puts the whole Texas driverless-taxi operation at 546 vehicles, including 420 Model Y cars.

Why it still matters.A fleet of a few hundred vehicles makes the regulatory question smaller in absolute terms but not in kind: the dispute is about whether the vehicle design can be certified at all without an exemption, and any recall or exemption requirement would apply to every Cybercab already carrying passengers. Whether the regulator publishes Tesla's answers, extends the deadline or opens a formal investigation will be visible only when it chooses to say so.

Watch items.Any regulator statement on receipt of the answers; any change in Cybercab ride availability in Austin; the planned expansion of the service to Houston, which community trackers suggest is close.

TESLAFinancingfiled 29 Sept 2026

Tesla arranges $30 billion of new credit lines but says it will not draw on them in 2026

Credit lines are borrowing capacity agreed in advance: the company pays fees to hold them and borrows only if it chooses. According to the technology outlet TechCrunch, citing a regulatory filing dated 29 September, Tesla has set up three bank facilities totalling $30 billion. Citibank provides a $20 billion three-year loan that can be drawn in instalments. Wells Fargo provides an $8 billion five-year revolving line (money that can be borrowed, repaid and borrowed again) and a $2 billion revolving line that runs for 364 days. Tesla said it does not expect to use the money in 2026, because it has already planned at least $25 billion of capital spending (money spent on factories and equipment) for the year.

Context & background.TechCrunch reports that Tesla held roughly $9 billion of debt and more than $40 billion of cash and investments at the end of the second quarter, so the new lines are headroom rather than a response to a cash shortage. It frames them as support for three products that each need dedicated factories: the Cybercab, Optimus (Tesla's humanoid robot) and the Semi (its electric heavy truck).

The details.The structure matters more than the headline figure. A loan Tesla can draw in instalments and revolving lines are committed capacity, not money borrowed. The terms above are as TechCrunch reports them, including the statement that Tesla will not use the funds in 2026; the interest margin and fees are not public in that report. A separate analysis by the investing site 24/7 Wall St. this week put Tesla's free cash flow (the cash left after running costs and investment) at negative $1.09 billion in the second quarter, the backdrop to arranging financing in advance.

Why it still matters.Securing $30 billion before it is needed suggests spending on new factories is expected to rise beyond the $25 billion already planned, but only 2027 plans will show by how much.

SPACEXUpdateflew 28 Sept 2026

Starship reaches orbit on its fourteenth flight and releases 26 satellites, then its ship breaks up on splashdown

Starship is SpaceX's giant reusable rocket system: a Super Heavy booster (the lower stage, which falls away) topped by the Starship "ship" (the upper stage, which carries the cargo). It lifted off from Starbase, Texas at 12:48 coordinated universal time on 28 September (7:48 a.m. local time) and the ship, number 41, reached low Earth orbit about half an hour later. That is the first orbital flight, meaning the vehicle circled the planet, after thirteen suborbital ones, which go up and come straight back down. It released 26 third-generation satellites for Starlink, SpaceX's satellite-internet service, its first working payload (the cargo a rocket exists to carry). The booster splashed down, landing in the water, in the Gulf of Mexico. The ship came down in the northern Pacific about 3 hours 8 minutes after launch and tipped over and exploded on contact with the water.

It was not a clean flight. One of the ship's three vacuum engines, designed to work in space, shut down early, mission control first called off the orbit attempt and then reversed that decision, and the planned ten-hour flight was cut to about three hours, with an early deorbit burn (an engine firing that drops the ship out of orbit) after roughly two orbits.

Context & background.Before launch the date had slipped four times, with reports linking the delay to a flight licence from the Federal Aviation Administration (the US aviation regulator), which an orbital attempt needs. Reports do not say when the licence was issued, so that explanation stays unconfirmed, though the launch itself shows the flight was cleared.

The details.Space.com says the booster lost several engines in flight without consequence; Astronomy puts it at one engine and a landing burn on 31 of 33; the sources differ on detail but agree the booster came down in the Gulf of Mexico. The ship's target orbit was about 275 kilometres up. Three of the 26 satellites carried cameras to photograph the ship's heat shield, the main unknown for re-entry; the explosion at splashdown means the vehicle itself was not recovered, and no report says what the cameras captured. The plan announced before launch, roughly six orbits and a splashdown near Chile, did not survive: the flight ended after about three hours (reports say one to two orbits) in the northern Pacific. The ship has three sea-level and three vacuum engines, so the loss of one left it with five.

Why it still matters.Reaching orbit with a real payload is the capability that SpaceX's plans for Starlink, and for the lunar missions it hopes to fly for NASA (the US space agency) in later years, depend on. The part not yet shown is a controlled re-entry and recovery of the upper stage. SpaceX's share price barely moved on the day, up 0.75% according to the investing site 24/7 Wall St., and one aggregator reports a 2.6% rise the next day.

Watch items.Whether the Federal Aviation Administration requires an investigation into the engine shutdown or the splashdown explosion; the date of the fifteenth flight; any release of the heat-shield camera images.

edit_noteEditor's view

One milestone reached, one deadline unanswered in public, one question settled

1. Starship now has an orbit to its name and a hole in the story. The flight put 26 satellites in orbit, the capability that matters commercially. It also lost a ship engine, cut its plan from ten hours to three and destroyed the ship at splashdown, so the re-entry test the flight was built for is not demonstrated.

2. The Cybercab deadline produced no information. The regulator owed no announcement and Tesla made none. The only established fact is the absence of a public statement by 30 September; what the answers say, if they were filed, is unknown.

3. The Cybercab fleet is smaller than first reported. The roughly 1,000 vehicles given earlier was too high; current counts are about 126. The regulatory exposure is unchanged, the scale is smaller.

4. SpaceXAI is SpaceX. The acquisition was announced on 2 February and the AI business now carries the parent's name. What remains uncertain is the legal form, not the fact of the combination.

5. Tesla is raising capacity while volume softens. $30 billion of committed credit, not to be drawn this year, arrives alongside a forecast of about 7% fewer deliveries than a year earlier. The 2 October report is the next hard number.

To watch: Tesla's July–September deliveries and any statement on the Cybercab answers; the Roadster reveal on 15 October; whether the Federal Aviation Administration (the US aviation regulator) requires an inquiry into Flight 14 and when Flight 15 is set; and whether Grok 4.8 is released.
