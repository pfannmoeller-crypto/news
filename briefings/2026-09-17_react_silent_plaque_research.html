The case, at full strength

In Carvalho's own order — strong enough that he would sign it.

Cardiology keeps moving its detection point earlier: chest pain, then risk factors, then the coronary artery calcium (CAC) score, then coronary CT angiography (CTA), which sees the soft plaque a calcium score misses. REACT moves the line again — 57.1% of 16,808 people with no diagnosed heart disease already carried plaque, from 8.7% of men and 6.7% of women at 18–29 to roughly nine in ten by 60–70.

The turn is where plaque sits: in the young it was "most often peripheral and confined to a single vascular territory" — the study's own words — and isolated coronary disease was uncommon at every age (at most 9.3% of men, 5.0% of women). A clean heart scan in your thirties tells you less than it feels like. Hence his advice: carotid or femoral ultrasound for those with risk factors or family history, and the ankle-brachial index (ABI), a leg blood-pressure ratio, as a free first pass. Hedges: "I'm not saying that every healthy 25-year-old should get one of these"; of the ankle test, "it's not the most precise test in the world … quick and dirty"; and, crediting the calcium score for what it is, "you can have a calcium score of zero and have substantial plaque."

The argument, walked through

Verdict scale: 5/5 primary source · 4/5 two independent sources · 3/5 single credible source · 2/5 aggregator only.

Prevalence 2026

"57% despite again being people with no symptoms, no diagnosed heart disease."

The headline number carries the video: most people scanned already had plaque, and the rate climbs steeply with age though nobody had a heart diagnosis. That's the abstract's own figure.

CheckTrue C 5/5 — 57.1% of 16,808 adults (95% CI 56.3–58.0) had detectable plaque. NEJM abstract.
True C 5/5 — 18–29: 8.7% of men, 6.7% of women, matching his "5 to 10%". NEJM abstract.
Mostly true C 4/5 — the paper's own wording is "without known atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease," not "no symptoms": chest pain and claudication (leg pain on walking) did not exclude anyone. REACT design paper.

The design and the 57% figure are not in question; "no symptoms" is a gloss the paper itself does not use.

Where the plaque sits 2026

"in the youngest people up to their 30s, maybe even 40s, plaque usually was not found in the heart."

He walks the figure decade by decade: peripheral-only in the 18–29 and 30s groups; by the 40s "60 to 70% of men" have plaque, "maybe half, maybe a little over half" of it peripheral-only; coronary involvement becomes common in men only in the 50s–60s, while women stay "about 50/50" even at 60–70 — read straight off the paper's published figure.

CheckMostly true C 5/5 — the pattern is the abstract's own sentence: in the young strata, atherosclerosis was "most often peripheral and confined to a single vascular territory," and isolated coronary disease was uncommon at every age (≤9.3% of men, ≤5.0% of women). NEJM abstract.
Unverifiable here C 2/5 — the decade splits come from a figure behind the paywall, not seen by this compiler. Blog-level: 82.0% with coronary plaque also had peripheral plaque; 30–39, ≥2 territories in 6.0% of men / 2.6% of women; 60–70, all three territories in 56.3% / 30.7%. dralo.net.

The argument survives on the abstract's own sentence alone; the decade percentages are the part that still needs the figure to check.

The calcium score as a late event 2026

"the calcium score is a pretty late event in the disease progression."

Against treating a zero calcium score as "home free," he argues the score itself lags the disease — plaque, even a heart attack, is possible at zero — which is why coronary CT angiography (CTA), which also sees non-calcified ("soft") plaque, came next.

CheckTrue C 2/5 — of REACT's 30–39-year-olds with coronary plaque on CT angiography, 41.8% of men and 48.4% of women had a calcium score of zero (blog-sourced). MESA (a large U.S. heart-disease cohort study, 3/5) puts calcium-zero event rates at up to ~5 per 1,000 person-years, higher with smoking, diabetes or kidney disease — low, not zero.
True C 3/5 — CT angiography also detects soft, non-calcified plaque a calcium score misses. Topic-matched literature (SCOT-HEART).

Correct, and the strongest part of the video — though he never says how often heart attacks actually happen at a calcium score of zero; described as rare after 45, unquantified below it.

CIMT is not plaque 2026

"they're sometimes called CIMTs or carotid intimal medial thickness so it's basically measuring the thickness of the artery wall."

He offers carotid intima-media thickness (CIMT) as the name for the carotid ultrasound that finds plaque. But CIMT measures wall thickness in segments that are, by definition, plaque-free; plaque is a separate, focal thickening. A conflation, not a false claim about the study.

CheckMisleading C 4/5 — the American guideline (ACC/AHA 2019) does not support CIMT for cardiovascular risk assessment. The European guideline (ESC 2021) accepts carotid or femoral plaque detection as a risk modifier — not CIMT. ESC 2021 guideline; PMC review.

His advice — get an ultrasound that reports plaque — survives; the name he gives it points a viewer at the wrong test.

The ankle test 2026

"It's not the most precise test in the world, as you might imagine, but it's a quick and dirty"

He walks through the ankle-brachial index (ABI): blood pressure at both arms lying down, the higher systolic value as reference, then each ankle's value divided by that reference. His limits, both stated: "if you have a small amount of plaque, this is not going to pick it up. It's going to give you a normal ABI." — and, for anyone with symptoms, "absolutely get it checked, even if your ABI numbers look totally normal."

CheckMostly true C 4/5 — ranges match AHA 2012: normal 1.00–1.40, borderline 0.91–0.99, abnormal ≤0.90, above 1.40 = non-compressible arteries (diabetes, kidney disease). Standard method: Doppler, higher of two ankle readings per leg. His at-home cuff is a substitute — 69% sensitivity, 96% specificity vs Doppler (10 studies, 2,015 people): misses ~a third of obstructions, rarely a false alarm — matches "quick and dirty." USPSTF 2018: insufficient evidence for ABI screening without symptoms.
True C 3/5 — ABI is a flow test; it misses an obstruction too small to affect flow. AHA statement.

The mechanics and ranges check out; whether a healthy adult should run this at home is a separate question his own hedge already answers.

Where it is contested For: Fuster, Ibáñez (REACT's senior authors) — Ibáñez: "portable ultrasound devices usable by any healthcare professional will become the standard tool for atherosclerosis screening from the early stages of adult life" (CNIC release). Attia: atherosclerosis "well under way long before angiography is able to identify it." What this side has not shown: that finding plaque earlier and treating it changes outcomes — its own randomised trial has not run. Quotations in this box are as extracted from the cited pages, not read in a raw transcript.
Against: John Mandrola (Sensible Medicine): plaque on imaging "is still a surrogate marker"; the DANCAVAS trials (comprehensive cardiovascular screening in Denmark) "did not improve mortality or major adverse cardiac outcomes," and "in DANCAVAS II, screening increased the risk of major bleeding" — "be afraid of imaging when you feel well." What this side has not shown: DANCAVAS screened men aged 65–74, not the young adults REACT and Carvalho are talking about; the outcome evidence cuts against screening the old, and is silent on the young. Foy and Mandrola oppose calcium scoring "for any cause." Split among critics: plaque-is-disease-but-no-screening-yet vs. imaging-as-marker rejected outright. Unanswered: what a 25-year-old with one femoral plaque should do. No independent ESC discussant found; REACT-PROTECT (the randomised phase) runs 2027–2032.

  • Plaque volume grows exponentially with age (abstract, 5/5).
  • Lipid-lowering use in the 40s: 8.5% men / 3.1% women (blog, 2/5).

What survives

  • The study: 16,808 adults, 57.1% with plaque, the age curve, the peripheral-first pattern in youth.
  • The calcium-zero point — the strongest piece of the video.
  • The ankle-brachial-index ranges and mechanics, hedges included.

What to discount

  • "Asymptomatic" — participants had no diagnosis, not no symptoms.
  • "CIMT" as the name for a plaque-finding ultrasound — wrong test, right advice.
  • The decade percentages — read off an unseen, paywalled figure.
  • "As we saw, they go on to grow plaque in the heart" — cross-sectional data cannot show that; PESA could.
  • The investigators' screening enthusiasm — their outcome trial has not run yet.
