Pet Food Briefing · 13–19 September 2026

Pet food dealmaking swings toward Europe as Peru's anchovy fleet wins a reprieve

All four of this beat's regular areas had genuine, dated news this week — a rarity for a niche market that usually moves slowly. Nestlé is putting fresh money into capacity in Thailand, Peru's regulator has eased pressure on the anchovy fleet that supplies a fifth of the world's fishmeal, a new industry analysis maps a widening split between European and North American acquisition activity, and one trade award gives a small, concrete look at how "premium" pet food claims are being tested. Three items are dated to the primary week; the private-equity-and-acquisitions and production-investment sections did not need the 30-day widening this beat sometimes requires.

LEADCAPACITY18 Sep 2026

Nestlé commits $196 million to a second wave of capacity in Thailand

Nestlé, the Swiss food company whose pet care division owns Purina, Pro Plan, Felix and Friskies, is adding two more production lines at its plant in Rayong, Thailand, taking the site from three lines to five. The company is putting $196 million (157 million Swiss francs) into the expansion, with the new lines expected to be running by 2028.

The investment keeps capacity growing outside Nestlé's home region: Thailand's pet food exporters together shipped $1.4 billion of product in the first five months of 2026, up 5.1% compared with a year earlier, and the country now holds 10.2% of the global export market by value — second only to Germany's 12.1%.

Context & background. Nestlé's pet care arm, Purina, has been one of the most acquisitive capacity-builders in the industry for years, and Thailand has become one of its preferred sites for supplying Asia-Pacific demand without routing product through Europe. The expansion sits inside a broader Thai push: national pet food exports reached $3.3 billion in all of 2025, with the United States the single largest buyer at $430.8 million, or 30.2% of the total.

The details. The Rayong site currently runs three production lines; the new investment adds two more, bringing the total to five once they come online. No breakdown of which brands each line will serve was published, but Nestlé's Thai output already covers Felix, Friskies, Pro Plan and other Purina ranges sold across Asia and re-exported further afield. Thailand's other large producer, the Thai conglomerate Betagro, is separately targeting a near tripling of its own pet food sales — from roughly $83 million in 2025 to a target of about $172 million within three years — through premium and therapeutic products, which suggests the country's capacity build-out is broader than one company.

Why it still matters. The contrast with Europe is real, if not part of the same announcement: Mars — a separate company from Nestlé — said in April 2026 that it plans to close a dry-food plant in Minden, Germany, by late 2026, shifting production of its Pedigree and Crave lines to other European sites and affecting around 110 jobs, while it simultaneously spends roughly one billion euros ($1.15 billion) expanding newer plants in France, Poland and Spain. Read together, the pattern for 2026 looks less like "Europe versus Asia" and more like large manufacturers replacing older single-purpose lines everywhere with newer, more flexible ones, wherever demand is growing fastest.

Raw-material inputs

1 item
FISHMEAL16 Sep 2026

Peru's anchovy fleet wins a quota reprieve as the next season's survey begins

Peru's Ministry of Production has exempted the country's anchovy fishing fleet from a penalty mechanism that could have cut its future catch limits, just as government scientists begin the biomass survey (a survey estimating the total weight of fish in the water) that will set the size of the fishery's second 2026 season. Peru supplies roughly a fifth of the world's fishmeal — the ground, dried fish protein used in a large share of the pet food, farmed-fish feed and livestock feed made worldwide — so how much its fleet is allowed to catch each season moves a global raw-material input, not just a Peruvian one.

The reprieve comes after a difficult year for the fishery: the catch limit for the first north-central season (Peru's anchovy fishery is split into two named seasons a year in its north-central zone) of 2026 was set at 1.9 million metric tons in the spring, more than a million tons below the equivalent 2025 season, after government surveys found a smaller anchovy population and forecasters flagged a coastal El Niño weather pattern that can push the fish into colder, deeper water and out of the fleet's reach.

Context & background. Fishmeal and fish oil, rendered mainly from Peruvian anchovy, are the highest-quality protein and fat inputs available to pet food makers — used heavily in wet food, treats and some premium dry formulas — and they compete for the same Peruvian catch with the much larger fish-farming (aquaculture) feed industry. A poor Peruvian season does not stop production; it makes the fishmeal that is available more expensive relative to substitutes like poultry meal or plant proteins, which is a cost pressure rather than a supply cut-off.

The details. Coverage of the ministry's specific decision is limited outside subscriber-only trade reporting; what is public is that the anchovy fleet will not face a quota reduction as a penalty, and that the government's marine research survey for the second 2026 season is under way. That survey's biomass estimate is what regulators will use to set the second season's catch limit, expected to be announced in the coming weeks — last year's equivalent second-season limit, set in November 2025, came in at 1.63 million metric tons.

Why it still matters. This is a supply-geography story, not a price call — a prediction about where prices go: the point for a pet food buyer is that Peru's output is running behind pre-El-Niño norms for a second consecutive year, which keeps the world's dominant fishmeal source structurally tighter than it was three years ago, regardless of what the next specific quota number turns out to be.

Private equity & acquisitions

1 item
DEAL ACTIVITY16 Sep 2026

European pet food buyers are expanding abroad while American ones consolidate at home

A new trade-press analysis of 2026 acquisition activity in pet food finds European buyers pursuing growth outside their home markets, while companies in the United States and Canada are mostly buying to strengthen positions they already hold. Belgium's Nasta Pet Food, owned by Holding Babin, has combined with Canada's FirstMate Pet Foods on the back of 120 million euros ($139 million) in financing, creating a business with a combined production capacity of 54,000 tonnes of dry pet food and 4,000 tonnes of wet pet food a year.

German investment firm AlphaPet Ventures has kept up a similar pace of serial buying — its acquisition of German pet-supplements company Tierliebhaber was its sixth deal since 2020 — while Belgium's United Petfood, which also holds a stake in Germany's SmartPetPro, is estimated to be spending around 100 million euros ($115 million) a year on takeovers, including a dedicated push into North America. Across the market, typical 2026 deal prices have settled at roughly ten times a target's yearly profit before financing and accounting adjustments, down from the richer prices paid during the pandemic years, and private-label (store-brand products sold under a retailer's own name) products now make up 34% of pet food value sold across Europe's six largest markets, according to retail data firm Circana.

Context & background. The pet food deal market spent roughly three quiet years after the pandemic boom: 2025 saw about 23 tracked transactions, against 26 in 2024 and 48 to 58 a year during 2020 through 2022, as higher interest rates and a gap between what buyers wanted to pay and what sellers wanted to receive kept deals from closing. The pattern described this week is less a single mega-deal than several serial acquirers — financial buy-and-build platforms that grow by repeatedly acquiring smaller companies rather than through one large purchase — continuing to add smaller brands one at a time.

The details. Beyond Nasta-FirstMate and AlphaPet Ventures, the same pattern shows up elsewhere named in the analysis: Assisi Pet Care, a UK-based platform, bought a dog-chew brand in mid-2025 and a second all-meat chew brand in May 2026; French group Inspired Pet Nutrition added two French fresh-food and supplement makers in the summer of 2025; and Nordic-backed consolidator The Nutriment Company has made twelve acquisitions since January 2025 alone. None of these are single blockbuster transactions — they are a lot of smaller, serial deals, which is a different shape of "M&A cycle," referring to mergers and acquisitions, than the industry saw in the 2020-2022 boom.

Why it still matters. The private-label figure and the deal-price reset are connected: as supermarkets' own-label pet food keeps taking share from name brands, financial buyers are willing to pay for platforms that can supply both branded and private-label product, and the lower typical price makes more of those deals pencil out than a year or two ago. That combination — cheaper entry prices plus private label's rising share — is the more likely driver of the next wave of deals than any single company's plans, which this newsletter does not speculate about.

1 item
PREMIUMISATION16 Sep 2026

An award for one treat brand shows premium pet food's rising evidence bar

Yantai China Pet Foods, which sells treats under the Wanpy brand, won the 2026 "Jerky-Style Dog Treat of the Year" award from the Pet Innovation Awards for a chicken jerky treat built around measurable formulation numbers rather than marketing language: at least 90% real meat, at least 40% protein and at least 93% digestibility (a measure of how much of the food a dog's body can actually absorb and use), with no artificial preservatives, colours, grains, gluten, added sugar or artificial flavour enhancers.

The award matters less for the single product than for what the judging criteria signal about where premium claims are heading. The global pet food market was worth an estimated $158 billion in 2025, and buyers who once accepted words like "premium" or "natural" at face value are increasingly asking brands to show the ingredient percentages and production detail behind them.

Context & background. Premiumisation — trading buyers up to higher-priced, higher-claimed-quality products — has been the main brand-level growth lever in pet food for several years, as growth in everyday, low-price kibble volumes has flattened in most developed markets. Independent third-party checks on premium claims are rare in a category where "natural" and "premium" carry no fixed legal meaning in most markets, which is what makes an awards body publishing specific formulation thresholds noteworthy even at the scale of one brand.

The details. Yantai China Pet Foods is one of China's larger pet food and treat exporters; the win is a reminder that premiumisation and the push for measurable claims is no longer a purely Western trend. The Pet Innovation Awards judged the Wanpy jerky specifically against the four thresholds above, and the brand also received a separate excellence award from the World Pet Association the same week.

Why it still matters — and what is not yet known. One award for one product is not evidence that measurable-standard labelling is becoming a category-wide norm rather than a marketing advantage for the brands that adopt it early; no industry-wide survey of how many pet food companies now publish formulation percentages was found in the public record this week. That is the open question worth watching, not a claim this edition makes.

Editor's view

A capacity map redrawing itself, and a deal market that finally has something to show

  1. Capacity is moving to where growth is, not staying where history put it. Nestlé adding lines in Thailand while Mars removes a line in Germany is not one company's story — it is the same reallocation logic playing out at two different manufacturers, and it is worth watching whether Europe's next production-investment news is another expansion or another closure.
  2. The "deal cycle" so far is width, not size. Every named transaction this week is a small add-on purchase attached to a company the buyer already owns (a "bolt-on"), not a single large purchase — which fits a market where typical deal prices have come down and rising private-label (store-brand products sold under a retailer's own name) share rewards buyers who can supply both branded and store-brand product from the same factories.
  3. One award is a data point, not a trend. The Wanpy treat's published formulation numbers are genuinely verifiable, which is more than most premium claims offer — but a single brand's award is a long way from proof that the rest of the category is following.
