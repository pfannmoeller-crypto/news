Ukraine / Russia — talks held, little agreed

3 items
Diplomacy10–12 Nov

The Abu Dhabi talks ended with a deal to swap prisoners but no breakthrough on ending the war

Two days of US-mediated talks between Russia and Ukraine ended on 11 November. Russia's chief negotiator Vladimir Medinsky said both sides had agreed on a large prisoner exchange; Ukraine's Rustem Umerov confirmed it. The numbers and the date were still open, with final figures expected on 12 November.

US envoy Jared Kushner called the meeting "very tough" but productive and said territory would be the hardest issue. Kushner and fellow envoy Steve Witkoff then flew on to Moscow to brief President Vladimir Putin.

Context & background. Our 9 November edition named this round as the first three-way meeting since February and noted that earlier rounds this year produced no breakthrough. The format held: Medinsky led Russia, Umerov led Ukraine, and Witkoff and Kushner led the US side. Britain, France, Germany, Italy, the North Atlantic Treaty Organization (NATO) and the European Commission met the Ukrainian and US teams separately but never sat at the table with Russia, a point the Kyiv Post framed as Europe being sidelined.

The details. The first day lasted about four and a half hours, according to the Kyiv Post, and covered humanitarian and de-escalation steps, prisoner releases and security guarantees. Ukraine's team said no broader framework could be built before a full ceasefire. Reuters, as relayed by Meduza (a Russian news site based abroad), reported that Russia wanted the talks to centre on postwar European security, with Ukraine only "one aspect". Zelensky had floated swaps of 1,000-for-1,000 or 1,500-for-1,500; the Kyiv Post reported that Kyiv hopes for a record swap and a deal by New Year, which is a Ukrainian hope, not an agreed timetable. Reports differ on whether Witkoff and Kushner would go on to meet Zelensky afterwards; one says in Dublin. We could not read the full original articles, only short summaries of them.

Why it still matters. A prisoner swap is a real humanitarian result but it is the one thing the sides have agreed on in every round. The test of this round is the Moscow visit: if Putin accepts a new three-way round, the process lives; if the swap is the only outcome, it is a pause. Watch for: the swap date and size, the official account of the Putin meeting, and any date for a next round.

Territory11–12 Nov

Kyiv says it will not hand over Donbas, and the Kremlin is pressing wider European security demands

Zelensky said on 11 November that Ukraine will not surrender sovereign territory to end the war. The British newspaper The Telegraph reported that the US floated redrawing the borders of Donetsk and Luhansk along the current front line; a Ukrainian source said Kyiv would never recognise Russian occupation. A different Ukrainian source told Ukrainska Pravda no formal US initiative existed.

So whether a specific US plan was rejected is disputed. What is not disputed is that Moscow still demands Ukraine leave Donbas, the eastern industrial region it has not fully captured.

Context & background. Putin has repeatedly demanded that Ukraine withdraw from Donbas. The idea of freezing the line where it stands instead has circulated in earlier peace drafts. Our 9 November edition quoted Trump saying Ukraine would "end up with a little bit different shape"; this is the first reporting that puts a concrete border idea on the table, and it is second-hand.

The details. The New Voice of Ukraine, citing the Institute for the Study of War, reported that the Kremlin revived demands it first made in 2021 about NATO. The Kyiv Post also reported that Zelensky rejected Russian demands for security guarantees, saying no one has attacked Russia for decades, and that Ukraine wants the West to fund an army of 800,000 for ten years after the war. A US official was quoted as saying any territorial deal would likely come late in the talks. All of this is from short summaries and headlines; we could not open the underlying articles.

Why it still matters. Two incompatible positions are now public: Russia wants more than the front line, Ukraine says no to even the front line as a legal border. That makes the prisoner swap the ceiling for now. Watch for: whether Washington confirms or denies the front-line idea, and what Putin tells Witkoff and Kushner.

Strikes11–12 Nov

Russia hit Kyiv with ballistic missiles hours after the talks ended, then a drone hit an apartment block

The Kyiv Independent reported that Russia attacked Kyiv with ballistic missiles hours after the Abu Dhabi talks closed. On the morning of 12 November a Russian drone hit a nine-storey apartment building in the city, injuring two people and starting a fire, according to the Kyiv Post.

Context & background. This repeats the pattern from our last edition: heavy strikes and diplomacy inside the same days. On 6–7 November Russia fired 36 ballistic missiles at Kyiv before the talks were confirmed.

The details. We have headlines and a short summary for these two reports, not the full text, so damage and casualty figures beyond "two injured" are unknown to us. The Kyiv Post also carried a report of a Russian colonel killed in a bombing in St. Petersburg, part of a series of attacks on Russian military figures; we did not verify it.

Why it still matters. Strikes continuing through a negotiation means the swap is not leading to a wider pause in fighting, at least for now. Watch for: whether a pause on energy strikes, one of the reported agenda items, appears in any official account.

Iran / Hormuz — no talks, no reopening, Israel reportedly readying

1 item
US–Iran9–12 Nov

Iran denies any talks and says Hormuz stays shut; Trump says the US won but keeps the blockade

A senior Iranian security official, Mohsen Rezaei, said on 11 November that Iran is holding no talks with the Americans and that the Strait of Hormuz will not reopen until the US meets Tehran's seven conditions. Trump said the US won the war and that the naval blockade continues, after calling the war "effectively over" following a meeting with Qatar's prime minister on 9 November.

Israeli media reported that Israel's security cabinet ordered the military to step up preparations for an attack on Iran, while Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu reportedly pushes Trump to resume the war. Netanyahu's office denied the call was hostile.

Context & background. Our 9 November edition rested almost entirely on Al Jazeera. This one adds NPR (US National Public Radio), CNN (a US television news network), the Times of Israel and Fox News, as read through short summaries. It also corrects one line: we reported zero strikes for 5–9 November, but Fox News quotes Trump calling US attacks on Qeshm island, in the Strait of Hormuz, "minor strikes" on Iranian air defences and launchers, and Iranian state media reported them. Our earlier note that the Qeshm explosions were "headline-level only" therefore understated an actual US action.

The details. CNN (a US television news network) described US and Iranian officials as negotiating quietly, with Qatar mediating; Iran publicly denies it. The Times of Israel reported that Vice President Vance is weighing direct talks with a top Iranian security official, which Rezaei says is not planned. Rezaei also warned of strikes on US bases in Europe if Trump resumes the war. Fox News and the Times of Israel disagree on how heated the Trump–Netanyahu call was, and the Israeli military chief is reported to have warned against ill-advised action. The Kepler (an energy-data firm) figure comes from NPR (US National Public Radio)'s summary; it implies exports are recovering despite the closure, which fits CNN (a US television news network)'s picture of a stalemate with leaks. Our Markets briefing covers oil prices.

Why it still matters. This is a standoff where each side publicly says the other must move first. The new risk is on the Israeli side: a unilateral Israeli strike would test Trump's stated preference for waiting. We could not confirm the cabinet order beyond Israeli media reports. Watch for: any Israeli strike, any Vance meeting, and the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation summit conversations with Xi Jinping and Putin that Trump mentioned last week.

Israel — coalition talks begin

1 item
Israel9–12 Nov

Eisenkot's road to 61 seats runs through ultra-Orthodox parties that want draft exemptions he refuses

Gadi Eisenkot has 28 days from 8 November to form a government. United Torah Judaism told him it will not join unless he meets its demands on military-service exemptions, funding for its schools and the religious status quo; Eisenkot vowed not to give way on service for all. Shas, the other ultra-Orthodox party, had told its members to recommend Netanyahu.

Avigdor Liberman, whose party sits in Eisenkot's bloc, rejected relying on Arab-led parties and warned that a minority government would quickly bring Netanyahu back.

Context & background. This follows the 7 November decision by President Herzog that we reported on 9 November. Eisenkot's four-party alliance holds 48 seats; the arithmetic still requires either both ultra-Orthodox parties or outside support from Arab-led parties that he has said he will not govern with.

The details. The bloc agreed to nominate Matan Kahana of Eisenkot's Yashar party for speaker of the Knesset (Israel's parliament), a first vote that tests its unity; United Torah Judaism said it would not back replacing the current speaker. One member, Yair Golan, reportedly prefers a minority government backed from outside by Arab-led parties, and there are reported doubts about former prime minister Naftali Bennett's commitment. Netanyahu is calling for a national unity government and told ultra-Orthodox lawmakers not to trust Eisenkot; sources close to him floated a unity plan with Eisenkot, which Eisenkot's bloc rejects. A former Netanyahu aide said Netanyahu is in "panic", which is one person's view. Most of this is from short summaries.

Why it still matters. Eisenkot cannot win the ultra-Orthodox without conceding on the draft, and cannot concede without splitting his own bloc. A failure hands the mandate back and keeps Netanyahu in office as caretaker, with the Iran question above still live. Watch for: the speaker vote and any signed deal with United Torah Judaism.

The planned talks happened and settled little

1. Abu Dhabi produced a swap, not a framework. Both sides called it useful, and the only concrete result is prisoners. Territory is where it stops.

2. The Putin meeting is now the real test. If the US envoys come back with a date for another round, the track survives. We have no official account yet.

3. Iran's public line and the reporting do not match. Tehran says no talks; CNN (a US television news network) says quiet ones. Both can be true, but we cannot tell which parts are theatre.

4. Israel is the unpredictable factor on Iran. The reports of strike preparations are Israeli media only, but they came the same week Trump called the war effectively over.

5. A correction. Our last edition said no US strikes were confirmed on 5–9 November. Trump himself described attacks on Qeshm as "minor strikes".

Next: the Putin account of the meeting, the swap date, whether Israel acts alone, and the speaker vote in the Knesset (Israel's parliament).
