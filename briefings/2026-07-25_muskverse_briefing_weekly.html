TESLAEarnings22 – 24 Jul 2026

Record revenue, a hard EPS miss, negative free cash flow — TSLA lost roughly $140B in market value in the two trading days after the print

Tesla reported second-quarter 2026 results after market close on 22 July: revenue of $28.24 billion (up 26% year over year, beating the ~$26.4B consensus this briefing flagged last week), against non-GAAP earnings per share (EPS) of $0.33 — a roughly 38% miss against the ~$0.53 consensus. Automotive gross margin excluding regulatory credits came in around 16.3–16.8% depending on the source, below the higher end of pre-earnings chatter. Operating income fell 57% year over year to $398 million, operating margin collapsed to 1.4% from 4.1%, and free cash flow turned negative at -$1.09 billion — Tesla's first cash-burning quarter since early 2024 — as capital expenditure more than doubled to $5.79 billion.

The stock's reaction was the sharpest of any Tesla earnings print this briefing has tracked: TSLA closed at $319.69 on 23 July, down 14.52% from the prior close of $374.01, with Electrek separately framing the move as a 12% single-day drop erasing roughly $140 billion in market capitalisation — the two figures likely reflect different reference points, but both describe the same order of magnitude. The stock fell a further 3.2% on 24 July on continued margin and rate-sensitivity concerns, extending the slide to a third consecutive session.

Last week's edition closed its Tesla earnings preview by noting that options traders were pricing a wider move (6.4–7.6%) than the average analyst's implied upside (7.6% to the $408.07 target) — itself a signal that the sell-side's price targets were not doing the work of expressing genuine uncertainty. Die Details. The number that did move the stock is the one last week's edition specifically flagged as the real information event: automotive gross margin ex-credits, which landed around 16.3–16.8% depending on the source — below Electrek's Q1 baseline framing of "12.5%" is actually an improvement quarter over quarter, but it fell short of the higher, roughly 17–18% figure some pre-earnings technical notes had been citing, which is exactly the ambiguity this briefing flagged as making the print a genuine event rather than a formality. The more structurally important numbers are the ones beneath margin: operating income down 57% to $398 million even as revenue grew 26%, meaning costs are now growing dramatically faster than sales; operating expenses up 47% to $4.35 billion; regulatory credit revenue down 67% to $146 million, continuing the collapse this briefing has tracked since the federal EV (electric vehicle) purchase incentive lapsed; and free cash flow swinging to -$1.09 billion as capital expenditure more than doubled to $5.79 billion. Tesla's own framing, per the earnings call, is that 2026 is "the largest investment period" in company history, with $25 billion-plus in 2026 capital expenditure committed across six new factories, artificial intelligence (AI) infrastructure, and Optimus manufacturing capacity — a deliberate choice to burn cash now against a set of unproven future revenue lines.

Einordnung. This is the single clearest data point yet for the thesis this briefing has tracked across multiple editions: that TSLA is no longer trading primarily as an automaker, but as a bet on whether robotaxi and Optimus revenue arrives before the company's cash position and cost structure force a reckoning. A 14.5% single-day move on a revenue beat is not how the market treats a car company's earnings; it is how the market treats a growth story that just had its central premise — that scale would eventually translate into profit — directly contradicted by the numbers. The Needham analyst line captured in this week's reporting, that "Tesla is asking investors to fund an increasingly capital intense growth strategy as earnings power weakens," is as concise a summary of the bear case as this briefing has seen stated by a sell-side voice rather than an outside critic. Set against SpaceX's Starship finally flying the same week (see below), the portfolio's pattern holds: the hardware side of the Muskverse is demonstrating real progress on individual milestones while the financial and safety-validation side of the same ventures keeps deteriorating.

SPACEXFlew24 Jul 2026

Starship Flight 13 finally launched on its third attempt — the in-space Raptor relight worked, but sourcing conflicts sharply on how clean the booster's return really was

After aborting at T-0 on 16 July and slipping past a 23 July weather delay, Starship Flight 13 launched from Starbase, Texas at 6:51pm Eastern Time (22:51 UTC) on 24 July. Ship 40 deployed all 20 Starlink V3 satellites and successfully reignited a Raptor engine in the vacuum of space — the flight's core technical objective, and the prerequisite milestone for every subsequent Artemis lunar-lander step. The Ship then completed what SpaceX described as the softest Starship splashdown ever achieved, coming down intact in the Indian Ocean and continuing to transmit telemetry; Musk confirmed on X that "Starship is intact, floating in the ocean and transmitting telemetry."

Sourcing diverges sharply on the Super Heavy booster's Gulf of Mexico landing burn. One detailed account reports only 5 of 13 planned engines fired during the landing burn, causing a hard water impact rather than a soft splashdown; a separate account describes all 33 Raptor 3 engines performing "flawlessly" and a fully controlled splashdown. This briefing cannot reconcile the two and flags the discrepancy rather than picking a side — a pattern consistent with the inconsistent SPCX-adjacent reporting this briefing has noted since the June IPO (initial public offering).

Kontext & Vorgeschichte. Last week's edition closed the Starship story with a week-long delay against an Artemis calendar with no slack, NASA's Blue Origin fallback sharpening every day the vehicle stayed grounded, and a four-day engine-swap turnaround on Booster 20 held up as a genuine, if narrow, data point about Starbase's operational maturity. Die Details. The mission's core technical objective — demonstrating an in-space Raptor relight — succeeded without dispute across every source reviewed, which matters more than any single splashdown outcome: it is the specific capability NASA's Human Landing System dependency chain needs before orbital refuelling tests, lunar transfer profiles, and ultimately a crewed Artemis IV landing can proceed. All 20 Starlink V3 satellites deployed cleanly, extending the operational validation of SpaceX's next-generation satellite bus. Where sourcing breaks down is the booster: Space.com's detailed account describes a landing burn where only 5 of 13 planned engines fired, producing a hard, high-velocity water impact rather than a controlled splashdown — consistent with the May 2026 flight, where the booster's return burn also failed and it hit the Gulf uncontrolled. A separate, less detailed account claims all 33 Raptor 3 engines performed flawlessly and describes the booster splashdown itself as the softest ever achieved. Given that one of the two accounts also carried SPCX stock-price figures (a claimed 74% drawdown and $1 trillion of erased market value) that are wildly inconsistent with every other source's much smaller ~50%-off-peak figure, this briefing weights the more internally consistent account — successful ship splashdown, troubled booster — as more credible, without treating either as confirmed.

Einordnung. Even under the more cautious reading, this is SpaceX's most significant Starship result in months: the flight's one genuinely load-bearing objective for NASA's calendar succeeded, on the third attempt, after a week-plus of additional delay. That is enough to keep the Artemis dependency chain alive without resolving the underlying question this briefing has tracked since the original abort — whether Raptor 3's ignition reliability is now solved or merely patched. A booster that cannot reliably complete its own landing burn, if the harsher account is accurate, means Starship's path to rapid reusability — the entire economic premise behind its Starlink V3 capacity step-change — remains unproven even as its lunar-relevant capability advances. The stock, for its part, closed at $115.07 on 24 July, down 2.68% on the day and roughly 50% off its post-IPO peak — a decline that appears to reflect pre-launch positioning and the same-week Tesla selloff more than any post-launch reaction, since the market close preceded the evening launch.

Watch-Items. (1) An authoritative, single-source reconciliation of the booster landing-burn engine count — the single most important unresolved technical fact from this flight. (2) Whether SpaceX's own post-flight technical readout, once published, confirms either account. (3) Any NASA statement crediting this flight toward Artemis IV schedule confidence. (4) SPCX price action in the first full trading session after the launch (Monday, 27 July), the first real market reaction window. (5) Timeline to Flight 14 and whether the booster issue, if real, triggers another multi-week delay.

edit_noteEditor's view

A three-day window that delivered a year's worth of binary events — and mostly confirmed the reads this briefing already had

1. Tesla's earnings crash is the cleanest evidence yet that the market has stopped grading on deliveries. A 26% revenue beat and a record delivery quarter produced a 14.5% one-day stock decline because profitability — the thing deliveries were supposed to eventually translate into — went the wrong direction instead, with free cash flow turning negative for the first time since early 2024. This is the bear case this briefing has tracked for weeks, now expressed in the loudest single-day price move of the year.

2. Starship flew, and that matters more than the disputed booster outcome. The in-space Raptor relight — the one objective this briefing has consistently flagged as the actual load-bearing milestone for Artemis — succeeded without dispute. Whatever happened to the booster's landing burn, SpaceX bought itself real credibility with NASA on the third attempt, after a cumulative eight-day slip from the original target.

3. The robotaxi fleet-count mystery quietly resolved in the direction this briefing expected. Tesla's own earnings-call language — cumulative miles and growth percentages instead of a hard current fleet number — reads far closer to Electrek's small-fleet estimate than to Bank of America's larger one. Management never said so directly, which is itself informative: a company confident in a larger number usually states it.

4. Boring's reported funding round is the sharpest contrast available in the portfolio right now. In the same week Tesla shed $140B and SpaceX drifted to half its post-IPO peak, a private, unlisted Musk venture with no earnings calls and no AI narrative is reportedly being priced up more than 3x since 2022. That is not a coincidence about which businesses are exposed to quarterly scrutiny and safety-critical validation, and which are not.

5. Musk's "carried away" admission is personal, not substantive — and still notable. It is the first time in this briefing's coverage that Musk has conceded overreach on DOGE/Trump in his own words, even while continuing to defend the initiative's record. Read against the America Party's two-week stall from last edition, the throughline is a Musk whose political bandwidth — not conviction — appears to be genuinely receding.

6. What would change the read. SPCX's first full post-launch trading session on Monday, 27 July, is the most immediate test available — a run at $150 would say the market believes Thursday's flight was a clean technical win; continued drift would say either the harsher booster account is gaining credence, or Tesla-driven sentiment is now the dominant force on SPCX regardless of SpaceX-specific news. Neither is resolved as of this edition.

Three days, not seven — but three days that delivered the year's sharpest Tesla stock move, Starship's most consequential flight since its abort streak began, and the first personal political concession this briefing has recorded from Musk himself. The next edition, a full week out, should have room to judge whether any of these three threads actually changed direction, or whether — consistent with the pattern this briefing tracked through most of July — the gap between event and resolution simply widened again.
