SpaceX — the bull case and the bear case, walked through

Two months after the largest IPO in history, the stock trades slightly below its issue price while sell-side consensus implies roughly 74% upside. Both readings are held by serious people with real numbers behind them. This lays out each at full strength, checks the figures against filings, and discloses which of the bullish banks were paid to sell the offering.

What you are actually buying

The single biggest reframe. On 2 February 2026 SpaceX acquired xAI outright in an all-stock merger — not a minority investment, not a partnership. xAI, X and the cloud business are now a fully consolidated third segment inside SpaceX. Analysis that still treats this as "rockets plus satellites" is describing a company that no longer exists in that form.

The Q2 2026 revenue split shows how far that has gone:

Launch — the business the company is famous for — is now the smallest segment by some distance. The investment question is no longer "can they fly rockets cheaply." It is whether a satellite broadband operator bolted to a frontier AI lab, inside a launch company, is worth $1.75 trillion.

Analyst estimates put 2026 capex near $46bn and do not see free cash flow turning positive before 2029. AI infrastructure consumed $12.7bn in 2025 — more than Space and Connectivity combined.

The bull case, at full strength

Stated as its most credible advocates would state it, before any rebuttal.

Three separate trillion-dollar markets, one cost structure

Satellite connectivity, orbital launch and frontier AI are each enormous end-markets, and SpaceX is the leading or co-leading player in all three simultaneously. No other company holds that position. Goldman's initiation rests precisely on this: three segments, each addressing markets measured in trillions, sharing engineering talent, capital and — critically — launch capacity that the company sells to itself at marginal cost.

The vertical integration is not a slogan

A Starlink satellite costs roughly $500,000 to build and roughly $3m to launch — but that launch is bought from itself. Every competitor deploying a constellation must purchase launch on the open market from ULA, Arianespace or Blue Origin, at prices set partly by SpaceX's own capacity. Amazon's constellation flies on Atlas V. The advantage compounds: more launches lower unit cost, which lowers constellation cost, which funds more launches.

Starlink is already a real, profitable, fast-growing business

Not a promise — a P&L. The Connectivity segment did $11.39bn in 2025, up 49.8%, with a 63% segment adjusted-EBITDA margin and $4.4bn of GAAP operating income. Subscribers doubled in twelve months to 12 million. It has been cash-flow positive since 2024, and it is the segment financing everything else.

The high-ARPU mix is shifting the right way

Consumer broadband is the low-margin end. Aviation runs about $300,000 per aircraft per year, with 7,500+ aircraft installed or contracted across 46+ airlines. Maritime runs roughly $34,000 per vessel. Government and defence — Starshield — won over $6bn of new contracts in 2026 alone, including $2.29bn for a Space Force data network and $4.16bn for air-target tracking. These are high-margin, politically protected and largely invisible to competitors.

Optionality that is genuinely hard to price

Direct-to-cell with T-Mobile is live in 22 countries with 650+ capable satellites. Starship, if fully reusable, changes launch economics by an order of magnitude — target costs of $67–100/kg against roughly $250–600/kg today. Orbital compute sits on top of all of it. Morgan Stanley's Adam Jonas builds his $300 target on revenue reaching $319bn by 2030 and $3.3tn by 2040. You do not have to accept those numbers to accept the shape: a company with three credible paths to enormous scale is not valued like a normal business.

CheckTrue C 5/5 — Contracted backlog stands at $47.5bn (Q2 2026 release). NASA HLS is worth up to $4.5bn with ~$2.7bn already paid against 49 milestones; the Space Force NSSL Phase 3 Lane 2 award gives SpaceX roughly 28 of 54 missions, about $5.9bn. Cumulative US federal contracts are put at ~$22bn by a third-party database (C 2/5 on that last figure).

The bear case, at equal strength

Not the mirror image of the bull case — a different argument, held by named people with their own arithmetic.

The valuation requires a future that has to arrive almost perfectly

At roughly $1.75tn on $18.7bn of 2025 revenue, the multiple is approximately 85–95× sales, with reported EV/EBITDA around 873×. Mega-cap technology companies typically trade in the low-to-mid double digits on sales. Whitney Tilson — who publishes an investment newsletter and therefore has his own commercial interest in high-conviction calls, noted here for the same reason the underwriters' fees are — calls it "the most overvalued large-cap stock of all time" and does the arithmetic plainly: even at a generous $50bn run-rate by end-2026 and a 10× sales multiple, you get $500bn — roughly a trillion dollars below where it trades. Morningstar's independent fair value is $62–63, against a street consensus of $231.

The cash consumption is structural, not transitional

This is the number that matters most and gets discussed least. Free cash flow was −$13.95bn in 2025; Q2 2026 capex alone was $18.4bn. The AI segment is the most capital-hungry business in the world right now, and SpaceX has entered it at full stride with a planned data-centre programme reported at up to $119bn. The company holds $100bn of cash — which is a lot until you set it against that burn rate.

Roughly 78% of the IPO money was spoken for before it arrived

Of about $80bn in planned proceeds, approximately $62.8bn was pre-committed to repaying Valor Equity Partners, servicing X Corp/xAI investor debt, and closing the EchoStar spectrum deal. Under $18bn remained for actual expansion. Proceeds raised in a growth offering were substantially applied to refinancing existing obligations.

Governance is unusually bad, and institutions have said so on the record

Musk holds roughly 42% of equity but 82.4% of votes through a dual-class structure. Public shareholders have economic exposure and effectively no governance rights. The Danish pension fund AkademikerPension blacklisted the stock before the IPO on governance grounds and valuation, stating it would not invest at a lower price either. CalPERS, the New York State Common Retirement Fund and the NYC Comptroller jointly objected. MSCI rates governance 3.2/10 and the overall ESG grade CCC.

Key-person risk that the company itself flags and does not insure

The S-1 states plainly that SpaceX is highly dependent on Musk, carries no key-man insurance on him, and concedes he does not devote his full time and attention to the company. He simultaneously runs Tesla, has been reassigning executives from Starlink and Tesla into xAI, and has publicly floated a Tesla–SpaceX merger — a transaction in which he would sit on both sides of the table.

The S&P 500 door stays shut

Nasdaq-100 inclusion arrived on 7 July 2026 under a relaxed rule. The S&P 500 is a different matter: S&P Dow Jones Indices declined a fast-track change in early June and reaffirmed its GAAP profitability requirement. On a $4.94bn net loss, SpaceX does not qualify, and the earliest realistic eligibility is mid-2027. A large block of index-driven buying that many investors assumed was coming is not coming yet.

No significant secondary selling has been documented yet, which is a genuinely positive signal — but three weeks is not a test.

The conflict, stated plainly. Six of the most bullish houses — Goldman, Morgan Stanley, BofA, Citi, Wells Fargo and Cantor — were underwriters on the offering, sharing a fee pool reported at around $500m. Their coverage began when the quiet period expired in early July, which is the standard mechanism by which a wave of Buy ratings follows an IPO. This does not make them wrong. It does mean their ratings are not independent evidence, and the two most negative published views — HSBC at Hold and Morningstar's $62 fair value — both come from houses with no underwriting relationship.

The spread between Morningstar's $62 and Raymond James's $800 is among the widest I found for a company of this size. That is not a sign of a market efficiently pricing a known business. It is a sign that nobody agrees on what this company is.

Other viewsThe analogies being traded. The most common bull framing is Amazon 1997 — unprofitable, expensive, unproven. The rebuttal is arithmetic: Amazon listed at a $438m valuation, roughly 4,000× smaller than SpaceX's entry point, and reached profitability on a path visible at the time. Saudi Aramco 2019 is invoked for IPO scale, but Aramco was highly profitable at listing. And the "get in early like Tesla" frame fails on its own terms — SpaceX is already worth more than Tesla, so there is no early left to be.

Starlink specifically — the case both ways

The segment carrying the company. Roughly 55% of Q2 revenue and the only durably profitable business inside the group.

For

Subscribers doubled year on year to 12 million across 160+ countries, with 1.7m net additions in Q2 alone. The segment produced $4.4bn of GAAP operating income in 2025 on a 63% adjusted-EBITDA margin. Competition is further behind than the headlines suggest: Amazon's constellation had only 210–302 satellites in orbit against an FCC obligation of 1,618 by 30 July 2026, forcing an extension request and 22 additional contracted launches. Chinese constellations plan more than 28,000 satellites but had launched roughly 350. Starlink has over 10,800 operational.

Against

ARPU is falling and the company says it will keep falling. Consumer ARPU dropped from about $86 to $66 per month between Q1 2025 and Q1 2026. Starlink has been cutting aggressively — introductory pricing near $39/month, hardware from $499 to $249. The S-1 states outright that ARPU is expected to decline further as international and discounted customers grow as a share. Growth is increasingly bought with volume, not price.

The replacement treadmill is the most underrated bear point. Satellites last five to seven years, and the fleet exceeds 10,800. Estimated annual replacement capex runs around $8bn — roughly a quarter of revenue on some models. Crucially, the 63% segment EBITDA margin does not include capex at all, so the headline margin systematically flatters the economics. And SpaceX deorbited roughly 500 satellites younger than five years in the first half of 2025, which suggests real service life sits at the bottom of that range or below.

The genuine competitive threat is not Amazon. It is AST SpaceMobile in direct-to-cell — not on fleet size, where it is far behind, but on distribution: 50+ mobile-operator agreements covering roughly 3 billion subscribers, including AT&T, Verizon and Vodafone, which are precisely the US carriers not aligned with the Starlink/T-Mobile partnership, plus $1.2bn of contracted revenue commitments.

Regulatory exposure is real and growing. A French court revoked Starlink's operating licence in 2026 on monopolisation grounds. Service is barred in China, Russia, North Korea, Syria, Afghanistan and Belarus, and several African states have blocked licensing or marketing.

Other viewsDirect-to-cell is where the bull case is thinnest. Market sizing estimates vary by more than a factor of ten — from roughly $6bn a year on a sceptical link-budget analysis to $93bn cumulative on the most bullish. Notably, Morgan Stanley itself — the house with the $300 target on SPCX — has written that the perceived risk of Starlink Mobile disrupting US wireless is greater than the actual risk. The most bullish bank on the stock is publicly cautious on its biggest optionality claim; readers can weigh that themselves.

Starlink is a real business with real profits and a real lead. The question is not whether it works, but whether it grows into a valuation that assumes it becomes something considerably larger than satellite broadband.

Stepping back

The error pattern here is different from a single-author piece. There was no author to be wrong — but there is a clear directional bias in the source material: the most optimistic published numbers come disproportionately from institutions that were paid to place the stock, and the most sceptical from those with no fee at stake. Two prior findings from this archive — the 78% pre-commitment of proceeds and the S&P 500 exclusion until roughly mid-2027 — were independently confirmed, which raises confidence in the June briefing. The one material correction to my own earlier framing is the xAI merger: I had it as an investment; it is a full consolidation.

What the market is telling you

The stock rose 19% on day one, peaked at $225.64, and now trades around $133–134 — slightly below its $135 issue price. Consensus says $231. Either roughly 35 analysts are collectively right and the market is mispricing a mega-cap by 74%, or the market has looked at the same filings and reached a different conclusion. Both happen. A consensus that far above a liquid, heavily-covered large cap is unusual either way, and the reason for the gap is worth establishing before weighting either side of it.

The four things that would actually move this

  • GAAP profitability. It unlocks S&P 500 eligibility and the passive flows that come with it. Watch the trajectory of the net loss — Q2's narrowing from −$4.3bn to −$541m is the most encouraging single data point in the filings, though I could not verify how much of that came from one-off merger effects.
  • Starship full reusability and orbital refuelling. Not demonstrated. Flight 13 in July 2026 achieved in-space engine relight, a step toward it. Everything in the launch-cost bull case depends on this.
  • The remaining lock-up tranches in late August and September, and the first 13F filings due mid-August, which will show what institutions actually did rather than what they said.
  • ARPU stabilisation at Starlink. If ARPU keeps falling while subscriber growth decelerates, the compounding story weakens quickly.

What could not be substantiated

  • The FY2025 segment figures do not sum to total revenue — Connectivity plus Space is $15.47bn against $18.67bn reported. The remainder is unattributed in what I could access.
  • How much of Q2's dramatic loss narrowing was one-off merger accounting versus operating improvement.
  • Company FY2026 guidance — none located.
  • 13F positioning — filings not yet available at time of writing.
  • Government share of total revenue as a separate line.
  • Positions of several bears often cited in this context — Chanos, Burry, Grantham — no specific SpaceX position found for any of them. The named bear case rests substantially on Tilson, Morningstar and HSBC.

What survives on each side

  • Bull, strongest point: Starlink is a genuinely profitable, fast-growing business with a structural cost advantage no competitor can replicate, and the competition is measurably further behind than the narrative suggests.
  • Bear, strongest point: The cash burn is structural and enormous, roughly 78% of the IPO money went to refinancing rather than growth, and at 85–95× sales the valuation requires near-flawless execution across three capital-intensive frontiers at once.
  • Both agree on: governance is poor and uninsured against its single point of failure. Bulls treat that as the price of the man's track record; bears treat it as an unpriced risk. That is a judgement about Musk, not about the numbers.

What to discount

  • Underwriter price targets as independent evidence. Six of the top nine came from banks sharing a ~$500m fee pool. Read them; don't count them.
  • Adjusted EBITDA as a profitability measure for a business whose defining characteristic is capex.
  • Segment EBITDA margins at Starlink without deducting the ~$8bn annual replacement treadmill.
  • The Amazon 1997 analogy. The entry valuation is roughly 4,000× different.
  • Direct-to-cell sizing in either direction — the range spans more than a factor of ten, and Morgan Stanley is publicly cautious on it while holding a $300 target on the stock.
  • Tilson's multiple as a valuation. A generous run-rate times a round 10× is a directional argument, not a model. It shows the gap is large; it does not establish that $500bn is the right number.
  • The breadth of the named bear case. It rests substantially on three sources — Tilson, Morningstar and HSBC. Several bears commonly invoked in this context turned out to have no documented SpaceX position at all.
  • The $8bn replacement-capex figure is an analyst estimate, not a disclosed number, and the assumed satellite service life driving it is itself contested.
  • Single-agency governance scores. MSCI's CCC grade is one methodology among several, and ESG rating methodologies disagree with each other more than they agree.
