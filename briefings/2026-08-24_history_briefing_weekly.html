10 years ago2016Amatrice earthquake · Proxima Centauri b
DISASTER2016August 24, 2016

A magnitude-6.2 earthquake levels Amatrice and kills 299 in central Italy

Shortly after 3:36 a.m., a shallow magnitude-6.2 earthquake strikes the central Apennines near Accumoli, flattening the historic town of Amatrice and damaging villages across the Umbria, Lazio, Abruzzo and Marche regions. "Amatrice is not here anymore, half of the town is destroyed," the town's mayor tells reporters within hours.

The death toll, finalized weeks later, reaches 299; more than 2,500 aftershocks follow over the next week, and the region is struck by two further major quakes that October.

Context & background. The central Apennines sit on one of Italy's most seismically active belts, where extensional faulting along the mountain crest has produced repeated destructive quakes, including the 2009 L'Aquila earthquake 45 km to the south, which killed over 300 people and displaced 65,000.

The details. Rescue teams pulled 238 people alive from the rubble in the first days, 215 of them by the Vigili del Fuoco fire corps. A subsequent investigation by the Rieti public prosecutor found that not all Amatrice buildings had been built or retrofitted to the antiseismic standards in force since 1974 — a family died under the rubble of a church that had not been reinforced, and the town's elementary school partially collapsed despite a 2013 seismic retrofit that fell short of 2012 standards.

Why it still matters. Amatrice is still cited in Italian debates over enforcement of seismic building codes, and the region was struck again on 30 October 2016 by a magnitude-6.6 quake, the strongest in Italy since 1980. A Charlie Hebdo cartoon mocking the victims as pasta dishes days later triggered a separate diplomatic row between Paris and Rome.

SCIENCE2016August 24, 2016

Astronomers announce the closest exoplanet ever found, orbiting Proxima Centauri

A European Southern Observatory team led by Guillermo Anglada-Escudé announces Proxima Centauri b — a roughly Earth-mass planet orbiting the Sun's nearest stellar neighbour every 11.2 days, inside its star's habitable zone. The discovery, built on years of Doppler-spectroscopy data from the Pale Red Dot project, is confirmed the same day the earthquake strikes Italy.

At 4.2 light-years away, it remains, a decade later, the closest known exoplanet to Earth and the most publicly recognisable one.

Context & background. Proxima Centauri had been searched for planets since the 2000s; early surveys in 2008–09 ruled out anything larger than Earth in its habitable zone. Motion anomalies in the star's spectrum that flares and chromospheric activity could not explain pointed to an unseen companion, prompting the dedicated Pale Red Dot observing campaign launched in January 2016.

The details. Because Proxima Centauri is a flare star bathing the planet in far more ultraviolet and X-ray radiation, and stronger stellar wind, than the Sun gives Earth, whether the planet has kept any atmosphere at all remains unresolved a decade on. It is also likely tidally locked, with one face perpetually toward its star.

Why it still matters. Proxima b anchors the Breakthrough Starshot project's case for interstellar probes and remains the benchmark target for next-generation telescopes such as the James Webb and Nancy Grace Roman observatories attempting to characterise a rocky exoplanet's atmosphere directly.

25 years ago2001Aaliyah's death · killing of Abu Ali Mustafa
CULTURE2001August 25, 2001

R&B singer Aaliyah dies at 22 when an overloaded plane crashes in the Bahamas

Minutes after taking off from Marsh Harbour Airport in the Abaco Islands, a twin-engine Cessna 402 carrying 22-year-old singer Aaliyah and eight others — cast and crew who had just wrapped the video shoot for "Rock the Boat" — crashes and bursts into flames. All nine aboard are killed.

Investigators later find the aircraft was roughly 700 pounds over its weight limit and that the pilot, who was not certified to fly that aircraft type, had cocaine and alcohol in his system.

Context & background. Aaliyah had just released her third, self-titled album days earlier and was filming in the Bahamas for a music video; the flight back to Florida was chartered through the production, using a smaller aircraft than had been booked after an earlier flight was delayed.

The details. The plane went down less than a minute after takeoff. A wrongful-death lawsuit filed by Aaliyah's family against the production company that arranged the transport was settled for an undisclosed sum in 2003.

Why it still matters. Aaliyah's death at the height of a crossover breakthrough — "Rock the Boat" and her fourth album were still being promoted — froze her critical reputation early and fed a posthumous influence on R&B and pop production that critics still trace through the 2010s and 2020s.

CONFLICT2001August 27, 2001

Israel kills PFLP leader Abu Ali Mustafa in a helicopter missile strike

Two Israeli Apache helicopters fire rockets through the office windows of Abu Ali Mustafa, Secretary-General of the Popular Front for the Liberation of Palestine, as he sits at his desk in Al-Bireh in the West Bank. He is killed instantly, the highest-ranking Palestinian official targeted for assassination since the Second Intifada began eleven months earlier.

Israel says Mustafa was building a PFLP network among Palestinians in East Jerusalem; the Palestinian Authority calls the strike a declaration of "all-out war."

Context & background. Mustafa, a former Arab Nationalist Movement organiser who co-founded the PFLP in 1967, had returned to the West Bank in 1999 under an Arafat-Barak deal and was elected the group's Secretary-General in July 2000. Israel held him responsible for a string of PFLP car-bomb attacks during his tenure.

The details. Thousands attended his funeral procession in Ramallah, including rival Palestinian factions and, notably, leaders of Hamas and Islamic Jihad marching together. Seven weeks later, on 17 October 2001, PFLP member Hamdi Quran assassinated Israeli tourism minister Rehavam Ze'evi in explicit revenge for Mustafa's killing.

Why it still matters. The PFLP renamed its West Bank armed wing the Abu Ali Mustafa Brigades in his memory, and the strike is repeatedly cited as an early, high-profile case in what became Israel's sustained policy of targeted killings of militant leaders — a tactic still debated, and still used, more than two decades later.

50 years ago1976Raymond Barre named PM · the Soufrière blast
POLITICS1976August 25, 1976

Raymond Barre replaces Jacques Chirac as France's prime minister

President Valéry Giscard d'Estaing accepts Jacques Chirac's resignation as prime minister and, the same day, appoints Raymond Barre — a little-known economics professor and outgoing minister of foreign trade — to replace him, presenting him to the French public as "the best economist in France." Barre also takes the economy and finance portfolio, the only person under the Fifth Republic to hold both jobs at once.

He will govern for nearly five years, longer than any prime minister of the era, and become the face of French austerity economics.

Context & background. Chirac had clashed with Giscard d'Estaing over the direction of economic policy and the neo-Gaullist Rally for the Republic he was about to found; his resignation opened the way for a technocratic outsider seen as above party politics — Barre had never held elected office and was not a party member.

The details. Barre's first austerity package, the "Barre Plan," followed weeks later on 22 September 1976: a three-month price freeze, VAT cuts, wage and salary controls, tighter money supply, and higher income, car and luxury taxes, aimed above all at inflation. He mocked union protesters as "the bearers of banners" and told them to work rather than grouse.

Why it still matters. Barre stayed prime minister until Giscard's 1981 election defeat, then ran for president himself in 1988, finishing third. His technocratic style and the term "Barrisme" remain a reference point in French debates over economic austerity delivered by unelected experts.

SCIENCE1976August 30, 1976

A volcanic blast nearly kills France's two top volcanologists atop La Soufrière

Fifteen days after the evacuation of 73,600 people from southern Basse-Terre, Guadeloupe, volcanologists Haroun Tazieff and Claude Allègre climb to the summit of La Soufrière together to assess the risk. Near the Tarissan crater, a sudden explosion showers them with rock blocks up to a cubic metre in size for thirteen minutes; Tazieff and two colleagues are hospitalised with light injuries, the rest evacuated by helicopter.

The near-miss becomes the opening chapter of a public feud between the two men over whether the volcano was actually dangerous — a dispute that split French volcanology for decades.

Context & background. La Soufrière had been rumbling since a first phreatic explosion on 8 July 1976; Tazieff, already a celebrity volcanologist, visited early and judged the risk minor. Allègre, newly installed as director of the Institut de physique du globe, argued the opposite — that a magmatic eruption was a real possibility — and the prefect ordered the mass evacuation of Basse-Terre's southern tip on 15 August on the strength of that warning.

The details. The two men climbed together on 30 August specifically to settle the disagreement on the ground. The explosion that nearly killed them produced no fresh magma in analysis afterward, which Tazieff took as vindication; Allègre maintained the danger had been real. Tazieff was later removed from his post over the dispute and successfully sued Allègre for wrongful dismissal; Allègre later won a defamation suit against Tazieff in turn.

Why it still matters. No magmatic eruption ever followed — the 1976 crisis is now understood as phreatic, not magmatic — but the evacuation cost Guadeloupe an estimated equivalent of hundreds of millions of euros and the Tazieff-Allègre feud became a textbook case, still taught in France, of what happens when experts disagree publicly during a live disaster.

Editor's view

Half of this week's six events turn on a safety judgment made under incomplete information, with the person making the call getting it wrong in different directions. Tazieff downplayed La Soufrière's danger and was nearly killed proving it; the 1976 evacuation he doubted may have been the more defensible call even though no eruption came. Amatrice's elementary school passed a seismic retrofit that still fell short of the standard that mattered in 2016. Aaliyah's charter flight went ahead 700 pounds over weight with an unqualified pilot at the controls. None of the three is really a story about villainy — each is a story about someone, or some system, deciding a risk was acceptable, and being tested on it within days or minutes.
