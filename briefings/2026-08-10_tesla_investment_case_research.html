Tesla — the bull case and the bear case, walked through

In the last reported quarter deliveries grew for the first time in two years, revenue hit a record, and operating margin fell to 1.4%. Published analyst targets run from $25 to $600 — a spread without parallel among mega-caps. Both readings rest on the same filings and reach opposite conclusions. This lays out each at full strength and checks the figures.

The bull case, at full strength

As its strongest named advocates make it.

The margin compression is a decision, not a failure

Operating margin fell because capex rose 142% and operating expenses 47%, both driven by AI infrastructure, Dojo, robotaxi manufacturing and Optimus tooling. A company harvesting a mature auto business would show the opposite: fat margins and no investment. Tesla is deliberately trading current earnings for a position in physical AI, and management said plainly it prioritises record deliveries, FSD growth and robotaxi expansion over near-term margin. Judging it on this quarter's operating margin is judging the wrong variable.

The demand problem appears to be turning

After two down years, deliveries grew 25% year on year and 34% sequentially. Revenue set a record. Services grew 50% at record gross margin — a high-quality, recurring revenue line that gets almost no attention. The refreshed lineup and a cheaper Cybertruck variant are only beginning to contribute.

Energy is a real, fast, high-margin business

13.5 GWh deployed in the quarter, up 41%, the second best quarter ever. Gross profit rose to $846m even as average selling prices fell — margin per unit improving under price pressure. Reported Megapack bookings of roughly 43 GWh worth $9bn in a six-week span point to a backlog that is not speculative. Lathrop and Shanghai each run around 40 GWh a year with a third factory coming.

Robotaxi is deployed, not promised

Driverless paid rides now operate across Austin, the Bay Area, Dallas and Houston, with Miami added — roughly 500 vehicles and around 700,000 cumulative paid miles. Bulls argue the decisive variable is not who is technically ahead today but who can scale: a camera-only stack costs a fraction of a lidar rig and can in principle be activated across millions of vehicles already on the road. Waymo must build and deploy every car it operates.

The sum-of-the-parts logic

Dan Ives (Wedbush) holds a $600 target with an $800 bull case, valuing FSD subscriptions, robotaxi fleet revenue and Optimus as separate businesses rather than automotive line items. Pierre Ferragu (New Street) is also at $600 on the autonomy lead alone. Bank of America's sum-of-the-parts model to 2040 puts 45% of total value in the robotaxi platform. Gene Munster expects up to 40% of operating profit from robotaxi and FSD licensing by 2030. On this framing, the auto business is the customer-acquisition channel, not the product.

CheckThe highest targets rest on pre-revenue components C 2/5 — The $600 and $800 figures from Ives and Ferragu value FSD subscriptions, robotaxi fleet revenue and Optimus as standalone businesses. Two of those three currently generate no meaningful profit, and one ships no product commercially. That does not make the targets wrong — option value is real — but they are forecasts of businesses that do not yet exist at scale, not multiples on current earnings.
CheckTrue C 3/5 — The energy figures hold: 13.5 GWh (+41%), gross profit $846m, and FY2025 storage revenue of $12.8bn at a 29.8% gross margin on 46.7 GWh (+48%). One conflict left open: two reports give Q2 energy gross margin as 20.4% and 39.5% respectively. I could not reconcile them and am not choosing one.

The bear case, at equal strength

You are paying a software multiple for hardware economics

Trailing P/E around 300×, forward roughly 170–185×. General Motors trades near 6× forward, Toyota near 10×. Toyota earned roughly six times Tesla's profit last year while Tesla's market capitalisation exceeds the next 37 automakers combined. Profit per vehicle has fallen to about $2,140, down 40% year on year, converging on Toyota's $2,078 — at a fraction of Toyota's valuation multiple.

The growth quarter was bought

Deliveries rose 25%, but from a base depressed by two down years, and with average discounts around $3,197 on the Model 3, 0% 72-month financing, and the loss of the $7,500 federal credit absorbed rather than passed through. The result is visible one line down: operating margin of 1.4%. Volume was at least partly purchased with price — though the bull reading attributes the same margin line to investment rather than discounting, and neither side can fully separate the two effects from outside.

Competition is winning where it counts

BYD sold 557,090 battery-electric vehicles in Q2 2026 against Tesla's 480,126 total deliveries. In FY2025 BYD sold 2.26m BEVs to Tesla's 1.64m, and nearly three times as many vehicles including hybrids. In Europe both hold about 2.4% share — but BYD came from 1.0% while Tesla fell from 1.6%, and BYD led every month of 2026. Chinese brands took 10.7% of Western European sales in Q2, up from 5.7%.

Brand damage is measurable, not anecdotal

European sales fell 49% year on year in April 2026, a thirteenth consecutive monthly decline. A July 2026 survey of 1,861 respondents across all 27 EU states found 39% say Musk negatively affects their perception of Tesla; when reminded of his political donations, his net favourability moved from −31 to −47 and purchase intent to −40. Estimated brand value fell from $66.2bn in January 2023 to $27.61bn. Analysts are careful to note an ageing model line and Chinese competition also contribute — the Musk effect is real but not the sole cause.

The attention question is no longer theoretical

Since xAI merged into SpaceX in February 2026, Musk controls an entity worth more than Tesla. That entity has been losing people: all eleven original xAI co-founders have departed, along with 50+ researchers, and Musk himself said xAI was "not built right" and needed rebuilding from the ground up. A chief executive occupied with rebuilding a frontier AI lab plausibly has less attention left for automotive margin — an inference from the departures and his own statement, not something Tesla or Musk has confirmed.

The named bears, and how far apart they are

Gordon Johnson (GLJ Research) carries a $25.28 target — implying over 90% downside — on expiring credits, weak China and an expected 15% delivery decline in 2026. Toni Sacconaghi (Bernstein) has been at $120–150. Jim Chanos questions why FSD remains Level 2 after years of promises and calls the Optimus projections implausible. Per Lekander argues the vertically integrated model is "brilliant when growing, deadly when stagnating." David Trainer (New Constructs) says the required return on invested capital exceeds that of the world's most profitable companies.

CheckOne bear indicator is weaker than the rhetoric C 2/5 — Short interest is only about 2.25% of float, historically low for a stock this contested and falling. Whatever the loudest bears say publicly, few are backing it with size. The bear case here is fought through price targets, not positions — which cuts against the "everyone knows it's overvalued" framing.

Two things this table shows that a consensus number hides. First, the distribution is bimodal rather than clustered: 17 Strong Buy and 6 Buy against 18 Hold, 2 Sell and 4 Strong Sell. The average of $397.87 describes almost nobody's actual view. Second — and this is the striking one — JPMorgan moved from Underweight at $145 to Neutral at $475, a shift that coincided with a change in covering analyst from Brinkman to Gupta. Whether that reflects fresh analysis, a different reading of the same facts, or genuine fundamental change in between — deliveries did turn positive and revenue did set a record — cannot be determined from outside the firm. But a 3.3× revision inside one institution is a reminder of how much of this coverage is judgement rather than measurement.

Unlike the SpaceX case, there is no underwriting conflict to disclose here — Tesla has been public since 2010 and these are ordinary coverage relationships. The bias to watch is different: Tesla generates unusually strong analyst and retail identification in both directions, and several of these houses have held their directional view across multiple years and been repeatedly repriced by the stock rather than the reverse.

Robotaxi — the gap between deployment and leadership

Tesla runs roughly 500 vehicles and 700,000 cumulative paid miles. Waymo runs about 500,000 paid rides per week across eleven cities with some 3,500 vehicles, targeting a million a week. Baidu's Apollo Go operates 1,000+ driverless vehicles across 15 cities with over 100 million cumulative rides. On deployed scale Tesla is behind Waymo and the Chinese operators, roughly level with Amazon's Zoox, which only started paid rides this month.

Two specifics bulls should weigh. In California the CPUC confirmed in March 2026 that Tesla holds a limousine permit, not an autonomous-vehicle permit — it cannot legally run a robotaxi service in its home state. And NHTSA escalated its FSD inquiry to an Engineering Analysis in March 2026 covering 3.2 million vehicles, focused on behaviour in glare, fog and dust. Reporting also indicates safety monitors were in some cases moved to a trailing chase car rather than removed outright.

The scale argument for camera-only is genuine and cost-based. The evidence that it currently matches sensor-fusion safety is not there — and Tesla's own crowd-sourced disengagement data is too noisy between releases to establish a trend.

Other viewsThe credibility discount has a decade behind it. Musk has forecast full autonomy roughly annually since 2016 — coast-to-coast by 2017, a million robotaxis by 2020, "feature complete" repeatedly. He acknowledged the pattern himself in 2023, calling himself "the boy who cried FSD." That does not mean the current deployment is not real; it is. It means the appropriate prior on any new Tesla autonomy timeline is materially worse than for a company without that history. On the other side: the service does now exist and carry paying passengers without a driver in the seat, which was not true a year ago.

Optimus — the widest gap between rhetoric and floor

Musk's stated 2026 target is 50,000–100,000 units; as of July the company described installing initial production lines with output starting "later this year." Rodney Brooks, iRobot co-founder, calls the humanoid enthusiasm "pure fantasy thinking," arguing video-trained end-to-end learning cannot substitute for human hand tactility. Meanwhile Agility's Digit is already doing paid warehouse work (100,000+ boxes moved at GXO), Figure carries a $39bn valuation with paid pilots at BMW and Mercedes-Benz, and Unitree shipped 5,500+ units in 2025 from $16,000. Tesla leads on announced manufacturing ambition, not on demonstrated paid deployment.

Energy — real, and not defensible in the way bulls imply

The business works. But Tesla does not make the cells: it buys them from CATL and BYD — direct competitors. CATL holds 27.1% of the global energy-storage cell market; BYD has passed Tesla on deployment volume and shown a 14.5 MWh unit nearly three times a Megapack's capacity. Tesla here is a systems integrator with good products, not a technology moat. That is a perfectly good business — it is simply not the kind that earns a platform multiple, which may be exactly why bulls under-weight it.

Stepping back

The error pattern. Unlike the SpaceX piece, the source bias here does not run one way. The bullish figures come mostly from Tesla's own disclosures, which are audited; the bearish figures come mostly from third parties and competitors' disclosures, which are not directly comparable. Two source conflicts I could not resolve are left open rather than smoothed: market capitalisation (reported between $1.05tn and $1.3tn) and Musk's Tesla stake (20% vs 28.44% depending on source, before any effect of the new pay package). Both are basic facts about a company this size, and the fact that they are unclear is itself worth knowing.

The Tesla–SpaceX merger question

Live, material, and mostly speculative. On the 22 July earnings call Musk pointed to "more and more overlap" between the companies but declined to elaborate, deferring to process through Tesla's general counsel. The named overlaps are concrete: Terafab, the chip fabrication project, which Musk said Tesla needs access to or will be constrained in scaling Optimus production; and Starlink integration into CyberCab for fleet-wide connectivity.

The probability estimates diverge sharply. Dan Ives puts it at 80%. Polymarket prices an official announcement in 2026 at roughly 18%, up from 11% in mid-July on about $1m of volume. When a prominent bull and a liquid prediction market are four-fold apart, the market price is usually the better-calibrated number — though thin volume weakens that. Chanos was dismissive of earlier joint-venture reports as another narrative shift.

Why it matters to a Tesla holder specifically: Musk holds roughly 20–28% of Tesla but around 82% of SpaceX votes. Any combination would be negotiated by a person with materially different economics on each side. That is a governance question a Tesla shareholder cannot vote their way out of.

Compensation and governance

Shareholders approved a $1 trillion, ten-year, twelve-tranche package in November 2025 against the recommendations of Glass Lewis and ISS. In December 2025 the Delaware Supreme Court found the Chancery Court's rescission of the 2018 package "too extreme" and restored it. The new package could raise Musk's voting power from roughly 13% toward 25%.

What could not be substantiated

  • Market cap and Musk's stake — sources conflict materially, as above.
  • YTD and 12-month price performance — not established within the search budget; the stock sits roughly 34% below its 52-week high of $498.83, which is an inference, not a measured return.
  • Q2 energy gross margin — 20.4% vs 39.5% in two reports.
  • Fleet-wide average selling price for Q2 2026 — no reliable single source.
  • Historical annual capex series — one source gave a sequence that contradicts the reported upward trend.
  • A current full sum-of-the-parts from Morgan Stanley — Jonas has handed coverage to Percoco and no updated breakdown was located; the BofA 45%-robotaxi figure is used as the comparable.
  • Board composition changes — not researched.

What survives on each side

  • Bull, strongest: deliveries turned positive after two down years, energy storage is a genuinely profitable and fast-growing business with a visible backlog, and the margin compression is traceable to identifiable investment rather than to demand collapse.
  • Bear, strongest: a 300× trailing multiple sits on a 1.4% operating margin and negative free cash flow, the credit income that flattered past profits is legislatively gone, BYD now outsells Tesla in BEVs, and roughly 45% of the most-cited bull valuation rests on a robotaxi business where Tesla is measurably behind Waymo and cannot legally operate in California.
  • Both agree: the outcome depends almost entirely on autonomy. There is no version of this valuation that works on automotive fundamentals alone, and no version of the bear case that survives a genuine autonomy breakthrough.

What to discount — on both sides

  • Bull: Optimus contribution to any target — it is not shipping commercially. Sum-of-the-parts models discounted from 2040. Any autonomy timeline taken at face value given a decade of missed ones.
  • Bull: "Energy is undervalued" — true, but Tesla buys its cells from CATL and BYD and is an integrator, not a technology owner.
  • Bear: GLJ's $25 target implies a near-total collapse of a company with $43.5bn of cash and record revenue — it is a statement of conviction, not a base case.
  • Bear: "Everyone knows it's overvalued" — short interest at 2.25% of float says otherwise.
  • Bear: Attributing the European decline mainly to Musk's politics. The survey effect is real and measured, but an ageing model line and Chinese competition are confounded with it and analysts say so.
  • Both: the consensus $397.87. With a bimodal distribution and a 3.3× revision inside JPMorgan on an analyst change, the average is a statistical artefact rather than a view.
