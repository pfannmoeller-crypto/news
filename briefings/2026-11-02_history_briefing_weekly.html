10 years ago2016A 108-year losing streak in baseball ends · climate treaty · India’s cash shock
SPORT2016Nov 2, 2016 (ended after midnight, Nov 3)

The Chicago Cubs won baseball’s US championship for the first time in 108 years

On the night of 2 November 2016 the Chicago Cubs, a baseball team, defeated the Cleveland Indians 8–7 in the deciding seventh game of the World Series (the US championship), which needed extra time: ten innings, the rounds of play, where a normal game has nine. The game ended shortly after midnight local time.

The first team to win four of seven games takes the title, and the Cubs had been behind one game to three. The deciding game was tied 6–6 after nine innings and interrupted by a 17-minute rain stoppage before the Cubs scored twice in the tenth.

Context & background. The Cubs had gone 108 years without a championship, one of the longest waits in North American professional sport and long blamed in fan lore on a “curse”. Cleveland’s own wait, since 1948, stood at 68 years, so one of the two long waits was certain to end that night. The Cubs’ manager was Joe Maddon.

The details. Cleveland’s Rajai Davis hit a ball out of the park in the eighth inning, scoring two runs against the Cubs’ pitcher Aroldis Chapman and tying the game at 6–6. After nine innings a cloudburst stopped play for 17 minutes, the first rain stoppage in a deciding seventh game. In the tenth inning Ben Zobrist, who got a hit in about 36 percent of his turns at bat during the series, drove in the run that put the Cubs ahead, and he was named the series’ best player. It was the fifth deciding game to go past nine innings and the first won by the away team. More than 40 million people in the US watched, the largest audience for a baseball game since 1991.

Why it still matters. The result ended a century-old losing streak that had shaped how a major city and a whole sport talked about the club. Only six teams have ever won a best-of-seven World Series after being behind one game to three.

POLITICS2016Nov 4, 2016

The Paris climate agreement became international law

The Paris Agreement on climate change entered into force on 4 November 2016, less than a year after it was adopted. It needed two conditions: at least 55 countries, together producing at least 55 percent of global greenhouse-gas emissions, had to formally join, followed by a 30-day wait.

Those conditions were met on 5 October 2016, when a group of countries and the European Union formally approved the agreement at home and filed the paperwork with the United Nations.

Context & background. The agreement was reached by negotiators in Paris in December 2015. Earlier climate treaties had needed years to take effect, and the two conditions (enough countries and enough emissions) were designed so that the treaty could not start without the big emitters.

The details. The tipping point came on 5 October 2016, when Austria, Bolivia, Canada, France, Germany, Hungary, Malta, Nepal, Portugal and Slovakia, together with the European Union, filed their papers with the United Nations Secretary-General. At that point 73 countries had formally joined, covering more than the required 55 percent of emissions, while 191 had signed. The Secretary-General, Ban Ki-moon, said: “What once seemed unthinkable, is now unstoppable.” A country that joins later becomes legally committed 30 days after it files its own papers.

Why it still matters. Under the treaty each country sets and publishes its own targets for cutting greenhouse gases. Its legal status dates from this day, not from the Paris meeting a year earlier.

BUSINESS2016Nov 8, 2016

India withdrew its two largest banknotes overnight, covering 86 percent of banknotes in public hands

In an unscheduled televised address on the evening of 8 November 2016, Prime Minister Narendra Modi announced that all 500- and 1,000-rupee notes (about $7 and $15 at the time) would no longer count as money from midnight. New 500- and 2,000-rupee notes were issued in their place.

The government aimed to uncover money hidden from the tax authorities and to remove fake notes. People queued for weeks at banks to swap or deposit old notes.

Context & background. India’s economy ran largely on cash, and the two withdrawn notes made up about 86.4 percent of the roughly 17.97 trillion rupees (about $265 billion) in public hands. The government expected that about 20 percent of the value, around 5 trillion rupees, would never come back because it was hidden from tax authorities; money never exchanged would simply have vanished, leaving the government better off.

The details. Exchange limits were tight at first: 4,000 rupees per person (about $60) over the counter from 8 to 13 November, then 4,500, then 2,000, and exchange stopped on 25 November. Notes could be deposited until 30 December. The Reserve Bank of India’s 2018 report found that about 15.3 trillion of the 15.41 trillion rupees withdrawn had been deposited, roughly 99.3 percent, so the plan to remove hidden money by non-return largely did not materialise. The number of deaths linked to the queues is disputed: a news site counted 82 in the first month, while the finance minister told parliament in 2018 that four people had died, among them bank staff. Economic growth slowed from 8 percent to 7.1 percent in the year to March 2017, although economists disagree on how much of that was due to the measure.

Why it still matters. Digital payments rose sharply in the months afterwards, and cash later climbed above its pre-ban level, reaching a record of 29.17 trillion rupees in October 2021. In January 2023 India’s Supreme Court ruled 4–1 that the decision could stand; the one judge who disagreed said the government had not followed the legal process.

SPORT2001Nov 4, 2001

Arizona won baseball’s US championship with a game-ending hit against the Yankees’ star pitcher, seven weeks after the 11 September attacks

On 4 November 2001 the Arizona Diamondbacks defeated the New York Yankees 3–2 in the deciding seventh game of the World Series (baseball’s US championship). With the game tied 2–2 in the home team’s last turn at bat, Luis Gonzalez hit a weak ball just over the head of the Yankees’ infielder Derek Jeter, who was standing close in, scoring the winning run.

It was the franchise’s first title in only its fourth season, the fastest of any team newly added to the league.

Context & background. The terror attacks of 11 September 2001 had delayed the baseball season. The series therefore began on 27 October, its latest start ever, and the seventh game became the latest in the calendar year that any championship game had been played to that point. Games 3 to 5 were played in New York, and the seventh game in Phoenix; President George W. Bush threw the opening pitch before Game 3 at Yankee Stadium.

The details. Arizona’s Randy Johnson replaced the starting pitcher Curt Schilling in the eighth inning and pitched into the ninth; he won his third game of the series and shared the best-player award with Schilling. In the ninth the Yankees’ Mariano Rivera, their specialist for protecting a lead, failed to hold it, which baseball calls blowing the save. Tony Womack tied the score with a hit, and a fielding mistake by Rivera let Arizona keep batting before Gonzalez’s hit scored Jay Bell. Arizona outscored New York 37 to 14 over the seven games.

Why it still matters. The result is remembered because a team that had existed for only four years defeated a Yankee side that had won the previous three titles.

BUSINESS2001Nov 8, 2001

Enron admitted its accounts had overstated four years of profit

On 8 November 2001 the energy company Enron told the US government’s stock-market watchdog (the Securities and Exchange Commission) that it would correct its published accounts for 1997 through 2000 and for the first six months of 2001. The new figures cut reported profit for each of the four full years.

Less than four weeks later, on 2 December 2001, Enron applied for court protection from its creditors.

Context & background. Enron was a Houston company that bought and sold electricity and gas, and it had been among the largest US companies by reported revenue. On 16 October 2001 it had announced its first quarterly loss in more than four years, after about $1 billion of charges on poorly performing businesses. Questions then grew about partnerships, run by its finance chief Andrew Fastow, that kept debt and losses out of its published accounts. The 8 November filing was the company’s own admission that earlier accounts had been wrong.

The details. The filing lists the changes in profit year by year: 1997 from $105 million to $9 million, 1998 from $703 million to $590 million, 1999 from $893 million to $643 million and 2000 from $979 million to $847 million, a total cut of $591 million. Profit for the first two three-month periods of 2001 moved up slightly, by $17 million and $5 million, and the third-quarter loss of $618 million became $635 million. A merger with a rival, Dynegy, was announced the next day, 9 November, but Dynegy withdrew on 28 November, citing Enron’s incomplete disclosure of its hidden debt.

Why it still matters. Enron’s auditing firm, Arthur Andersen, admitted destroying thousands of documents and was dismissed by Enron on 17 January 2002. Between 16 October and 28 November 2001 Enron’s share price fell from more than $30 to less than $1.

POLITICS1976Nov 2, 1976

Jimmy Carter narrowly beat President Gerald Ford to win the White House

On 2 November 1976 Democrat Jimmy Carter, a former governor of Georgia, defeated Republican Gerald Ford, the sitting president. The US chooses presidents by state-by-state electoral votes, 270 of which are needed to win; Carter won 297 to Ford’s 240, and 50.1 percent of all votes cast nationwide to 48.0 percent.

It was the first time since 1964 that a Democrat won more than half of the votes cast nationwide.

Context & background. Ford had become president in 1974 after Richard Nixon resigned over the Watergate scandal (illegal spying and a cover-up), and he later pardoned Nixon. The pardon cost him heavily in the polls, and he was also seen as too close to the discredited administration he had served. Carter ran as an outsider.

The details. Carter carried 23 states and the capital, Washington; Ford carried 27. Carter won every state of the Deep South (the south-eastern US states) and lost only Virginia and Oklahoma in the wider South, taking his home state of Georgia with 66.7 percent. Ohio and Wisconsin were decisive: had Ford won both, he would have kept the presidency. One of the people who formally cast Washington state’s vote broke ranks and chose Ronald Reagan, a former California governor, rather than Ford, which is why Ford’s total is 240 and not 241.

Why it still matters. Carter’s sweep of the Deep South, helped by having a Georgian on the ticket, together with narrow wins in Ohio and Wisconsin, gave him the presidency by a thin margin.

POLITICS1976Nov 2, 1976 (lower house vote)

India’s parliament, while Indira Gandhi had suspended normal democratic rights, voted 366 to 4 to rewrite the constitution

On 2 November 1976 India’s Lok Sabha (the lower house of parliament) passed the Forty-second Amendment by 366 votes to 4, with most opposition members staying away. It was passed during the state of emergency, in force since June 1975, under which Prime Minister Indira Gandhi ruled with civil liberties suspended.

The amendment strengthened the central government and limited the courts’ power to overturn laws.

Context & background. The emergency had suspended many civil liberties and let the government rule with few checks. The amendment is often called a “mini-constitution” because it changed so much at once.

The details. It described India in the preamble as a “sovereign, socialist, secular, democratic republic”, adding “socialist” and “secular” to the state’s stated character, and changed “unity of the nation” to “unity and integrity of the nation”. It added a list of fundamental duties for citizens, limited the courts’ power to overturn laws and widened parliament’s power to amend the constitution. The upper house, the Rajya Sabha, passed it around 10–11 November. After approval by the states, the president signed it on 18 December 1976.

Why it still matters. After the emergency ended, the Janata government (the opposition coalition that won power in 1977) reversed parts of it through the Forty-third and Forty-fourth Amendments, and in 1980 the Supreme Court, in the Minerva Mills case, struck down two of its provisions. The preamble words “socialist” and “secular” remain in the constitution today.

CULTURE1976Nov 3, 1976

Brian De Palma’s horror film Carrie opened in 17 cinemas around Washington and Baltimore

Carrie, based on the horror writer Stephen King’s 1974 novel, had its first public showings on 3 November 1976 in a limited regional release of 17 cinemas in the Washington–Baltimore area. It opened in nine Chicago cinemas two days later, in 53 New York cinemas on 16 November and in Los Angeles on 17 November.

It earned Academy Award (Oscar) nominations for Sissy Spacek and Piper Laurie.

Context & background. King’s novel was his first published book. De Palma directed for the studio United Artists, which first set the film’s budget at $1.6 million; it later rose to $1.8 million.

The details. The opening was staged city by city rather than nationally, a common practice then. The film grossed $33.8 million, earning about $14.5 million in US and Canadian rentals (the share paid to the film company) by January 1978. It reached the United Kingdom on 13 January 1977. Spacek was nominated for best actress and Laurie for best supporting actress at the 49th Academy Awards.

Why it still matters. It was the first big-screen adaptation of a King novel and began a long line of his works on film. Carrie itself was remade for the cinema in 2013, after a 1999 sequel and a 2002 television film.
