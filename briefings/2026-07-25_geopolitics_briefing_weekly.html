Geopolitics · 22–25 July 2026 (3-day window)

Ukraine fires its Soviet-style commander-in-chief, Iran turns down a ceasefire and pulls the Houthis into the Red Sea, and a NATO ally shoots down a drone in its own airspace for the first time in the war.

Three days ago the frame was two frozen negotiations and two escalating wars. Some of that moved: Zelensky dismissed Commander-in-Chief Oleksandr Syrsky on 21 July and installed the more popular Mykhailo Drapatyi, but Mykhailo Fedorov — the sacked defence minister whose ouster triggered the whole crisis — turned down the deputy-premiership he was offered as a consolation and remains outside government. In Iran, Tehran rejected a US ceasefire proposal relayed via Iraq's prime minister because it didn't resolve the final status of Hormuz, then reportedly threatened to strike Tel Aviv and ask the Houthis to blockade the Bab al-Mandab strait — and on 23 July, Houthi missiles hit two Saudi tankers in the Red Sea, pushing Brent above $100 a barrel for the first time since May. Separately, the Pentagon quietly cut its own published Iran-war death toll from 18 to 14 and blamed a website "glitch," triggering bipartisan anger in Washington. And in Romania, the hybrid-warfare warning flagged in last week's edition arrived in concrete form: a Romanian F-16 shot down a drone over its own territory — a first for any NATO member in this war — hours after a Ukraine-bound coal carrier exploded off the Romanian coast under still-unclear circumstances.

Ukraine internal21–23 Jul

Zelensky fires his Soviet-style commander-in-chief, but Fedorov turns down the consolation prize — the crisis is half-resolved

On 21 July, under continued pressure from the protests over Fedorov's dismissal, Zelensky removed Commander-in-Chief Oleksandr Syrsky — nicknamed "the butcher" for his centralized command style and the heavy losses of operations like Bakhmut — and replaced him with Mykhailo Drapatyi, 43, a decentralization-minded general who organized the defence of Kryvyi Rih and led the liberation of Kherson. Zelensky then offered Fedorov the newly created post of Deputy Prime Minister for Military Innovation. Fedorov declined on 23 July, saying no position short of defence minister carries the authority to fix corruption in procurement — leaving him outside government even as the demand that triggered the crisis (Syrsky's removal) was met.

Kontext & Vorgeschichte. Last week's edition flagged this as "Ukraine's most serious domestic political rupture of the full-scale war to date," with the crucial unresolved variable being whether Zelensky's own party could actually deliver a parliamentary majority for his proposed fix. He never tested that vote for his first proposed replacement, Interior Minister Ihor Klymenko — instead the crisis escalated past the defence-minister question entirely and reached the commander-in-chief, a figure protesters and soldiers had separately been demanding be removed for his role in high-casualty operations at Bakhmut and Avdiivka, where soldiers reportedly disobeyed orders to avoid what they saw as a needless bloodbath.

Die Details. Drapatyi's profile is the mirror image of Syrsky's: he publicly supported Fedorov's procurement and drone-doctrine reforms, favours decentralized decision-making, and built his reputation on the Kryvyi Rih defence and Kherson liberation rather than attritional set-piece battles. That makes his appointment a substantive, not just personnel, shift in doctrine — consistent with the drone-and-dispersal warfare pattern this briefing's front-line reporting has described for weeks. Fedorov's rejection of the Deputy PM offer is the more interesting data point: his own explanation was explicitly about institutional power, not pride — a deputy premiership for "military innovation" without control over the defence ministry's procurement budget, he argued, cannot deliver the anti-corruption reforms his team had begun. That reasoning, on its face, seems credible given Ukraine's well-documented procurement-corruption problem, but it also means Zelensky spent significant political capital on the Syrsky move without fully closing the credibility gap that opened when Fedorov was first sacked.

Einordnung. This is a partial win for the protest movement, not a full one: the demand seen as most urgent by soldiers and drone-procurement reformers (Syrsky out) was met, while the demand with the most symbolic weight (Fedorov back, with real authority) was not. Whether that split proves durable depends on whether Drapatyi can be seen delivering visible battlefield or procurement improvements quickly — if the front line doesn't visibly improve and Fedorov remains sidelined, the same protest coalition could reconstitute around a narrower "reinstate Fedorov" demand. For Western backers, a credible reformist commander plus a marginalized anti-corruption champion is a mixed signal at a moment when procurement transparency is a precondition for sustained aid.

Watch-Items. (1) Whether Fedorov is offered — or accepts — any other role in coming weeks. (2) Early signals of doctrinal change from Drapatyi's command (decentralization in practice, not just rhetoric). (3) Whether protests resume if visible improvement doesn't follow within weeks. (4) Klymenko's status — he was never confirmed and his nomination appears to have quietly lapsed.

Confidence: high (multi-outlet, on-record quotes from Fedorov and officials)
Diplomacy24 Jul

US and Ukraine align on a new peace push — officials float a partial air ceasefire, Witkoff and Kushner expected to travel to Moscow

After a week with no reported diplomatic movement, Kyiv and Washington began coordinating a new proposal for Moscow on 24 July, betting that Ukraine's deep-strike campaign and its own Patriot shortage give both sides fresh incentive to accept at least a partial air-war truce. Trump envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner are expected to travel to Russia in the coming days after coordinating with Kyiv. Separately, Zelensky is expected in Washington around 28 July for the funeral of Senator Lindsey Graham, where a Trump meeting is being arranged. The Kremlin's public response so far — "we remain open to the negotiation process" — commits to nothing concrete, and Moscow continues to insist any Zelensky meeting happen on Russian soil, a condition Kyiv rejects.

Kontext & Vorgeschichte. Last week's edition described the Ukraine-Russia negotiating track as entirely silent for the first time in a while, with the most recent substantive marker being the 8 July NATO Ankara summit's aid pledge. This week's development is the first concrete sign of renewed US-Ukraine diplomatic coordination since then, though notably it is a US-Ukraine alignment exercise, not yet a US-Russia or Ukraine-Russia one — Moscow has not been formally presented with a proposal as of this writing.

Die Details. The "air truce" framing — a ceasefire limited to missile and drone strikes rather than a full front-line ceasefire — is a notably narrower ask than prior peace frameworks, and the logic behind it is symmetric: Ukraine's chronic Patriot-interceptor shortage (exposed starkly by the 19 July barrage) gives Kyiv reason to want the missile war paused, while Russia's exposure to Ukraine's now much-improved deep-strike campaign (159+ vessels, refineries, an air base) gives Moscow a parallel incentive. That symmetry is precisely what makes US officials cautiously optimistic it could work where broader ceasefire proposals have failed. The Graham funeral detail is procedurally significant less for the funeral itself than as a diplomatically convenient, low-commitment venue for a Trump-Zelensky meeting without the formality (and possible collapse risk) of a dedicated summit.

Einordnung. An air-only truce, if achieved, would not end the war or resolve the front line, but it would materially change the character of it — shifting the contest back toward the grinding, low-territorial-movement ground war this briefing has described as static at ~1.6 km²/day, while removing the civilian-casualty and infrastructure-damage dimension that drives most Western public attention. Whether Moscow has genuine interest or is simply managing the optics of "remaining open" while conditions (Zelensky travelling to Russia) it knows Kyiv will refuse remain on the table is the key unresolved question. Given Russia's serial rejection of neutral-venue talks throughout the war, treat "openness" language with real skepticism until a specific venue and date is confirmed by both sides.

Watch-Items. (1) Confirmation of a Witkoff/Kushner Moscow trip and its outcome. (2) Whether the Trump-Zelensky meeting at the Graham funeral actually produces a substantive readout. (3) Any Kremlin response beyond boilerplate "openness" language. (4) Whether an air truce, if agreed, actually holds given both sides' recent record of large barrages timed around diplomatic moments.

Confidence: medium (officials cited anonymously; no confirmed Moscow response yet)
Front line22–24 Jul

Still static on the ground — Ukraine keeps hitting Wildberries warehouses, Russia keeps sending drones at civilians

Neither side achieved confirmed territorial advances in this window, per the Institute for the Study of War's 22–24 July assessments. Ukraine's long-range campaign continued its unusual retail-targeting pattern, striking roughly ten warehouses belonging to Wildberries — Russia's dominant e-commerce platform, often compared to Amazon — over the preceding week, alongside continued strikes on refineries and logistics. Overnight into 24 July, Russia launched five Kh-59/69 guided missiles and 180 drones at Ukraine. Separately, a Romanian F-16 shot down a drone that entered Romanian airspace on 24 July (covered in full under Hybrid/Europe below), and an explosion damaged a coal-carrying bulk vessel bound for Ukraine off the Romanian coast the same day.

Kontext & Vorgeschichte. This extends directly from last week's finding of ~1.6 km²/day Russian gains and Ukraine's then-best-ever deep-strike week (159 vessels, an Engels-2 bomber base, the Yaroslavl refinery). The Wildberries targeting is new in this window and represents an escalation in target selection logic: rather than purely military-logistics or energy targets, Ukraine is now hitting a civilian retail and logistics backbone used daily by ordinary Russians.

Die Details. ISW's assessment frames the Wildberries strikes as dual-purpose — degrading a logistics network that likely also carries some dual-use military-adjacent freight, while simultaneously straining Russian air defence (which must respond to drone threats regardless of target type) and creating direct civilian inconvenience that domestic Russian media cannot easily spin as a foreign military target. The 180-drone, 5-missile barrage on the night of 23–24 July is a smaller nightly total than the 41-ballistic-missile barrage of 19 July, suggesting Russia has not sustained that record pace, though drone saturation attacks of this size remain a serious air-defence burden on their own.

Einordnung. The front line's continued stasis, now confirmed across three more days of ISW assessments, reinforces last week's framing: this war's near-term trajectory is being decided by production capacity and political durability far more than by manoeuvre. The Wildberries campaign specifically signals Ukraine is willing to extend its deep-strike campaign into the Russian civilian economy in ways that go beyond energy and military-logistics targeting — a trend worth tracking for both its military effect and its escalatory risk.

Watch-Items. (1) Whether Wildberries-style civilian-economy targeting expands to other retail/logistics firms. (2) Nightly barrage sizes trending up or down from the 19 July peak. (3) Confirmed territorial change at Pokrovsk specifically. (4) Any Russian retaliatory rhetoric or action specifically citing the Wildberries strikes.

Russia internal24 Jul

Russia's Central Bank cuts rates for the tenth time since 2024, but warns inflation is heading to 6-7% — Ukrainian strikes are named as a direct cause

The Bank of Russia cut its key rate a further 25 basis points to 14% on 24 July, continuing a gradual easing cycle from a 2024 peak of 21%. At the same time, it sharply revised its inflation outlook upward to 6-7% for the rest of 2026 (from 5.9% currently) and downgraded GDP growth to just 0-1% for the year. Crucially, the bank explicitly cited "direct and second-round effects of the temporary decline in production capacities" from Ukrainian drone strikes on refineries as a driver of higher fuel costs and inflation — a rare direct acknowledgment by a state institution that Ukraine's deep-strike campaign is materially affecting the domestic economy.

Kontext & Vorgeschichte. This is a direct continuation of last week's "abyss" data points — the Moscow Exchange's steepest one-day drop since 2022, a 27-month-low Central Bank sentiment reading, and Putin's five-point approval decline. This week adds an official monetary-policy acknowledgment that Ukraine's deep-strike campaign is a named driver of Russian inflation, which is a materially stronger and more specific admission than last week's more diffuse market signals.

Die Details. A rate cut alongside an inflation-outlook upgrade is an unusual, difficult-to-defend combination in ordinary monetary policy — it signals the Central Bank judges the growth slowdown (GDP now forecast at just 0-1%) to be the more urgent problem, even at the cost of letting inflation run hotter, which itself indicates real distress in the underlying economy rather than routine policy calibration. The explicit citation of Ukrainian refinery strikes as an inflation driver validates, from an official Russian source, the strategic logic behind Ukraine's energy-infrastructure targeting campaign described in this and prior editions. Russian equities reportedly rose about 1.8% on the rate-cut news, suggesting markets read the move as accommodative relief rather than as a distress signal — a split reaction between market pricing and the underlying economic narrative worth noting.

Einordnung. Ten rate cuts since a 2024 peak of 21% down to 14% describe a central bank trying to engineer a soft landing for a war economy under mounting external pressure — sanctions, a shrinking correspondent-banking network (see Sanctions section), and now, by its own admission, physical damage to refining capacity. None of this is collapse, but it is the most concrete evidence yet, from Moscow's own institutions rather than Western analysts, that the war's cost is compounding rather than stabilizing.

Watch-Items. (1) Whether inflation actually reaches the 6-7% band forecast, or overshoots it. (2) Further rate cuts or reversals if inflation accelerates faster than expected. (3) Additional refinery/energy strikes and their cited economic effect. (4) Whether the Moscow Exchange sustains its 16 July drop or recovers.

Confidence: high (official Central Bank statement, independently reported)

UN: 1,396 Ukrainian civilian deaths in H1 2026 — 37% higher than the same period in 2025

A United Nations tally cited this week found 1,396 verified Ukrainian civilian deaths in the first half of 2026, a 37% increase over the same period in 2025, with total verified civilian deaths since 2022 reaching 16,431. The figure sits alongside this week's ISW-documented casualty data (10+ civilians killed in strikes across Donetsk and Odesa on 21 July) and reinforces that even with a static front line, the war's civilian cost is rising, not falling, into its fifth year.

Mehr lesenexpand_more

Kontext & Vorgeschichte. UN verified-casualty data is generally treated as the most methodologically conservative civilian-casualty count available in this war, since it requires documentary or witness corroboration rather than accepting government-reported figures at face value — meaning the true toll is very likely higher, not lower, than the verified count.

Die Details. The 37% year-on-year increase is notable precisely because the front line has not moved meaningfully and the war has, if anything, become more static — the rising civilian toll is therefore attributable primarily to the missile and drone barrage campaign (like the 19 July record strike on Kyiv) rather than to ground offensives or shifting front lines. This tracks with this and last week's reporting on both the scale of missile barrages and Ukraine's own acknowledged Patriot-interceptor shortage.

Einordnung. A war where the front line is frozen but civilian deaths are rising sharply is, by definition, one in which the strategic contest has shifted from territorial capture to attrition against population centres and infrastructure — consistent with this briefing's recurring finding that both sides are now fighting an air/drone war layered on top of a largely static ground war. That shift has direct relevance to the ongoing air-truce diplomacy covered above: an air-only ceasefire would target precisely the mechanism driving this civilian toll increase.

Watch-Items. (1) Whether the civilian death rate continues rising through H2 2026. (2) Correlation between missile barrage frequency/size and the casualty trend. (3) Whether an air truce, if achieved, visibly reduces this figure in subsequent UN updates.

Iran / Hormuz — a ceasefire offer rejected, the Houthis pulled in, oil above $100, and a Pentagon casualty-count scandal

Iran rejects a US ceasefire proposal relayed via Iraq's PM — and reportedly threatens Tel Aviv and a Houthi blockade of Bab al-Mandab

Iran rejected a US ceasefire proposal passed along by Iraqi Prime Minister Ali al-Zaidi, with officials saying they are not interested in "a temporary deal" that fails to resolve the final status of the Strait of Hormuz. Iranian officials reportedly warned that continued escalation would result in strikes on Tel Aviv, and that Iran would ask Houthi forces to impose a blockade on the Bab al-Mandab strait, the Red Sea's own chokepoint. Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi separately responded to Trump's threat to seize frozen Iranian assets to cover war damages, calling it "an incendiary precedent." Within a day, a version of the Houthi threat materialised (see below).

Mehr lesenexpand_more

Kontext & Vorgeschichte. Last week's edition documented UN Secretary-General Guterres and a China-Pakistan mediation push calling for a return to the 17 June memorandum of understanding, with no formal proposal from Washington reported at the time. This week's Iraq-relayed proposal is the first concrete US ceasefire offer this briefing has been able to confirm since that MoU collapsed — and its rejection, on the specific grounds that it doesn't resolve Hormuz's final status, confirms Hormuz access (not the nuclear file, not the regional strikes) is Tehran's central bargaining demand right now.

Die Details. The structure of Iran's rejection is informative: it is not a rejection of negotiating in principle, but a rejection of a "temporary" arrangement — Iran appears to want a durable settlement on Hormuz transit rights and control before agreeing to stop fighting, rather than a ceasefire first, details-later sequencing that the US and mediators have generally proposed. The reported threat to involve the Houthis specifically weaponises a chokepoint the US and Gulf states cannot as easily militarize as Hormuz itself (Bab al-Mandab sits mostly outside the Gulf, adjacent to Yemen), giving Iran an escalation lever with lower direct cost to itself. The Tel Aviv threat, while rhetorically serious, echoes threats made and not executed earlier in the war and should be read with the same caution this briefing has applied to prior unfulfilled Iranian ultimatums (see last week's Rezaee item).

Einordnung. This rejection, followed within roughly 24 hours by an actual Houthi strike on Saudi tankers, is the clearest evidence yet that Iran is willing to translate rhetorical escalation into action via proxies even while its direct war with the US continues to grind on. It also reframes the diplomatic problem: any future ceasefire proposal that doesn't address Hormuz specifically, and doesn't account for Iran's demonstrated willingness to widen the war geographically via Yemen, is likely to be rejected on the same grounds.

Watch-Items. (1) Any revised US proposal that explicitly addresses Hormuz's final status. (2) Further Houthi actions in the Red Sea specifically linked to Iranian requests. (3) Any actual strike toward Tel Aviv, which would be a major escalation beyond rhetoric. (4) Iraqi PM al-Zaidi's continued role as an intermediary.

Confidence: medium (single-sourced reporting on the threats; the rejection itself multiply corroborated)
Escalation23 Jul

Houthis strike two Saudi tankers in the Red Sea — Brent tops $100 for the first time since May

Houthi forces struck two Saudi oil tankers, the Encelia and the Layla, with missiles and drones in the Red Sea on 23 July, claiming the vessels violated a Houthi blockade of Saudi ports. The Encelia was confirmed hit, causing a fire at the bow; all crew on both vessels were reported safe. Brent crude surged to $101.10 a barrel — the highest since May and a more than 30% rally over the month of July — while WTI advanced to $91.49. The strikes landed the same day CENTCOM completed its twelfth consecutive night of strikes on Iranian targets, meaning oil markets are now pricing dual chokepoint risk (Hormuz and Bab al-Mandab/Red Sea) simultaneously for the first time this war.

Mehr lesenexpand_more

Kontext & Vorgeschichte. This is the first Red Sea/Bab al-Mandab escalation this briefing has recorded since the Iran war began dominating coverage in the spring, and it arrives within roughly a day of Iran's reported threat to ask the Houthis to blockade the strait (see above) — a sequencing that strongly suggests coordination, even though no Iranian government statement has explicitly claimed credit for directing the strikes. The Houthis have a long independent track record of Red Sea shipping attacks dating to the Gaza-war period, so an alternative reading is that this is Houthi-initiated opportunism riding on Iran's rhetorical cover rather than a directly ordered operation.

Die Details. Targeting Saudi-flagged tankers specifically, rather than Israeli-linked or Western shipping as in earlier Houthi campaigns, is a notable shift — it directly pressures Saudi Arabia, a US partner that has so far stayed largely outside the direct Iran-US fighting, and could be calculated to test Riyadh's tolerance and complicate US-Saudi coordination on the war. The price move (Brent $88 to $101 in roughly a week, over 30% for the month) is one of the sharpest short-term oil rallies of this entire war, reflecting that markets treat a second chokepoint opening as materially different from continued disruption at a single one.

Einordnung. A two-chokepoint oil shock is a substantially more dangerous scenario than the single-strait crisis this briefing has tracked for months: it removes the alternate-routing option that has partly cushioned the Hormuz disruption (cargo can, in principle, reroute around the Cape of Good Hope, but that adds weeks of transit time and cost, and now both a northern and southern Gulf-adjacent route carry live attack risk). If this pattern continues, expect further upward pressure on Brent and renewed pressure on Washington to protect Saudi as well as Emirati and Qatari shipping interests, potentially widening US military commitments beyond the current Iran-focused campaign.

Watch-Items. (1) Any explicit Iranian claim of responsibility or coordination with the Houthi strikes. (2) Saudi Arabia's response — military, diplomatic, or both. (3) Further Red Sea shipping attacks and whether major carriers begin rerouting around Africa. (4) Brent's trajectory — whether $100+ becomes a sustained floor or proves a spike.

Confidence: high (multi-outlet, confirmed strikes and price data)
Casualty transparency24 Jul

The Pentagon quietly cuts its own published Iran-war death toll from 18 to 14, blames a website "glitch" — Congress is not buying it

The Pentagon's public Defense Casualty Analysis System (DCAS) database dropped its reported Iran-war deaths from 18 to 14 and wounded from 482 to 420 on 24 July, effectively erasing four recently killed service members from the public count. Acting Press Secretary Joel Valdez attributed the change to "temporary data disruptions," saying "site anomalies are currently being resolved." Senate Armed Services Committee Democrats demanded "a full and accurate accounting"; Rep. Thomas Massie called it an "absurd ruse" to avoid War Powers Resolution reporting deadlines; New York Governor Kathy Hochul objected specifically to the removal of Sgt. Angel Rampersad from the count, saying "the Trump administration does not get to revise the human cost."

Kontext & Vorgeschichte. Last week's edition flagged that the Pentagon was "managing disclosure pace" on casualties — releasing names in batches of six, then two more — describing a pattern likely to erode domestic tolerance for the war over time. This week's development is a sharper version of the same concern: not a pacing issue but an outright downward revision of the headline number, with an official explanation (a website "glitch") that multiple lawmakers from both parties have publicly rejected as implausible.

Die Details. The specific figures matter: a four-death and 62-wounded reduction is too large and too specific (naming an individually identifiable service member, Sgt. Rampersad, as one of the removed) to be easily explained by routine data-entry error, which is the core of the bipartisan skepticism. Massie's "War Powers Resolution" framing points to a specific legal mechanism: the Resolution requires reporting and, under some readings, Congressional authorization renewal tied to the scale and duration of hostilities — a lower official casualty count could plausibly affect political pressure around invoking or enforcing that mechanism, which is the substance behind the "ruse" accusation even though it remains an allegation, not a proven motive.

Einordnung. Whether this is genuine technical error or a deliberate undercount, the controversy itself is now a political fact independent of the true casualty number — it adds a domestic-accountability dimension to a war that, per this week's other reporting, already features an estranged relationship between combat reality and official messaging on both the US and Iranian sides. A war where the Pentagon's own casualty figures become a bipartisan credibility issue is one where domestic political tolerance is being tested on a second front, alongside the war's direct battlefield costs.

Watch-Items. (1) Whether the Pentagon restores the original 18/482 figures or provides a technical explanation that withstands scrutiny. (2) Any Congressional hearing or subpoena tied to the discrepancy. (3) Whether War Powers Resolution enforcement becomes a live legislative fight. (4) Further revisions to the public casualty count in either direction.

Confidence: high (official DCAS data change confirmed, bipartisan reaction on record)

Lloyd's List Intelligence data for 13-19 July shows non-Iran-linked ship transits falling to just 25 vessels for the week, down from 108 the week before; inbound traffic fell to 8 from 43. Tanker transits fell to 39 from 85, with non-Iran-linked tankers dropping hardest (17, down from 53). Nearly 70% of tanker transits are now occurring with AIS tracking systems disabled, up from 55% the prior week — a signal of rising operational secrecy and risk-avoidance. Iran-linked tanker traffic has proven comparatively resilient, recording 21 transits despite renewed US sanctions pressure. Total Hormuz traffic is now roughly 90% below the same period last year.

Kontext & Vorgeschichte. Last week's edition reported daily transit counts around 7-8 vessels against a 100+ pre-war baseline; this week's weekly aggregate data (25 non-Iran-linked transits across seven days, i.e. roughly 3.5/day) confirms that collapse has, if anything, deepened rather than stabilised, and provides a fuller weekly comparison (108 → 25) that makes the scale of the drop clearer than single-day snapshots.

Sanctions & Trade — the 21st package finally passes, but heavily diluted

1 item
Adopted23 Jul

The EU's 21st sanctions package passes on the third attempt — but Greece, Bulgaria, France and Italy all extracted concessions

The EU Council adopted the 21st sanctions package on 23 July: 218 new designations (48 individuals, 170 entities) — described as the largest single batch of listings in four years — including asset freezes on 94 banks, transaction bans on 33 more credit institutions, 14 crypto-platform designations, 41 additional shadow-fleet vessels, and 56 individuals/companies tied to drone production. But the package passed only after significant last-minute dilution: Greece secured an exemption letting pre-2022 Russian LNG contracts continue to non-EU markets, subject to annual review; Bulgaria got Patriarch Kirill and oligarch Vagit Alekperov removed from the list entirely; Portugal and Germany killed proposed fisheries restrictions; and France and Italy downgraded a Schengen entry ban on Russian combatants to a non-binding commitment. Sources disagree on the oil price cap's fate — the EU Council's own statement says the cap mechanism is suspended until 15 July 2027, while other reporting says the existing $44/barrel cap was simply preserved; this briefing cannot reconcile the two and flags it as unresolved.

Die Details. Einordnung. Hybrid warfare / Europe — last week's warning arrives: NATO's first drone shootdown of the war

First of its kind24 Jul

A Romanian F-16 shoots down a drone over Romanian territory — the first such shootdown by any NATO member in this war

Around 11:02am local time on 24 July, a Romanian F-16 shot down a drone roughly 114 km from Bucharest, near the village of Padina, after Italian Eurofighters and Romanian F-16s on NATO air-policing duty tracked it from 9:39am. President Nicusor Dan confirmed the shootdown, noting the intercept area was uninhabited. Romania has experienced "dozens" of prior drone incursions since the full-scale war began without engaging militarily; a 2025 law — passed after a May 2026 drone strike damaged an apartment building and wounded two people — first gave Romanian forces legal authority to intercept. Foreign Minister Oana Toiu credited "seamless coordination with Italian Eurofighters" and pledged further investment in anti-drone capability.
