Geopolitics · 30 October – 5 November 2026

Talks are pencilled in, strikes are planned, and nothing has stopped

Every theatre this week is defined by something that has been announced or prepared but not yet done. Russia and Ukraine are talking about talking while the missiles continue. The US and Iran are each preparing for another round of fighting while each says the other must move first. Israel has held an election but has no new government yet. Sanctions and Europe's hidden-warfare incidents (sabotage and drone attacks that Russia is suspected of) had no confirmed news this week.

Status Quo — where the 29 October edition left off

bookmarkPrior read (compiled 29 October, covered 15–29 October)

  • Ukraine and Russia: President Trump announced an "immediate" energy ceasefire on 11 October without either side having agreed. Ukraine said Russia ignored it; the Institute for the Study of War, a Washington research group that tracks the fighting, said none existed. Russia kept striking Ukrainian power sites.
  • Iran and Hormuz: The US and Iran have been at war since 28 February. October was the worst month so far for attacks on ships near the Strait of Hormuz, the narrow sea passage for much of the world's oil. Iran's foreign minister said on 18 October that its seven-day offer to reopen the strait, if its conditions are met, was still on the table.
  • Shipping and oil: Counts of attacks differ by source (28 in October by one research group, 113 since the war began, 135 incidents by a British naval monitor) and cannot be added together. Brent, the standard international oil price, was above $115 a barrel in late October (a barrel is about 159 litres).
  • Sanctions: On 16 October Trump signed an order that brought the Graham Act (a US law letting the president punish countries that buy Russian energy) into force but suspended all of its tariffs and sanctions on buyers of Russian energy. A US Treasury licence permitting dealings in Russian diesel runs to 7 April 2027.
  • Hybrid warfare in Europe: No confirmed incidents were found for 15–29 October; the last confirmed picture dated from 1 October.

Ukraine / Russia — talks promised for November, strikes unchanged

1 item
Diplomacy21–22 Oct · latest confirmed event

Russia says it will resume US-mediated talks with Ukraine in November, while its missiles keep falling on Kyiv

After US envoy Steve Witkoff met President Vladimir Putin in St Petersburg on 21 October, Russia said it was ready to resume talks with Ukraine, with the US mediating, the following month. Ukraine's president, Volodymyr Zelensky, said he hoped they would start early in November. They would be the first since February.

The talks have not been confirmed as scheduled, and no reliable report dated 30 October – 5 November on this war was available. The energy ceasefire Trump announced on 11 October has no sign of taking effect.

Context & background. Three rounds of US-mediated talks took place in Abu Dhabi and Geneva at the start of 2026, and three short meetings were held in Istanbul in 2025; none produced a breakthrough, according to Reuters. In September Zelensky told Axios, a US news site, that he preferred a three-way meeting of Washington, Kyiv and Moscow with an energy ceasefire as an early result. Witkoff met Zelensky and European officials in Oslo on 20 October, and brought a set of proposals drafted there to Putin. A senior Russian official told Reuters the St Petersburg meeting was relatively short and not substantive.

The details. The Kremlin aide Yuri Ushakov said the "nuances" the Americans reported from Oslo "do not entirely satisfy" Moscow. On the night after the meeting, Ukraine said Russia fired at least 21 ballistic missiles in three waves, three of them carrying cluster munitions (bomblets that scatter over a wide area), and Zelensky said two people were killed and 34 injured across a dozen regions. Russia said Ukraine launched 630 drones towards Moscow and that it struck port infrastructure in the Odesa region. Both sides exchanged war dead. Reuters notes the front line is largely static and that both sides have escalated long-range strikes: Russia on Ukraine's power grid, Ukraine on Russian oil refineries. A secondhand summary of other reports gives mid-November (after 10 November) for the talks and a 27 November US deadline for a revised peace plan, but we could not check the original reports, so treat both dates as unconfirmed.

Why it still matters. The pattern repeats: a diplomatic opening is announced, and heavy strikes follow within hours. The first real test is whether a date, a venue and a delegation list are published for the talks. Until then the 11 October energy ceasefire remains an announcement, not an event. Moscow's decision to talk only after the US midterm elections of 3 November may reflect a wait for the result; that is our reading, not a Russian statement.

Iran / Hormuz — attacks continue, US strikes prepared

2 items
Hormuz shipping30 Oct – 1 Nov

Ships are still being hit in and around Hormuz, with October the worst month since the war began

The British navy-run monitor of commercial shipping reported on 30 October that a small boat attached two explosive devices to a tanker's hull in the strait, causing an engine-room fire. On 1 November a tug, the Panama-flagged Selat Storm, was reportedly hit by an unidentified projectile in the strait, and a tanker was attacked in the upper Gulf off Saudi Arabia.

Al Jazeera counted more than 120 attacks on ships in October, the highest monthly total of the war. Who carried out each attack is mostly not stated.

Context & background. The strait has been effectively closed to normal commercial traffic since the war began on 28 February. Our last edition cited 28 confirmed or reported attacks in October from a Washington research group; Al Jazeera's figure of more than 120 uses a different basis (all reported attacks, as of 25 October), and the two should not be compared. Iran claimed that seven oil tankers had caught fire or been damaged in a single week in late October.

The details. The 30 October and 1 November incidents appear only in shipping-trade reports that relay the British monitor's warnings; no detail on damage, casualties or who was responsible is available, and nothing is reported for 2 November. On diplomacy, Iran's foreign minister Abbas Araghchi said on 29 October, according to a secondhand summary of reports we could not check, that Iran was in close contact with intermediaries on how to carry out its seven-day plan, and that the measures remain subject to agreement. Pakistan and Qatar are the main mediators. The plan, as Araghchi described it in September, would end hostilities in Lebanon, release at least $12 billion of frozen Iranian assets, waive oil sanctions and lift the US naval blockade, with the strait reopening on the last day. Trump has said he rejected the proposal; a US official called the talks constructive. The dispute is over who moves first.

Why it still matters. Each ship hit removes carrying capacity and raises insurance costs even if a deal arrives, and the attacks show that talk of negotiation has not changed behaviour at sea. Oil prices are covered in our Markets briefing. Watch for: a published US reply to the seven-day plan, any ship attributed to a named attacker, and the next monthly attack count.

US military optionsReported 22–25 Oct · nothing newer confirmed

The US and Israel have plans for new strikes on Iran after the election; no strike is confirmed

US media reported in late October that US Central Command (the US military headquarters for the Middle East) has options ready for renewed attacks on Iran, and the New York Times said the US and Israeli militaries drew up joint plans for after the 3 November election. Trump said on 8 October the US would not attack before the vote; on 24 October he said the US "may do another little Epic Fury", the name of the operation that began the war on 28 February, and kept open a "big, beautiful" agreement.

No strike has been reported through 5 November, so whether the plans have been approved is unknown.

Context & background. The war began on 28 February with US-Israeli strikes. A ceasefire in April and a June agreement outline brokered with Pakistan's help both unravelled, and the US carried out several rounds of strikes in June, July and early September. Reports say Trump delayed an Israeli operation planned for October because he did not want a new conflict before the election.

The details. According to Al Jazeera, Axios reported that the Pentagon told US Central Command to finish preparations for a possible return to major combat, which could target Iranian energy sites, infrastructure and nuclear sites. The Hill reported options had been prepared for Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth. The US is sending a third aircraft carrier group, built around the carrier Theodore Roosevelt, plus about 10,000 soldiers and the 13th Marine Expeditionary Unit (a US Marine fighting force of about 2,000 troops) by the end of November. A memo from the Director of National Intelligence (the US government's top intelligence official) said Iran had "substantially increased efforts to target US personnel", and the State Department cut staff at several regional embassies. Iran's Revolutionary Guards (its elite armed force) announced a more "offensive" doctrine under which countries hosting bases used against Iran could be targets, and Reuters reported Iranian plans to hit undersea communications cables in the Gulf. Iran's president Masoud Pezeshkian said Washington had derailed talks but that efforts for a deal should continue. A single low-quality outlet claimed a strike would come "within 96 hours" of the vote; we give that no weight. None of this is an order to attack.

Why it still matters. Julie Norman of Chatham House, a London foreign-policy think tank,, quoted by Al Jazeera, calls the plans both posturing and a real option, and asks what another bombing round would achieve that earlier ones did not. Analysts quoted also warn of a high risk of rapid escalation by miscalculation. The decision rests with Trump alone, and the post-election window is the first moment he has said he is free to use it. Watch for: an approval or cancellation, new carrier movements, and any Iranian move against shipping or cables.

Other — Israel's coalition fight; Europe's hybrid incidents

1 item + 1 note
Israel27 Oct – 4 Nov

Netanyahu's bloc won the most seats but no majority, and the opposition is lining up behind Gadi Eisenkot

In Israel's election on 27 October, Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu's bloc finished first with 53 of the 120 seats in the Knesset (Israel's parliament), eight short of a majority. The opposition bloc won 48 and Arab-led parties a record 19. Likud, Netanyahu's own party, is the largest single party with 27 seats.

President Isaac Herzog began consultations with party representatives on 4 November. Reports say Naftali Bennett, Avigdor Liberman and Yair Golan back former army chief Gadi Eisenkot for prime minister, and the Arab-led parties were expected to do the same. Eisenkot has ruled out serving under Netanyahu.

Context & background. Israel's president, not the election winner, decides who gets the first try at forming a government, after asking each party whom it recommends. The candidate then has 28 days, extendable by 14, and a failure can lead to another election. The result matters beyond Israel: Al Jazeera reports that Netanyahu pushed for a strike on Iran as his poll numbers fell, and said overthrowing Iran's government remains Israel's "primary objective".

The details. One outlet adds the opposition's 48 seats to the Arab parties' 19 to reach 67, which would be a majority, but recommending a candidate is not the same as joining a coalition, and the parties differ widely. United Torah Judaism, a religious party, declined to name any candidate. Netanyahu and Likud amplified unsubstantiated claims that Arab-led parties had "stolen" the election; the election committee called allegations of systematic violations baseless. Ynet reported Herzog was seeking a Netanyahu-Eisenkot unity government; the President's Office denied the idea, according to a secondary report. The outcome of the 4 November consultations is not yet reported.

Why it still matters. A government led by Eisenkot, who would depend on Arab-led parties, would be a break from the current coalition's positions, while a deadlock would keep Netanyahu as interim leader with the strongest incentive to act on Iran. That second point is our inference. Watch for: who Herzog names by 11 November, and whether any Israeli move on Iran precedes it.

lightbulbEditor's view

The week was about waiting for 3 November, and our confirmed reporting stops at about 2 November

1. The US election result is now known, but its effect is not. Almost every open question here, from Iran strikes to Russian talks to the Graham Act pause, was tied by officials or analysts to the 3 November vote. Democrats won both the House and the Senate (live election coverage, first source, live election coverage, second source); the consequences for Ukraine aid, Iran and sanctions are not yet visible.

2. Planning is not a decision. Reports on US-Israeli strike plans against Iran, a third carrier group and embassy staff cuts all point one way, but none is an approval, and Trump keeps saying both things at once.

3. Talks about talks have not slowed the fighting. Russia agreed in principle to meet in November and hit Kyiv within hours; Iran says its offer stands while ships are hit. The test is a published date and delegation.

4. Counts that look alike are not. October ship attacks are 28 in one tally and more than 120 in another. Compare each only with its own earlier figure.

5. Israel's coalition fight is a foreign-policy variable. The 11 November deadline falls in the same weeks as the possible US decision on Iran, and a leader with no majority has little to lose by escalating. That is an inference, not a reported plan.

6. Quiet theatres stay flagged as unknown. Sanctions and European sabotage show nothing new; for the latter, little was reported at all.

Next week: whether talks get a date, whether strikes are ordered, who Herzog picks, and whether the Graham Act pause survives.
