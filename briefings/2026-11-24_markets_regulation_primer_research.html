1

Start here: the wrong question

Markets work when three things hold

Buyers can judge what they buy. The costs of an activity land on whoever causes them. Sellers compete. When one fails, a market can go wrong by its own logic: a factory does not pay for its smoke, a patient cannot judge a drug, a firm without rivals raises prices. Regulation is justified when one of these fails and the state can repair it for less than the damage. Most arguments skip one half of that sentence.

Both instincts, at full strength

"The EU over-regulates." Compliance is a fixed cost that small firms cannot spread (Bundesbank, below). Compiler's inference: rules can also protect incumbents from newcomers, and the unseen price is products never launched.

"Unregulated capitalism harms customers and health." Regulation protects people that markets cannot protect on their own: sellers usually know more than buyers, and some harms show up late and cannot be undone. Each side gets one steelman and one set of costs.

Companion pieces: the Draghi competitiveness long read and German rent regulation.

Four approaches

Each school at the strength its defenders would accept, then one line on where it breaks. Scale: 5/5 primary source · 4/5 two independent sources · 3/5 single credible source · 2/5 aggregator or memory only.

A. Market failure: markets can break on their own terms 1920–1970

Pigou (1920): when a factory's smoke harms neighbours, the price of its goods leaves that harm out; a charge equal to the harm puts it back. Akerlof (1970): if buyers cannot tell good used cars from bad ("lemons"), they offer an average price, owners of good cars withdraw, and quality spirals down. Arrow (1963): health care is unlike ordinary markets, because need is unpredictable and the patient cannot judge the treatment, so society builds licences, professional codes and insurance.

Check · Pigou, 1920Not re-checked C 2/5 — Welfare economics book, Macmillan, London, from memory. "Pigouvian tax" is a later label, not his term.
Check · Akerlof, 1970Mostly true C 5/5 Nobel part — Nobel 2001 confirmed, but shared with Spence and Stiglitz "for their analyses of markets with asymmetric information". The paper was not opened.
Check · Arrow, 1963Not re-checked C 2/5 — American Economic Review 53(5), December 1963, from memory.

Where it breaks: naming a failure does not show the state can fix it; the fix needs the knowledge the market lacked.

B. Government failure: the state can break too 1945–1971

Hayek (1945): prices carry knowledge scattered among millions that no planner can collect. Stigler (1971): industries have a concentrated reason to shape their rules and the public a scattered one, so regulation is often "captured" and ends up serving the industry. Nobel 1982 "for his seminal studies of industrial structures, functioning of markets and causes and effects of public regulation". Coase (1960): people who can bargain cheaply can settle harms without a tax or ban, but he stressed that bargaining is costly in practice; "Coase theorem" is Stigler's label.

Check · Hayek, 1945Not re-checked C 2/5 — American Economic Review 35(4), September 1945, from memory.
Check · Stigler, 1971Nobel confirmed; paper not re-checked C 5/5 Nobel · 2/5 paper — Citation from nobelprize.org; journal details from memory.
Check · Coase, 1960Nobel confirmed; paper not re-checked C 5/5 Nobel · 2/5 paper — Nobel 1991 "for his discovery and clarification of the significance of transaction costs and property rights". Journal details from memory. Coase's stress on transaction costs and Stigler's naming of the "theorem": from memory, not checked.

Where it breaks: it shows the state can fail, not that it must, and says little about harms a buyer cannot learn of in time.

C. Ordoliberalism and the social market economy 1930s–1960s

Walter Eucken and the Freiburg School held that the state should set and police the rules of the game, above all competition, and stay out of outcomes. Alfred Müller-Armack coined "Soziale Marktwirtschaft" (social market economy) in 1946, published 1947, and later worked under Ludwig Erhard at the Economics Ministry (Erhard's role unchecked). Compiler's judgement, not a finding: this is the closest thing to a bridge between A and B, since it accepts that markets need rules and distrusts discretionary power.

Check · Eucken, Müller-ArmackMostly true C 3/5 Eucken · 2/5 Müller-Armack — Eucken as founder of the Freiburg School: one lexicon (Federal Agency for Civic Education). The 1946 coinage: an encyclopedia entry. The doctrine summary is the compiler's, not checked.

Where it breaks: "rules, not outcomes" blurs fast; what counts as a fair rule is itself political.

D. Power and embeddedness: who shapes the market? 1944–2019

Polanyi (1944): markets are built by political decisions, and a self-regulating one strains society until society pushes back. Philippon (2019): since about 2000 US markets have become less competitive than European ones (more concentration, margins and lobbying, weaker antitrust). Ostrom (Nobel 2009): communities can govern shared resources such as fisheries with their own rules, neither by state nor by market.

Check · Polanyi, 1944Not re-checked C 2/5 — The Great Transformation, Farrar & Rinehart, New York, from memory; the summary is a paraphrase from memory.
Check · Philippon, 2019Mostly true, contested C 4/5 claim · 3/5 critique — Claim from a review; book not read. Side by side, Jan Eeckhout (Journal of Economic Literature, May 2020): EU data start about 2000 and both regions look alike in 2000–10, so the evidence does "not back up this claim"; concentration is a poor proxy.
Check · Ostrom, Nobel 2009True C 5/5 — Ostrom "for her analysis of economic governance, especially the commons".

Where it breaks: Polanyi gives a diagnosis, not a test; Philippon's data are disputed; whether Ostrom's local cases scale to a continent is the compiler's open question.

3

One case that holds both truths

Thalidomide and the 1962 US law 1957–1973

Thalidomide, a sedative, was sold in West Germany as Contergan by Grünenthal from 1957 and caused severe birth defects (from memory). In the US, FDA officer Frances Kelsey did not formally "block" it: she withheld approval of the Kevadon application pending safety data. The Kefauver–Harris Amendments of 1962, requiring proof of effectiveness as well as safety (from memory), followed; the bill predated thalidomide, which broke the political deadlock.

Check · Thalidomide and Kefauver–HarrisMostly true C 4/5 — In force 7 February 1963 (FDA history; embryo.asu.edu). Launch and signing dates not given by the sources.

Then the cost. Sam Peltzman (1973) found new chemical entities fell from 41.5 a year (1951–62) to 16.1 (1963–70), about 61% fewer, a net cost to consumers equal to a 5–10% tax on drug purchases.

Check · Peltzman, 1973True C 4/5 — Dollar totals seen only in summaries are not quoted.
Other viewsGrabowski, Vernon and Thomas (1978): Peltzman put the whole shortfall on the law and ignored supply-side causes (research running dry, rising testing costs, thalidomide itself); they use the United Kingdom as control.

Lesson (compiler's inference): there are two errors, approving a harmful drug and delaying a good one. The first has identifiable victims; the second has none. Politics tends to count one.

The other direction: US cases of thin oversight

Check · OxyContinTrue C 4/5 — FDA approval 12 December 1995. Purdue Frederick and three executives pleaded guilty to misbranding on 10 May 2007; on 21 October 2020 Purdue agreed to plead guilty to three counts. These facts show company misconduct, not thin FDA oversight.
Check · Boeing 737 MAXPartly confirmed C 4/5 — House Transportation and Infrastructure Committee report, 16 September 2020: "grossly insufficient oversight by the FAA" (Federal Aviation Administration). This is delegation, not an absence of rules; full report not checked.
Check · Supplements law of 1994True C 5/5 — Under the Dietary Supplement Health and Education Act (DSHEA), "FDA does not have the authority to approve dietary supplements before they are marketed".

Compiler-chosen examples, not a sample.

4

A test for any rule

The compiler's synthesis of the four schools.

  • Which failure does it fix? Information gap, uncaught cost or missing competition (A, D).
  • Who bears the harm, and can they protect themselves?
  • What does it cost, including invisibly? Compliance, and products never launched.
  • Ex ante or after the fact? A permit suits irreversible harms; liability suits small, compensable ones.
  • Who benefits? Mainly incumbent firms is a warning sign.
5

The EU's problem: what the evidence says

Is the burden high, and can anyone measure it?

Mario Draghi's 2024 competitiveness report says "the regulatory burden on European companies is high and continues to grow" (quoted by the think tank Bruegel). The numbers around it are weaker.

Check · Draghi on regulatory burdenMostly true, partly unverified C 2/5 figures · 4/5 gist — Second-hand here (TechPolicy.Press; the earlier Draghi long read read the report directly): 55% of small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) name regulation as their top challenge; about 100 tech laws and over 270 digital regulators. The second-hand source gives about 100 tech-focused laws. "Gold-plating" was not found: Unverifiable.
Check · "~13,000 EU acts vs ~3,500 US laws"Misleading C 3/5 — The US count omits agency rules, and the report itself says direct comparison is prevented by different legal systems. A critic puts the EU count nearer 30,000.
Other viewsBruegel (Bucher and Golberg, January 2026): EU-wide cost totals cannot be calculated reliably; the problem is process, with only about 60% of proposals getting impact assessments. Two years on: 12 simplification packages proposed, the first adopted February 2026 (C 3/5); trackers disagree: JEDI about 26% overall (0 of 30 key recommendations fully), EPIC 15.7% fully and 41.3% including partial (C 3–4/5).

Who bears it, and the data law

Check · Small firms bear mostTrue (direction) C 4/5 — Bundesbank, July 2026 (about 7,000 German firms): bureaucracy cost rose from about 5% of turnover (2022) to 7% (2024), hitting firms under EUR 10m sales hardest. Caveats: Germany only, association not causation. Side by side: European Investment Bank (2025, via Bruegel) puts EU regulatory time at 1.1% of turnover, 1.8% for SMEs.
Check · Data protection law and venture capitalMostly true C 3/5 — After the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR, May 2018), a National Bureau of Economic Research paper (WP 33909) finds a 19.2% relative fall in financing rounds by US investors without EU participation, in data-exposed EU industries, 2014–19. A think-tank headline says 13%. Caveat: US investors only. The concentration result (Johnson, Shriver, Goldberg) is unverified.

The AI law, devices and precaution

Check · AI Act and Digital OmnibusMostly true, needs updating C 5/5 — Council approval 29 June 2026 (an EEAS repost says 30 June). Stand-alone high-risk duties move from 2 August 2026 to 2 December 2027, those in products to 2 August 2028; transparency duties still start 2 August 2026; medical devices stay in scope. One note could not confirm Official Journal publication, another lists it in force 27 July 2026 via secondary sources: Open.
Other viewsBEUC: "simplification should not become a cover for deregulation", while welcoming the new image-abuse ban. Mariniello (Bruegel): "no convincing evidence" that rights protection explains weak EU AI; refine the Act, do not weaken it.
Check · Medical Device Regulation (MDR)Mostly true on bottleneck; effect size Open C 3/5 — The Commission itself proposed an overhaul on 16 December 2025 (notified-body fee cuts, no five-year certificate cap), conceding the burden. No hard figures on approvals or shortages were found, so none are stated.
Check · Precautionary principleMostly true C 5/5 Treaty · 3/5 Communication — Article 191(2) of the Treaty on the Functioning of the EU (TFEU), in the Environment title, says policy "shall be based on the precautionary principle". The Commission's Communication COM(2000) 1 extends its use; the Treaty does not make it a general principle.

The single market, and what works

Check · Barriers inside the EUMisleading as usually quoted C 4/5 — The International Monetary Fund (IMF, 2024): about 45% for goods (US states about 15%), about 110% for services, in tariff-equivalent terms (the tax that would have the same effect). A Centre for Economic Policy Research column on the IMF's working-paper version says this includes home bias, not only policy, and puts the sector figure at 30–32%.
Check · EU wins for consumersPartly verified C 3/5 — USB-C charger rule: the Commission's own estimate is about EUR 250m a year saved (single source). Philippon, Perrot and Jean (2019): about 8% more purchasing power for EU consumers in 2000–15 (from stable concentration and margins; the same US-versus-EU comparison Eeckhout disputes, see D); "competition policy is not the cause of Europe's troubles". Roaming, REACH (chemicals) and the Digital Markets Act: Unverifiable.
Check · The Brussels effectTrue C 3/5 — Coined by Anu Bradford in 2012; book from Oxford University Press, March 2020. EU rules become global standards (Bradford's thesis; only the coinage and book date were checked).

6

Stepping back

What the evidence supports, and where it is thin. It supports three things: compliance costs are real and fall hardest on small firms; some EU rules have measurable costs (GDPR and US-investor financing); some are credited with working (USB-C on the Commission's own estimate; competition policy per Philippon, Perrot and Jean, whose US-versus-EU comparison Eeckhout disputes; the global reach of EU standards is Bradford's thesis, not tested here). It is thin on the headline claim: no reliable EU-wide cost figure exists, the best effects are measured narrowly (US investors, German firms), and the single-market number is misread.

What survives

  • Compliance costs fall hardest on small firms (Bundesbank and European Investment Bank agree in direction).
  • Thalidomide and Peltzman together: both errors exist, one is easier to see (Peltzman's size estimate is contested, Grabowski et al. 1978).

What to discount

  • Market failure (A): diagnosis is not a cure. Government failure (B): shows the state can fail, not that it usually does.
  • Ordoliberalism (C): "the bridge" is the compiler's label.
  • Power (D): Philippon's claim is contested by Eeckhout.
  • "EU over-regulates": no reliable EU-wide cost total; act counts misleading; 45%/110% includes home bias.
  • "Regulation costs more than it saves": Peltzman's size estimate is contested (Grabowski et al.).
  • "Capitalism harms": the US cases are compiler-chosen; Boeing was delegation; OxyContin shows company misconduct.
  • "EU regulation works": the USB-C figure is the Commission's own single source; the 8% rests on the contested Philippon comparison; the Brussels effect was checked only for its coinage; BEUC, Mariniello and Bradford are one note each, arguments untested.
  • The compiler: chose the schools, their order and the examples; written with an Anthropic model, and Anthropic is regulated by the AI Act.
