Markets & Macro · Weekly

A Democratic sweep ends a week of tech share slumps and drags oil under $100

Three separate shocks hit in seven days. Disappointing results and cash-burn worries from big technology companies knocked shares down at the end of October. The US midterm elections (the national vote halfway through a president's term) then handed Democrats both houses of Congress, and shares, led by the Dow Jones Industrial Average (an index of 30 large US companies), had their best day in more than a year. The oil price, which had been driving inflation worries for a month, fell sharply as traders bet that the Iran war edges toward an end. Along the way the European Central Bank (the euro area's central bank) held its rate while its president announced she is leaving, and a record theft at the crypto exchange Binance pushed bitcoin (a digital currency) down before it recovered. Still ahead: the Bank of England's decision today, whose result was not yet available, and the US jobs report tomorrow.

US equities · Midterm electionsTue 3 – Wed 4 Nov 2026

The Dow has its best day in 19 months as Democratic wins send oil down and big technology up

Shares ended Tuesday 3 November mixed, then jumped on Wednesday 4 November after Democrats won control of both houses of Congress. The Dow Jones Industrial Average rose 3.03%, its best day since April 2025, the S&P 500 1.58% and the Nasdaq Composite 1.44%. Traders sold energy shares (down about 4.8%) and bought companies that gain from cheaper fuel and steadier policy: shares of consumer-spending companies (retail, cars, leisure) gained about 4.3%. Futures (contracts on where the market will open) had fallen about 1% overnight before reversing.

The gain did not lift every AI-related share. Microsoft rose about 6.7% and Amazon about 6.1%, while builders of data centres (buildings full of AI computers) fell: Hut 8 about 23%, IREN (a data-centre operator) 11%, Core Scientific 10%.

Context & background: This briefing covers the market reaction, not the politics. Tuesday's session was calm: the S&P 500 gained 0.4% to 7,568.92 and the Nasdaq 0.8% as chip shares bounced after a sell-off, while the Dow slipped 0.4% to 51,786.72. Once results showed a clear Democratic win, the first reaction was nervous. Global shares and the dollar fell, and investors bought US government bonds and gold for safety. By Wednesday morning that had flipped into a relief-driven rise, first in Asia, helped by President Trump's calming comments after the result. A strategist at Capital.com, a trading firm, said the recovery in Nasdaq futures showed investors recognising that a change in who controls Congress does not translate straight into a change in economic policy.

The details: Nine of the S&P 500's eleven sectors rose; energy and, in the session's later hours, industrials were the weakest. Reuters, as quoted by The Market Breakdown, said investors were weighing tighter limits on AI building. The idea is that a slower rollout of data centres favours established platforms with large cash flows (Microsoft, Amazon) and squeezes smaller operators that carry debt. That is a reading of the reaction, not a rule that has been passed: an election result is not a law changing building approvals, and The Market Breakdown's author says as much. A second effect was oil: Brent settled at $94.62, down 8.7%, so fuel-sensitive companies gained and oil producers lost. Apple, Amazon, Microsoft and Alphabet were among the Dow's biggest gainers; only four of its 30 members fell. TheStreet's reporters also warn the market may be too optimistic about technology, because Democrats have shown willingness to lead on pauses in data-centre construction.

Why it still matters: One day does not settle direction. The S&P 500 ended Wednesday still about 1.7% below its 6 October record of 7,818.93, and the Nasdaq is below where it stood a week ago. What would confirm the move is a second day of buying without oil rebounding. The honest uncertainty is whether the oil drop and the split inside AI shares persist once the election headlines fade.

US equities · ChipsFri 30 Oct 2026

Intel's worst day since 1974 drags the Nasdaq down 2.9% in the sharpest sell-off since June

Intel, the chip maker, fell about 29% on Friday 30 October after forecasting fourth-quarter sales of $15–16 billion against the roughly $17 billion analysts expected. It blamed a slump in personal-computer demand and a factory accident in Israel. The S&P 500 dropped 1.8%, its worst day since early June, and the Nasdaq Composite 2.9%. Reddit, the social network, fell about 25–29% on a weak sales forecast, and Amazon's results added to worries about AI spending (see Earnings).

Government bond yields (the interest rate the government pays to borrow) also fell as investors sought safety: the ten-year yield was 4.917% on Friday, 0.07 percentage points lower on the day. Drug maker Eli Lilly (+11% on the week) and Regeneron (+11.6% on the day) were rare winners. Sources differ slightly on the exact closing figures: TheStreet's table shows the S&P 500 at 7,578.97 against the Associated Press's 7,580.07; we use the latter.

Why it still matters: The shock hit exactly where the market was most concentrated. When a few large companies carry the index, one disappointing forecast spreads to the whole chip group. Wednesday's election rally recovered about half of Friday's S&P 500 loss, but chips did not join it (the chip index fell about 3.1% on Wednesday). Whether Intel's problem is specific (personal computers, one factory) or a sign that AI and other chip demand is cooling is not resolved by this evidence.

European Central BankThu 29 Oct 2026

The European Central Bank holds at 2.50% as euro-area inflation reaches 4% and Lagarde announces her exit

The European Central Bank (the central bank of the 21 countries that use the euro) left its deposit rate, the interest it pays banks that park money with it, unchanged at 2.50% on Thursday 29 October, after two rises this year, the latest in September. The decision was unanimous after a discussion of a rise, and the bank said it was "not pre-committing to a particular rate path". Hours earlier its president, Christine Lagarde, said she will leave on 7 February 2027, about nine months before her term ends. Six days later, on 4 November, the official first estimate showed euro-area inflation (the annual rise in consumer prices) at 4% for October, up from 3.8% and the highest since September 2023.

Context & background: The euro area has raised its rate twice in 2026 because the Iran war pushed energy prices, and with them inflation, well above the bank's 2% target. The usual argument for raising rates is that it cools demand and slows price rises. This time the bank is also boxed in by France: French ten-year borrowing costs rose above 5% on 21 October, the first time since 2002, as a divided parliament struggles to pass a budget, and the credit-rating agency Moody's kept France's rating but with a negative outlook. The Dutch bank ING (Internationale Nederlanden Groep) estimates that the rise in bond yields since September has done the work of a 0.25 percentage point rate rise in Germany and nearer 0.50 in France, so the bond market has partly done the central bank's job.

The details: In its statement the bank said energy prices "have continued to rise, posing upside risks to the inflation outlook and downside risks to the growth outlook", that wages show no material response yet, and that financing conditions have tightened. It repeated that its never-used Transmission Protection Instrument, a tool to buy a country's bonds when borrowing costs jump for no sound reason, is available. Its portfolio of bonds keeps shrinking. For October, Eurostat (the European Union's statistics office) put energy at about 1.9 of the 4.0 percentage points of inflation. Italy rose from 4.2% to 5.6%, Belgium to 5.2%, and France from 3.4% to 3.8%, while Germany bucked the trend. Underlying inflation held at 2.5%, as forecast. Markets expect one more rise to 2.75% by December, and Trading Economics reports that markets treat two more rises by April 2027 as certain (we have not checked its figures). Lagarde's successor race is led by Spain's Pablo Hernández de Cos and the Netherlands' Klaas Knot, according to Euronews; two other board seats, including the chief economist's, open in the first half of 2027.

Why it still matters: With underlying inflation steady and energy doing most of the damage, the bank's next move depends on oil. Brent's fall of nearly 9% on 4 November came after the 4% figure was compiled, so it is not in these data. The next meeting is in December, and a leadership change in February adds uncertainty for the French bond market just as French politics is stressed. One gap: we found no independent reporting of the bank's own press-conference transcript beyond Euronews; the bank's statement was not read directly.

Brent crude falls 8.7% to $94.62 in a day on bets that the Iran war is nearing an end

Brent crude (the standard international oil price, quoted per barrel of about 159 litres) settled at $94.62 on Wednesday 4 November, down $8.98, and traded under $100 for the first time in weeks. The US benchmark, West Texas Intermediate (the main US oil price), closed at $88.12, down 8.3%. Lower oil would reduce the case for further interest-rate rises at the Federal Reserve and the European Central Bank, and is the main reason shares and bitcoin rose on Wednesday. Watch whether Brent holds under $100 and whether new tanker incidents are reported.

A relief-driven jump with a split underneath it

1. The election moved shares more than any company did. The Dow's 3.03% gain on 4 November was its best since April 2025, yet the S&P 500 is still about 1.7% below its record and the Nasdaq below last week's level. One day is a reaction, not a trend.

2. The split inside AI is the lasting signal. Microsoft and Amazon rose, data-centre builders and chip shares fell, and Alphabet and Amazon were marked down on cash burn a week earlier. The market is now sorting AI companies by who can pay for it, one at a time.

3. Oil fell on hope, not on supply. Brent at $94.62 is $22 below the $116.72 of 23 October, but the cause is traders betting on diplomacy; Iran said talks are far from a deal and the shipping lanes are still disrupted. Our Geopolitics briefing covers the Strait of Hormuz.

4. Central banks are still leaning toward higher rates. The European Central Bank held at 2.50% with inflation at 4%, the Federal Reserve held with bettors giving a December rise 77.5%, and the Bank of Canada raised. Cheaper oil is the one thing that could change that.

5. Some figures are missing. No closing level for the DAX (Germany's main share index), no Wednesday gold close, no Bank of England result, and no exact share moves for Amazon on 30 October or Apple on 3 November could be found. Several macro figures come from one aggregator.

6. The next ten days carry the test. The US jobs report (6 November), the consumer price index (10 November), the published minutes (written record) of the Federal Reserve's last meeting (around 18 November), and a reported Anthropic listing in the week of 16 November all come before the Federal Reserve decides on 9 December.

Next to watch: today's Bank of England decision, whether Brent holds under $100, whether bitcoin keeps $80,000, and the jobs report tomorrow.
