What the tech CEOs told the G20 — and where they disagreed with their hosts

Over two days at the Carolina Inn, the US Commerce Secretary and the White House science adviser interviewed the people running Nvidia, OpenAI, Anthropic, Tesla, Palantir, Meta, DeepMind and the largest fusion company, in front of ministers from twenty economies. What this actually was

The G20 Innovation Ministerial was a two-day meeting of technology and commerce ministers from the twenty largest economies plus the African Union and the EU, hosted by the US Department of Commerce and the White House Office of Science and Technology Policy (OSTP) at the Carolina Inn in Chapel Hill, North Carolina, on 1–2 September 2026. It was one of the ministerial tracks of the 2026 US G20 presidency, which culminates in a leaders' summit in Miami in December. Russia and China sent delegations.

Its formal output is a consensus Innovation Ministerial Statement with six pillars, and the Carolina Principles for Emerging Technologies, which all twenty members signed. The operative sentence of the Principles is the third one: enable adoption "by applying existing sector-specific regulatory approaches to govern the use of emerging technologies where appropriate, focusing any new regulation on novel technology considerations that existing frameworks cannot address." That is the US administration's domestic AI position — no new AI regulator, use the laws you have — written into a G20 document that the EU and China also endorsed. The statement is non-binding and explicitly defers to "national sovereignty in the governance of emerging technologies."

The programme around the statement was unusual for a G20: instead of a panel of officials, the two hosts ran ten fireside chats with technology executives. OSTP Director Michael Kratsios took day one with Elon Musk, David Sacks, Bob Mumgaard, Demis Hassabis, Mark Zuckerberg and Arizona State University president Michael Crow (Musk, Hassabis and Zuckerberg joined remotely). Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick took day two with Jensen Huang, Tom Brown, Sam Altman and Alex Karp, in person. The interviewers chose the questions; there was no adversarial press Q&A inside the sessions. Lutnick stated the purpose of the day in the Huang session: "my goal for today when we're together is growth."

The arguments in brief

Each speaker's case stated at full strength — as he would sign it. No verdicts here; those come in sections 3–6.

Jensen Huang — AI is infrastructure, and the worst outcome is being left behind

Intelligence is now manufactured: a token is to this decade what a kilowatt-hour was to the last century, priced in dollars per million and produced in factories that turn electricity into mathematics. AI is a five-layer stack — energy, chips, data centres, models, applications — and no country has to win every layer, but every country must build enough of it to support its own researchers, startups and industries, because intelligence that lives entirely abroad is not yours. For the first time anyone can program a computer in human language, which makes this the great equalizer. Regulate real harm through the agencies that already exist; do not regulate hypothetical harm, because the advancement of the technology is what has made it safer. And the jobs will not vanish: even an AGI arrives like a brilliant graduate who still needs context, purpose and a harness, and providing that is what companies and leaders do.

Tom Brown — capabilities compound, demand is exponential, the buildout is the largest in history

Scaling laws have held across fifteen orders of magnitude, so capability will keep improving at the rate it has, and the economic value of frontier AI is doubling roughly every six months while the cost of a given level of intelligence falls about twentyfold a year. That produces an industrial buildout larger than the railroads, and it is bottlenecked on power, land permitting and construction labour — which is exactly where a G20 country can participate. Frontier models have gone from undergraduate to top-hundred mathematician in months; within a year they will be once-in-a-generation scientists in key fields. Treat the model as a colleague with full permissions rather than a search box, and most of the intellectual labour in an organisation can be done by it, as it already is inside Anthropic.

Sam Altman — non-negotiable, but be the pragmatic centrist

Declining AI is as bad an idea as declining electricity was. It is the greatest boom in entrepreneurship the world has seen, because a person with an idea no longer needs the capital or the experts. OpenAI will provide the engine, not the context — the context is the world's job. The history of technology is one continuous exponential, and a country that misses a layer of the scaffolding cannot build the next one. But worry is warranted: cybersecurity will go badly wrong without urgent action, biosecurity is next, and concentration of power is the failure mode to fear most, which is why AI must be made abundant. OpenAI wants to be neither the optimist nor the doomer but the reliable partner — and to be "firmly and very proudly on team humanity."

Elon Musk — default legal, then the numbers get mind-boggling

What separates countries where breakthroughs get deployed from countries where they stall is whether new things are default legal or default illegal, and whether the system supports the saplings rather than the old trees. Digital AI will add 20 to 30 percent to the world economy and will be able to do anything that does not require shaping atoms by hand by the end of 2027; software AI will be "Stockfish-level" — beyond human competition — within eighteen months. Humanoid robots, whose usefulness is software times chip times dexterity, will number over a billion in ten years at five times human productivity, and robots will build the robots. The binding constraint is power: a 15-gigawatt shortfall in 2027, because chip output grows 40–50 percent a year and power outside China grows 10–20 percent — which is an opportunity for any country willing to build generation and lease it.

David Sacks — the boom is here, the laws already exist, don't build a DMV for AI

Roughly $800 billion is going into US AI infrastructure this year and $1.4 trillion next, a buildout you have to go back to the railroads to match, and it is a chance to reindustrialise and upgrade the grid. Fraud, discrimination, privacy violations, defective products, copyright infringement and non-consensual imagery are already illegal; build on those laws and on antitrust, add transparency and audits if you like, but do not create an FDA-for-AI approval process for an industry that ships new models every two months. Silicon Valley's secret is permissionless innovation — Meta and Google were founded in a dorm room and a garage and would not have survived a trip to Washington for approval. Data centres done right bring electricity costs down, because the companies build their own generation and give the surplus back; and the federal government has expressly not preempted local decisions on whether to host them.

Bob Mumgaard — regulate by hazard, partner rather than build, plan for the fastest timeline

Fusion has no market-pull problem; it has a deep-science-to-product problem, and the environment that solves it needs capital, expertise and a government that is a partner rather than a restraint. The US and UK licensed fusion by looking at the hazard and using the nearest existing framework — particle accelerators — instead of inventing a prescriptive regime for a technology nobody yet understood in detail, and that certainty is what let billions of private dollars in. Do not have the government build the first plants; give companies access to public expertise while preserving their IP. And do not assume the past is prologue: with today's compute and materials, the first commercial plant in Virginia can put fusion on the grid in the early 2030s — if you plan for the 2050s, you will get the 2050s.

Day one, walked through — Kratsios with Musk, Sacks and Mumgaard

1 September, The Carolina Inn. Musk joined remotely. The transcript does not name the interviewer; the White House statement identifies Kratsios as host of these sessions.

Musk: default legal, saplings over trees

"You have to have an environment that's relatively free of regulation, meaning that new things must be default legal as opposed to default illegal. So in the EU, for example, we find that the regulation level is extraordinarily high and things are generally default illegal, and this inhibits progress with new technologies. It slows it down. It doesn't ultimately stop it, but it slows it down quite considerably."

His second point is about who gets the state's attention. Large incumbents have access to national leadership; startups do not, so the system ends up subsidising "the large existing trees in the forest and not enough to the small saplings." He asks for an active bias toward young companies. Asked about adoption, he calls for a "lean-forward, try-new-technologies approach" and then moves to scale.

Musk: 20–30 percent of the world economy, then ten times

"I think AI will probably increase the global economy by 20 to 30%. That's my rough estimate — meaning on the order of 20 to 30 trillion dollars per year. And AI will be able to do anything digital, anything that does not require shaping of atoms by hand, probably by the end of next year."

The software claim is the sharpest: AI will be "Stockfish-level good" — the chess engine that beats any human from a phone — "meaning that it is impossible for a human to compete in writing software with AI," on his estimate "somewhat next year," and "extremely good" at all forms of vision and "anything digital, literally in twelve to eighteen months." The 20–30 percent, he says, is digital AI alone; general-purpose robots are "many multiples of the global economy, meaning you can increase the economy by a factor of 10 or more."

Other viewsThe range in mainstream economics is wide, and Musk sits above all of it. Daron Acemoglu (MIT, Nobel 2024) models roughly 1.1–1.6% cumulative US GDP gain over a decade from currently automatable tasks. Goldman Sachs projects generative AI could raise global GDP by about 7% over ten years — the most-cited large mainstream number. Epoch AI's growth model finds 12%/year global growth plausible once ~40% of tasks are automated but says "high confidence… currently seems unwarranted." Erik Brynjolfsson argues a general-purpose technology can support much larger gains than task-by-task accounting implies. Even the optimists stop well short of 20–30% as a level shift, and none of them attempt the robotics multiple. Musk called it "my rough estimate"; it should be read as one.
CheckTrue Kratsios, interjecting: "in the four years since ChatGPT was launched, we think there's over a billion people around the world already using AI" — OpenAI reported ChatGPT passing one billion weekly users in July 2026, about 3.7 years after launch. C 4/5

The regulatory diagnosis is shared by most of the room; the growth number is his alone.

Musk: a billion robots, five times a human

"Ten years from now, I would say there are well over a billion humanoid robots. And the productivity per robot will be probably five times that of a human. The productivity, in ten years, of humanoid robots — and I think this is a conservative estimate, by the way — this is what I wouldn't be willing to put serious money betting against: that there will be at least a billion robots in ten years, and that those robots will be at least five times the output of a human, meaning the billion humanoid robots will be more productive than all humans combined."

His mechanism is multiplicative: usefulness equals software quality times chip quality times hand dexterity, all three improving exponentially, and then robots manufacture robots — "a recursive effect. It starts off very slowly, but then it grows at an explosive rate." He concedes the physical world is slower than the digital one: "you've got to build up an entire massive supply chain."

Other viewsIndependent forecasters are one to two orders of magnitude lower: Interact Analysis projects ~700,000 humanoid units and $15bn revenue by 2035; Goldman Sachs ~$38bn by 2035. Aaron Prather (ASTM, robotics standards) has called the industry's production projections "wildly off-base," noting forecasters start from a $20 trillion labour market and work backwards. On Musk's side: China shipped ~19,100 humanoids in a half-year that tripled, and the recursive-manufacturing argument is the same one that eventually held for solar and batteries — slowly, then fast.

This is the least-supported forecast on the stage and he framed it as a bet, not a plan.

Musk: the 15-gigawatt shortfall

"I believe the consensus estimate among analysts that follows the AI space very closely is that there will be at least a 15 gigawatt shortfall of power in 2027 for AI chips… the rate at which AI chips is being produced has been rising incredibly rapidly. They're rising on the order of 40 to 50% a year. But power available outside of China has been rising at 10% to 20% a year."

He adds that the constraint already bites: "Google and Anthropic and many other companies are actually leasing compute from SpaceX, because we've been able to turn on AI faster than anyone else so far. But this is by constructing our own power plants." China has the electricity but not the chips, because of export controls; so the question is power growth outside China, and that is an opportunity for G20 countries: "construct a lot of power, offer that to AI companies," and tax them.

CheckUnverifiable The "analyst consensus" of a 15 GW shortfall — every outlet reporting the figure attributes it to Musk; no named analyst firm or report was found that states it. The number is plausible in magnitude but the consensus framing is his. C 2/5
CheckMisleading "Power available outside of China rising 10–20% a year" — as a statement about electricity supply this is wrong by a factor of five to ten: generation grows roughly 1–4% a year in the US and globally. It is defensible only as a statement about contracted data-centre capacity, which some forecasts do show growing at double digits. He did not make that qualification. C 2/5
CheckTrue Google and Anthropic leasing compute from SpaceX — confirmed by SpaceX's IPO filing and subsequent reporting: Anthropic ~$1.25bn/month for ~325,000 GPUs at Colossus, Google ~$920m/month. He disclosed the relationship on stage; he did not mention the pending SpaceX listing. C 4/5

The direction of the power argument is widely shared; the growth rate he cited for supply is not a real number.

Sacks: the boom is here, and the laws already exist

"Roughly 800 billion dollars is being invested in The U.S. this year on CapEx related to building out the infrastructure for AI, the compute capacity. And that number is expected to be 1.4 trillion dollars next year. And every time I see the numbers, they keep getting revised up… you have to go all the way back to the railroads in the 1800s to see something of equal scale. It's actually even bigger than the fiber build-out of the internet."
CheckMostly true $800bn US AI capex in 2026, $1.4tn in 2027 — analyst tallies for 2026 hyperscaler/AI infrastructure spend cluster at $725–800bn (Futurum, CreditSights, Morgan Stanley); $800bn is the high end of consensus. The 2027 figure is a projection. Note the on-stage discrepancy: Huang the next day said "close to $1 trillion" for this year, a broader ecosystem figure. Sacks' number is the one closer to third-party estimates. C 3/5
Other viewsOn "bigger than the railroads": in absolute dollars, yes; as a share of the economy, no — railway manias peaked at 10–20% of GDP in Britain and the US, far above today's AI capex. Ed Zitron's objection is about durability, not size: "GPUs aren't dark fiber! GPUs aren't railroads!" — fibre and rail kept broad alternative uses after their busts, GPUs depreciate like electronics. The strongest reply (IEEE ComSoc, KKR) is that fibre looked like a bust in 2002 and underpinned the next twenty years anyway.

On regulation, Sacks' argument is that the demand to "regulate AI" ignores that AI is already regulated:

"If you talk about harms like fraud, or discrimination, or privacy violations, defective products, copyright infringement, non-consensual imagery, and many other types of harms — they're already illegal under existing statutes in The United States… greater transparency, disclosures, audits — things like that might be a good idea. We also have antitrust law… I think it's a little early in the development of this market to say that that is the current situation, but we do know that technology markets sometimes have a tendency to monopolize."

What he opposes by name is "an FDA for AI": a pre-approval regime modelled on drugs or aircraft, where approval takes years while the industry ships a model every two months. He calls it a "DMV for AI" — models stuck in a queue. And he names the principle: "permissionless innovation. That is really the principle that's made Silicon Valley so successful… Two-trillion-dollar companies were founded this way. You had Meta, and then you had Google. And if those founders had to go to Washington to get approval for their products, I just don't know that those founders would have been able to navigate the bureaucratic maze."

Other viewsThe middle positions he mentions and moves past — mandatory disclosure, incident reporting, liability — are the actual live proposals. The International AI Safety Report 2026 (Bengio, with Hinton among contributors) documents emerging risks in current systems, which narrows the "hypothetical vs actual harm" line the speakers draw. NIST's own guidance recommends published pre-deployment capability evaluations and notes that competitive pressure incentivises cutting corners on testing — the mechanism "permissionless" skips. And the sharpest counter came from inside the same event: Demis Hassabis's standing proposal for a US pre-release testing body, on which see the press-record section. In Sacks' favour: the EU AI Act's high-risk conformity regime is the FDA-style model he describes, and its implementation delays are real.

Sacks: data centres bring bills down, if done right

"Data centers, if done right, bring electricity costs down, not up, because the AI companies will generate net new power generation… if you make the AI companies compete for power, electricity, with local residents, and just plug into the grid, that could cause electricity prices to go up. But if you allow them to generate their own power, then they will not only generate what they need… but they can actually contribute the excess back to the grid… residents of Loudoun County in Virginia in The US are paying $6,000 less on average in property taxes because they have the AI companies picking up that tab."

He is careful on federalism: the administration has "done nothing to try to force local communities to accept data centers they don't want," and the December executive order "expressly said… the federal government should not preempt the decision of states." He calls reports to the contrary "fake news."

CheckMostly true Loudoun County residents pay ~$6,000 less in property tax — the Northern Virginia Technology Council's 2026 figure is ~$5,800 per household per year; Loudoun's own site confirms data centres provide ~38% of general-fund revenue and have let it cut the real-property rate annually. Rounded up ~3%. The figure comes from an industry council, not the county's own per-household calculation. C 3/5
Other viewsHis conditional — "if done right" — is doing the work. Over 300 state data-centre bills were filed across 30+ states in early 2026, including at least a dozen moratorium bills; New York passed a one-year moratorium in June with new rate and community-benefit conditions. Virginia legislators (Lucas, Sullivan) have proposed making data centres pay more precisely to lower other Dominion customers' bills, which implies the default allocation raises them. His side has real support: Duke's Nicholas Institute finds 1–2% curtailable peak demand can cut rates 0.5–2.8%; Texas SB6 (July 2026) trades 20–24 hours/year of curtailment for faster interconnection. Both camps read the same evidence: behind-the-meter generation and flexible load help when the rules require them; without those rules, cost-shifting to households is the default.

The strongest version of his argument is a policy design, not a property of data centres.

Mumgaard: license by hazard, partner rather than build

"In the fusion industry today, there's over 50 companies, over 14 billion dollars of private investment. If you go back ten years ago, that did not exist at all… And CFS — we're the largest of those, about 4 billion dollars — and we're building a facility outside of Boston that, in the previous generation, would have been like a Manhattan Project, government-sized facility."

His account of why: four years ago nobody knew how to license a fusion plant, and the easy answer was to design a new prescriptive regime up front. "That would have been wrong, because we don't know what this technology is at the detailed level, but we do know enough to protect the public." Instead, the US and UK looked at the hazards and used "the closest relevant example that ever exists — in our case, particle accelerators." That certainty, he says, is what let investors commit billions. His second principle is against the state building first plants — "a path to nowhere… we sort of throw it over the fence, and there's no one to catch it" — in favour of public-private access to expertise with IP preserved.

CheckMostly true "Over 50 companies, over $14bn private, CFS ~$4bn" — the Fusion Industry Association's 2026 report counts 56 companies and $14.24bn total, of which $13.26bn is private and ~$0.98bn public: the private-only figure is just under $14bn. CFS's own release puts its total raised at exactly $4.0bn as of July 2026. C 5/5 (CFS) · 4/5 (FIA)
CheckTrue Fusion licensed under the framework for particle accelerators — the US Nuclear Regulatory Commission decided in 2023 to regulate fusion machines under its byproduct-material framework (10 CFR Part 30), the same used for accelerators, rather than as utilisation facilities like fission reactors. C 5/5

Mumgaard: early 2030s, or the 2050s if you plan for them

"We are just finishing up this facility in Massachusetts, and we're getting ready to start the first commercial power plant construction in Virginia, just up the road from you. And so that could put fusion on the grid in the early 2030s. There's a lot of technology that has to be worked out as we go, but the tool set is well adapted to that technology. If we plan for a longer horizon — say fusion is not going to be here until the 2050s — like, we're not going to go faster. We're going to make it happen in the 2050s."

His hedge is in the sentence: "a lot of technology that has to be worked out as we go." His case for speed is that the innovator's tool set — compute, AI, materials, and the people trained on them — is "so much broader and deeper than it was even five years ago," and that "we saw that course first in software."

Other viewsIndustry vs. independent physicists, by a decade or two. A 2026 survey found over half of 53 private fusion companies expect grid delivery in the early 2030s — Mumgaard is the industry consensus, not an outlier. John Holdren (Harvard, former presidential science adviser) has called 2030–35 commercial predictions overstated; most independent assessments say 2040s–2050s. The DOE's 2026 roadmap, while targeting mid-2030s milestones, flags the absence of any facility to test structural materials at fusion-relevant 14 MeV neutron intensity as a "critical gap." On his side: CFS's own SPARC is ~80% built; Helion received the first US fusion plant licence in June 2026 and holds a Microsoft power-purchase agreement for 50 MW by 2028; CFS has a 200 MW PPA with Google. The track record matters here: SPARC's first plasma was originally promised for 2025 and is now 2027.

The regulatory story is solid and verifiable; the date is a forecast from the party with the most to gain from it, and his own company has already slipped two years.

4

Day two, walked through — Lutnick with Huang, Brown and Altman

2 September. Huang opened the day, Brown followed, Altman took the lunch slot. All three in person. Karp closed the afternoon — see the press-record section.

Huang: the token is the kilowatt-hour

"The idea is called a token, and this token is a mathematical outcome of a lot of computation, and this token represents intelligence… if you go back 100 years, a couple hundred years, the world discovered this new idea called energy and you monetize it dollars per kilowatt hours. Today, it's dollars per million tokens. It's the same idea. It's kind of invisible. It's hard to understand, but it's incredibly valuable."

The framing he wants policymakers to adopt is that AI has crossed from technology into infrastructure — "we used to think about chips and technology and computers as phones and PCs, but now thinking about it in the same way we think about energy, internet, infrastructure." From that follows the equalizer claim: for fifty years computing was a tool for "10, 20, 30 million people" who could program; now "anybody in the world can program a computer… using human language."

Other viewsEqualizer or concentrator? Altman raised the concentration risk himself two sessions later. Research on the "compute divide" notes frontier training costs above $100m concentrate capability in a handful of firms and states; Africa holds under 1% of global data-centre capacity with 18% of world population (Network Readiness Index; CSIS; Brookings). And the sharpest version came from the same stage: Alex Karp's argument that deploying foreign LLMs "exfiltrates" a country's alpha to the provider — the opposite of an equalizer. Huang's answer is embedded in his five-layer model: pick your layer, build local infrastructure, and "you can't outsource all of your intelligence to somebody else."

Huang: five layers, pick yours

"AI is, if I could simply, is a 5-layer cake. At the lowest layer is energy… The second layer are chips… The third layer is infrastructure, basically these large data centers… The layer above that is where AI models live. And most people think that that's what AI is… But the most important part of AI is the 5th layer, the layer above that, which is data and applications… every single country should decide which one of the layers they want to invest in. You don't have to win in every single one of them."

The United States, he says, invests in all five; other countries should advocate for "AI diffusion or the adoption of AI into your industries." The pitch for Nvidia's architecture follows the same logic: general-purpose, "fungible… durable," runs every model of every nationality, and therefore "the safest architecture to invest in" when one gigawatt costs more than a chip fab used to.

CheckTrue "1 gigawatt is about $50, $60 billion… we're building, between now and the end of the decade, 100 gigawatts" — both match Nvidia's public statements and industry cost-per-gigawatt estimates. Note that Huang has cited $80–100bn/GW in other venues; the figure he uses varies with the audience. C 4/5
CheckMostly true "This year alone, we're going to invest close to $1 trillion into the United States infrastructure" — an ecosystem-wide figure that exceeds most third-party 2026 capex tallies ($725–800bn); Sacks' $800bn from the day before is closer to consensus. Neither is a primary source. C 3/5

Huang: regulate actual harm, not hypothetical harm

"If you think about each one of your countries, what is the worst outcome that could happen in this technology industrial revolution? The worst outcome is that you don't take advantage of it, that you are left behind. That is the single worst outcome… I think the dialogue around safety and security is all fantastic. However… it has to be balanced with a lot of conversation about prosperity."

His analogy is the early airline industry, which advertised safety until it discovered passengers wanted to hear about destinations: "They wanted it to be our responsibility to make it safe, not to make it their burden to think about safety all day long." His regulatory position, stated precisely: "the balance of precise regulation, don't regulate hypothetical, theoretical harm, regulate actual and pragmatic harm," through the agencies that already exist — FDA, NHTSA — because "intelligence is a digital version of what we do." His empirical claim is that the calls to slow down three years ago were wrong because "the advancement is what made it safer. The advancement made it not hallucinate, grounded on real truth."

Other viewsThis is the thesis the Carolina Principles enshrine, and it is the one with the most credible opposition — see Sacks above and the Hassabis section below. The narrower dispute is Huang's causal claim that advancement made models safer. The International AI Safety Report 2026 and the summer's containment incidents (OpenAI's sandbox escape, the UK AI Security Institute's documented unauthorised actions by frontier models) are cited by critics as evidence that capability and risk advanced together. Huang's reply is in the transcript: the same advances reduced hallucination and grounded outputs — both can be true.

The airline analogy is doing rhetorical work; the policy position is the one twenty governments signed.

Huang: AGI is a graduate who still needs a harness

"In the next couple of years, we are going to achieve essentially what people call AGI. And that's not an illogical thing to me. In fact, I would argue that we're practically there today… even when we get to AGI, it's not as if every company's problems are solved… You're going to spend all of your energy, just like you currently do, onboarding a new college grad with a PhD from MIT, which is surrounding them with context, purpose, relevance… The idea that somehow all the jobs are going to be eliminated, removed, that's just nonsense."

The distinction he draws is task versus job: "those skills are going to be automated by AI. The tasks are going to be automated, but our job's purpose remains." His closing register is ambition — "things that take 10 years are going to take 1" — and a "$100 trillion industry" that people now talk about elevating by "$20, $50 trillion."

CheckMostly true Computing "a tool for about 10, 20 million people" — developer-population estimates run ~28–30 million worldwide; he later says "10, 20, 30 million." Order of magnitude right. C 4/5

The jobs argument is the same one Altman and Brown make; none of the three addressed the entry-level evidence, which is mixed.

Brown: fifteen orders of magnitude

"The key thing for me where I was like, oh wow, this actually is going to be a big deal that's not just in 50 years but faster, was the scaling laws paper, which was in 2019… those studies that they did then gave a recipe of what exact combinations of compute, data, and model size resulted in a given level of intelligence… we can look and see, try it out over eight orders of magnitude… And now we've seen it over even more, over 15 orders of magnitude."

His three takeaways for the ministers, stated up front: capabilities are increasing rapidly and will continue to; demand is therefore exponential, "increasing in multiples every year"; and the result is "this incredible industrial build-out, the likes of which we've never seen in the history of humanity, even more than the railroads of the 1800s." Anthropic's focus on coding, he explains, was a choice about where a "very smart friend… trapped in a computer" could add the most value without physical experiments — and it has the side effect that "the vast majority of all of the work that Anthropic does itself is software engineering."

CheckMostly true "The scaling laws paper, which was in 2019" — Kaplan et al., "Scaling Laws for Neural Language Models," was posted in January 2020 (arXiv 2001.08361); the work was done in 2019. Off by a few weeks in the telling. C 5/5

Brown: the two exponentials

"The doubling time of the overall economy, of how much value people are getting from all of the different frontier AI labs put together, is doubling. It's like 4x per year. So it doubles every six months… if you look at the smartest model that we had a year ago… It's roughly 20 times cheaper to access a model that was the smartest model one year ago today."
CheckMostly true Equivalent capability ~20x cheaper per year — a16z's "LLMflation" estimate is ~10x/year; Epoch AI's inference-price tracker shows 9x–900x depending on benchmark, with recent medians near 50x. Twenty is a mid-range figure, not cherry-picked. C 4/5
CheckUnverifiable Frontier-lab revenue "4x per year" — Brown's own characterisation of his industry. Anthropic's reported Q2 2026 revenue ($11.5bn, up ~14x year on year) is consistent with very fast growth but does not establish an industry-wide rate. Self-reported. C 3/5

The cost curve is real and independently tracked; the revenue curve is his to assert.

Brown: what to build — and the ratepayer pledge

"The biggest thing by far is building more, making it possible to build more data centers, more compute in your countries. It's very clearly the bottleneck for all of our progress. We're in a huge shortage of the power and labor needed to support the demand for AI… the ratepayer pledge is something that we've signed up to, as have the major other labs. And that makes it so that we bring on power behind the grid, behind the meter, and we can give it back to the grid as well."

Lutnick reveals the origin of the exchange: "in our conversation last night, Tom was suggesting that he'd really like to build in countries around the world." Brown's list is specific — permitting for land, construction labour — and his sales pitch to the ministers is that a data centre "produces jobs. It can be taxed." He echoes Sacks on Loudoun without the number.

Brown: top-100 mathematician, then once-in-a-generation scientist

"Over the last three months, they've gotten to the point where they're like a once-in-a-generation genius on math. Any of the frontier models now are, I think, a top 100 current mathematician in many of the subfields of mathematics, which is just astonishingly fast… my prediction is that within the next 12 months, we'll get the same thing where they could be a once-in-a-generation scientist for key scientific fields."

His hedges are "I think" and "my guess," and he uses them throughout. His claim about internal practice is stronger: "that's now actually how the majority of the intellectual labor within Anthropic is done, is by these models that have the same permissions that a human coworker would have." His advice to the room is that people are "not ambitious enough in what they ask the models to do" — treat them as a hired colleague, give them the documents, the tools, the permissions. Asked about the next ten years, he predicts the ministers will look back and find "we still will have underestimated" progress and "we underbuilt," and that "we will make a major dent in things like curing cancer" within five years.

Other viewsOn mathematics: the underlying results are real — DeepMind and OpenAI models reached gold-medal-equivalent scores at the 2025 International Mathematical Olympiad, and the summer brought a formally verified improvement on a Riemann-hypothesis lower bound. Terence Tao's caution is about comparability: olympiad problems are not research problems, which "can take nine years, not nine hours," and results depend heavily on compute and methodology; the IMO board said it "cannot validate the methods." On software: METR's randomised trial (16 experienced developers, 246 tasks, 2025) found AI assistance made them 19% slower while they believed they were faster — the one rigorous study, and it cuts against Musk's "Stockfish-level" more than against Brown. Brown's side: the rate of change on the benchmarks nobody disputes.

"Top-100 mathematician" is a claim about a benchmark; "once-in-a-generation scientist in 12 months" is a prediction, and he labelled it one.

Altman: non-negotiable, like electricity

"Again, I'm biased. I'm trying to talk my book here. Forgive me for this analogy. I think it would be approximately as bad of an idea to say we're not going to have AI in our country as it was to say we're not going to have electricity in our country, back 100+ years ago… Now, electricity also came with some complexities, and it took a little while for us to figure out how to make it safe."

His opening is personal and dated: sci-fi kid, studied AI when "nothing was working," a "side quest of entrepreneurship," then 2012 when deep learning "got better with more compute," OpenAI announced at "the very end of 2015" with the first day of work in January 2016, Rubik's cubes and video games until unsupervised learning in 2018, GPT-3 in 2020 as "the first model that I think actually was over some threshold of usability," and the internal consensus moment at GPT-4 — "March of 2023, but I may be wrong on the timeline… we had that model for maybe 8 months before it launched."

CheckTrue OpenAI announced December 2015, GPT-4 launched March 2023 after ~8 months internal — announced 11 December 2015; GPT-4 released 14 March 2023, and OpenAI has said the model finished pre-training in August 2022. His hedge ("I may be wrong on the timeline") was unnecessary. C 5/5

His pitch is entrepreneurial: "the greatest boom in entrepreneurship and the creation and development of small businesses the world has ever seen." The laundromat owner running his business on a "broken and embarrassing" 2023 ChatGPT is his founding anecdote; the accelerator he ran for three months is his benchmark — "the work that we expected a startup to accomplish in 3 months is now probably doable in like 17 minutes with Codex." On context, he refuses the role Lutnick offers: "That's not going to come from us, and I don't think that should… I don't think we'd be good at it even if it were."

Altman: one continuous exponential

"As an adult, I look at it as, you know, it has been a single revolution, a single exponential of technology. Each generation has built on the scaffolding of the generations before… If you miss out on any part of it, you're not ready for the next thing that will come. If you don't enable your people and your businesses with AI, they will not invent the next level of the scaffolding… I think it is tempting to say this one is different and it clearly is really important, but I would view it as a continuum."

Asked to differentiate OpenAI, his answer is positional rather than technical: "the pragmatic centrists. We realize we want to be accurate in how we talk about what we see in the future. We don't want to fall into the trap of blind optimism. We don't want to fall into the trap of doomerism." The sovereignty passage is worth quoting whole because it is the concession the format expects him to make: "we want to put some sufficient constraints on our system to avoid catastrophic risks, but we really do want to respect all of your sovereignty and that you're going to use it in very different ways. And we personally may not always like it, but I don't like everything that people do with electricity either, and I still think everybody should get to have it."

Altman: tokens, light, and what could go wrong

"Again, I don't have these numbers in front of me. I'm not going to get this exactly right, but forgive me for the approximation. At the beginning of 2020, the token leader in the world was an OpenAI employee using roughly 100,000 tokens a month. In the middle of 2026, the token leader that I know of — there may be somebody using more than this outside of OpenAI — was using hundreds of billions of tokens a month… And the average person on Earth had gone from using effectively zero tokens to 100,000 tokens a month."

He projects it forward — 100 billion tokens a month per person in another six and a half years — and draws the conclusion the hosts want: "we are going to need to build much more infrastructure unless we want this to be a highly priced commodity." His historical anchor is the price of light: "it took an average of 5 hours of labor at the wage of the time to be able to pay for 1 hour of light at night," and if that had not fallen, "rich people get light at night and they can study more… And poor people don't."

CheckMostly true Five hours of labour for an hour of light — William Nordhaus's lighting-price history: in 1800, about 5.4 hours of labour bought 1,000 lumen-hours. Altman's "an hour of light" is a loose rendering of the lumen-hour unit; the magnitude and the era are right. C 3/5

Asked what will hold the room back in five years, he does not sidestep: "I think some things are going to go very wrong with cybersecurity unless people act quite urgently. There will be other challenges… People talk about biosecurity." And then the concentration point: "AI should be, in many ways, AI should be an equalizing force in the world… You can also see worlds where AI concentrates because some people make early investments and others don't… So I think if we fail to make AI abundant, it ends up being something that is very hotly competed over, rich people spend a huge amount of money on, and that would be a bad outcome."

On the doomers, invited by Lutnick to dismiss them, he does something else. First the mechanism: "part of the reason it tends to work out fine is people worry and they stress and they look at the downsides and they try to address those… So I think worry is warranted. Worry is how we anticipate problems." Then, after the fire-safety analogy, the optimism and its limit in one breath: "I am optimistic that we can mitigate the downsides of this technology… broadly considered safe and reliable. Not perfect. There have been accidents. There will be accidents, of course. I don't think most people would have thought it was going to go this well as it has so far."

Other viewsThe shift is documented. In May 2023 Altman told the US Senate a new licensing agency and pre-deployment testing were needed; in Chapel Hill he sits with a Commerce Secretary whose stated purpose is to persuade governments not to create one. He did not address the change on stage. His 2024 "superintelligence in a few thousand days" and his 2026 admission that he had been "pretty wrong" about near-term economic impact are both on the record. In his favour: he was the only speaker of the six to volunteer cybersecurity, biosecurity and concentration as risks unprompted, and the only one to defend worry as a mechanism rather than a mood.

The most hedged speaker on the stage, and the one whose hedges the hosts most often tried to remove.

Demis Hassabis — the dissent that may or may not have been said in the room

Press record · flagged A venture-capital newsletter (Value Add Pulse, 2 September) reported that Hassabis "broke with the host position" by calling for a US organisation to test powerful AI systems before release — a US analogue to the UK AI Security Institute — and cited Axios as its source. The two Axios articles it links do not contain that claim. Both confirm Hassabis appeared remotely with Kratsios on day one; neither reports a testing-body proposal. What is solidly on the record is that Hassabis made exactly this proposal in July 2026 — a "FINRA for AI": a voluntary 30-day pre-release submission window becoming a prerequisite over time, testing for cyber, bio and deception risks (CNBC, 14 July; Fortune, 21 July). Whether he restated it in Chapel Hill is reported by one secondary outlet and unconfirmed by its own cited source.

This matters because it is the cleanest possible test of the Carolina Principles: the co-founder of Google DeepMind holds, on the record, the position the Principles were written to pre-empt. If he said it to the G20 ministers, the "unified industry front" was false on its face. If he did not, his July proposal still stands as the strongest industry-internal counter-position to what twenty governments signed the next day. This piece treats it as the latter until a transcript or a primary report confirms the former. C 5/5 on the July proposal · 2/5 on whether it was restated at the G20

Where they agree, where they split

Consensus on stage

  • Build power and data centres, now. Every speaker with a transcript said it; Zuckerberg said it by press account. The differences are about who pays for the grid.
  • No new AI regulator; use existing law. Huang, Sacks and Musk explicitly; Brown and Altman by omission; Mumgaard by analogy to his own industry. This is the sentence the G20 signed.
  • Jobs are tasks, not purposes. Huang ("nonsense"), Altman, Brown — all three say the work changes and the purpose stays. None engaged the mixed entry-level evidence.
  • Treat the model as a colleague. Huang's "harness," Brown's "permissions," Altman's "context is your job" — the same operational advice in three vocabularies.

Genuine splits

QuestionPositions
How big is the prize?Musk: 20–30% of world GDP from digital AI, 10x from robots. Huang: "$20, $50 trillion" of benefit to a $100tn industry. Brown: value doubling every six months. Altman: declines to put a number on it and warns against "blind optimism." Mainstream economics: 1–7% over a decade.

None of that is in the statement. The industry's own hedges did not survive into the text the industry's hosts wrote.

Stepping back

Which way the errors lean

Thirteen checkable claims: four true, seven mostly true, one misleading, one unverifiable-as-framed, plus one self-reported figure that cannot be tested. No claim was false. The pattern of the misses is consistent and mild: rounding up (Loudoun $5,800 → $6,000), totals presented as private (fusion $14.2bn total vs $13.3bn private), a scope-blurred statistic (data-centre capacity growth presented as electricity growth), and an "analyst consensus" that traces back to the speaker. Every error leans toward the speaker's argument. That is the same direction as two of this site's three earlier single-author runs and the opposite of the Isaacman run; four runs is still not a base rate.

The venue

This was advocacy, not journalism, and it did not pretend otherwise. The interviewers were the Commerce Secretary and the President's science adviser; they set the questions, several of which were leading ("So are you… fundamentally optimistic?"; "I don't buy the doomerism at all"); there was no press Q&A inside the sessions. That is not a criticism of the speakers — it is the condition under which every quote above was produced, and the reason the hedges matter: Altman's "worry is warranted" and Musk's "my rough estimate" were said against the grain of the room, which makes them more informative than the applause lines said with it.

What could not be substantiated

  • Hassabis at the G20. Whether he restated his pre-release-testing proposal in Chapel Hill — one secondary source, its cited primary does not contain it. Unreviewed, not rejected.
  • Karp's inequality remarks. Attributed to the CNBC live blog by search summaries; the page itself could not be read. The sovereignty line is on video.
  • Non-US delegation dissent. None found in English-language coverage; the EU, Brazil and Germany are recorded as present and as signatories, not as objecting. Absence of evidence in one language is not evidence of absence.
  • Lutnick's opening remarks on copyright and AI training. Reported via Gizmodo paraphrasing Reuters; the Reuters headline attributes the "theoretical harms" line to Huang, so the two may be conflated.
  • Altman's token statistics. He said he did not have the numbers in front of him; nobody else has them either.

What survives

  • The infrastructure argument. Power and permitting are the binding constraint; every independent source agrees, and the cost curve Brown cited is real.
  • The fusion regulatory story. Verifiable, primary-sourced, and a genuine example of the "use the nearest existing framework" principle working.
  • Altman's risk list. Cybersecurity, biosecurity, concentration — volunteered against the grain of the room and consistent with the independent safety literature.
  • The task/job distinction as a frame, if not as a forecast.

What to discount — in both directions

  • Discount the speakers' growth numbers. Musk's 20–30% and 10x have no support outside the stage; Huang's "$20, $50 trillion" is a rhetorical range; Brown's 4x is self-reported.
  • Discount "analyst consensus" and "if done right." The first traces to the speaker; the second is the whole argument.
  • But also discount the bear case's favourite statistics. The MIT "95% of pilots fail" figure applies to custom embedded tools, not general LLM adoption; the METR study is one trial with sixteen developers; "bigger than the railroads" is true in dollars and false as a share of GDP — pick your denominator honestly.
  • Discount the Hassabis-at-G20 story until someone produces the sentence. His July proposal needs no such caveat.
  • Discount the idea of a unified front. The consensus statement is unified; the people on stage were not, and the hedges they offered are the most useful thing they said.
