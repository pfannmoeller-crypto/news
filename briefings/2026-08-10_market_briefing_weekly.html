Markets & Macro · Weekly

A shockingly weak July jobs report flips Wall Street's rate-hike fear into its best week in months, lifting the S&P 500, gold and Bitcoin together — while Palantir, SpaceX and Disney turn a crowded earnings week into a broad beat and AMD proves a "double beat" alone no longer buys AI-chip stocks a rally.

Last week's edition closed with a hawkish Fed still ringing in investors' ears — a 9-3 hold with three dissents pushing for a hike, no forward guidance, and a fragile Iran ceasefire that had already cracked once. This week answered the open question of what would actually move the Fed's hand next: not another Iran headline, but Friday's July jobs report, which showed the US economy losing 23,000 jobs against forecasts for an 80,000 gain. That single data point did more to markets than the Fed's own meeting had the week before, sending the S&P 500 and Stoxx 600 to record closes, gold to a two-month high, and Bitcoin back above $65,000 — even as Iran's Strait of Hormuz negotiations hardened into a restrictive draft plan rather than a deal, and a dense earnings week delivered a genuinely mixed verdict on which AI-adjacent names still get rewarded for merely beating.

US equitieswk of 3–7 Aug

The S&P 500 and Nasdaq post their strongest week in months, closing at records as a weak jobs report erases hike fears from last week's Fed shock

US equities rallied through the week and closed it with a record: the S&P 500 gained 47.68 points (+0.62%) on Friday to 7,757.64, the Nasdaq Composite jumped 342.26 points (+1.30%) to 26,690.62, and the Dow added 151.83 points (+0.28%) to 54,036.93. For the week, the S&P rose roughly 3.58% — its largest weekly percentage gain in several months — while the Nasdaq packed on a striking 5.19% and the Dow gained close to 3%. The catalyst was Friday's July jobs report (see Macro section below): a surprise 23,000-job decline versus forecasts for an 80,000 gain, read by markets as making a Fed rate hike far harder to justify than last week's hawkish dissenters had implied.

Context & background: last week's edition closed on a market rattled by a genuinely hawkish Fed — a 9-3 hold with three regional presidents publicly dissenting for a hike, and a chair declining to offer forward guidance. That backdrop primed markets to treat any further inflation-side surprise as confirmation of the hawks' case. Instead, the surprise came from the labor side, and it pointed the opposite direction, which is why the reaction was so outsized: a single data point effectively overrode a week-old Fed narrative that Bank of America and others had called a "growing credibility problem."

The details: the rally was broad rather than narrow — Nasdaq's 5.19% weekly gain outpaced the S&P's 3.58%, consistent with rate-sensitive growth and AI-adjacent names benefiting most from lower hike odds, while the Dow's more modest ~3% reflects its lighter weighting toward those names. Friday's session itself was comparatively calm (S&P +0.62%) relative to the week's cumulative move, suggesting most of the repricing happened earlier in the week as investors positioned ahead of the jobs number, then confirmed it.

Context: this is the second consecutive week in which a single scheduled US data release — first the FOMC decision, now the jobs report — dominated the tape more than the earnings season running in parallel with genuinely large individual moves (Palantir +12%, AMD −5-8%). That is a market currently trading macro over micro, at least until Wednesday's CPI print gives the Fed's rate path a second data point to confirm or contradict Friday's read.

Watch-Items: whether Wednesday 12 August's CPI report corroborates a labor-market slowdown story or reintroduces the inflation concern that drove last week's hawkish dissents. Whether the record close holds through Applied Materials' and Coherent's earnings on 12–13 August, the next major AI-capex-adjacent data points. And whether the three dissenting Fed regional presidents soften their stance at the 16 September meeting now that the data has moved in the dovish direction.

Palantir's "otherworldly" quarter and SpaceX's first-ever public earnings both beat cleanly, giving the week's AI and space trades a strong opening leg

Palantir opened the week's earnings gauntlet with what Benzinga called an "otherworldly" second quarter: revenue of $1.94 billion beat estimates of $1.80 billion, up 93% year-on-year, with adjusted earnings of 41 cents per share against a 35-cent estimate. AMD delivers a genuine double beat and record revenue — and the stock still falls 5% to 8%, because "good" no longer buys AI-chip names a rally

AMD reported record second-quarter revenue of $11.54 billion, up 50% year-on-year and above the $11.28 billion estimate, with adjusted earnings of $1.66 per share against a $1.61 estimate; data-center revenue more than doubled. Jobs report · FedFri 7 Aug

The US economy loses 23,000 jobs in July against forecasts for an 80,000 gain — the single data point that undid last week's hawkish Fed narrative

The Bureau of Labor Statistics' July employment report showed nonfarm payrolls declining by 23,000, against Bloomberg-surveyed economist forecasts of an 80,000 increase; the unemployment rate ticked down slightly to 4.1%. May and June figures were also revised lower, with combined revisions showing employment 103,000 lower than previously reported. Markets read the miss as making a Fed rate hike far harder to justify than the previous week's three hawkish FOMC dissents had implied, sending stocks, gold and Bitcoin all higher on the day. Wednesday 12 August's Consumer Price Index release is the next scheduled data point on the Fed's path, with the next FOMC decision due 16 September.

Context & background: last week's edition flagged Bank of America's warning that the Fed faced a "growing credibility problem" after Chair Kevin Warsh held rates while declining to explain the reasoning, with three regional presidents publicly dissenting for a hike. This week's jobs report is the first real economic data since that meeting, and it lands directly against the dissenters' implicit case: a labor market this weak, with two months of prior gains revised away, is a difficult backdrop for arguing the Fed should be tightening further.

The details: the scale of the miss is unusual in both directions — an 80,000-job consensus estimate turning into an actual 23,000-job loss is a swing of roughly 103,000 jobs versus expectations, on top of 103,000 in downward revisions to the two prior months, meaning the labor market's true recent trajectory looks meaningfully weaker than anything reported in real time over the past quarter. The unemployment rate's modest decline to 4.1% is consistent with a shrinking labor force as much as with improving employment, a distinction this week's sourcing does not fully resolve.

Context: a market that sold off hard on a hawkish-sounding Fed one week and rallied hard on a weak jobs report the next is a market still fundamentally trading the same question — will the Fed hike, hold, or eventually cut — just with the data now pointing the opposite direction from where positioning sat a week ago. That whiplash is itself informative: it suggests conviction on the Fed path remains genuinely low, and single data points are moving the tape more than they normally would in a market with a settled view.

Watch-Items: whether Wednesday's CPI print confirms a genuine slowdown story or reintroduces the inflation concern that drove the FOMC's hawkish dissents. Whether the three dissenting regional presidents (Hammack, Kashkari, Logan) soften their public stance ahead of the 16 September meeting. And whether August's jobs report, due in early September, extends or reverses July's weakness.

Iran · HormuzThu 6 – Fri 7 Aug

Iran's draft plan to reopen the Strait of Hormuz bans US and Israeli ships outright, swinging oil sharply before the week still ends near a 9% loss

The Oman-mediated talks flagged in last week's edition produced a concrete document this week — and it complicated rather than resolved the standoff. Iranian state media published a draft plan on Thursday under which Iran would ban US and Israeli ships from transiting the Strait of Hormuz altogether, and require other nations "that have harmed Iran" to pay compensation before their vessels could transit. Crude jumped on the news, with Brent rising 3.8% to $82.49 and WTI gaining about 2.8% to $77.29. By Friday, prices eased again — Brent to $81.92, WTI to $76.96 — as investors weighed mounting obstacles to any actual deal, leaving the week tracking toward a roughly 9% loss overall despite Thursday's spike. Iranian semi-official media also reported explosions in the strait Thursday night, described as Iran intercepting "hostile targets." Cross-reference: full Hormuz negotiating detail and the military-incident timeline are covered in this week's geopolitics briefing.

Context & background: last week's edition described oil as having "stopped trying to price a resolution and started pricing a range," a read this week's data broadly confirms — Brent's Thursday-to-Friday round trip ($82.49 to $81.92) is a small move relative to the week's overall roughly 9% decline, meaning the market treated the draft-plan news as a negotiating datapoint rather than a resolution or a rupture. That the week nets out lower despite a genuine escalation headline (a ship-ban draft plan) shows how much of the earlier risk premium has already drained out of the price since July's peaks near $101-102.

The details: the draft plan's substance — banning US and Israeli vessels outright, and requiring compensation from other nations Iran considers to have "harmed" it — is a materially harder opening position than the "progress" on commercial shipping arrangements this briefing reported two weeks ago, and an SEB Research analyst characterized the draft as handing Iran a degree of leverage the Trump administration "could not politically stomach." That gap between what Iran has proposed and what Washington could plausibly accept is likely why oil eased rather than held its Thursday spike: traders appear to be pricing the draft as an opening position, not a final term sheet.

Context: a genuine intercepted-attack report (Thursday night's "hostile targets" language) failing to move oil more than a routine daily fluctuation is itself a data point: markets have become desensitized to the pattern of strikes and interceptions this conflict has produced since July, and are now responding almost exclusively to negotiating-text headlines rather than kinetic events, provided the kinetic events don't escalate beyond the established pattern.

Watch-Items: whether the draft plan is a genuine Iranian opening position subject to negotiation or effectively a rejection dressed as a proposal. Whether the compensation-payment demand becomes a sticking point serious enough to collapse the Oman-mediated track entirely. Whether Thursday night's interception incident is followed by further exchanges. And whether gasoline prices, already elevated over the past month per prior editions, respond to this week's net price decline or lag it.

lightbulbEditor's view

One data point undid a week of Fed hawkishness — and the market's bar for AI-adjacent earnings just got higher

1. A single jobs report outweighed a week of Fed messaging. Last week's hawkish 9-3 hold with three dissents built a narrative of a Fed leaning toward a hike; Friday's 23,000-job loss undid nearly all of that repricing in one session. Conviction on the Fed path remains genuinely low.

2. The market's bar for AI-adjacent earnings has moved from "beat" to "beat by enough." Palantir and SpaceX cleared it and were rewarded; AMD delivered a genuine double beat with more-than-doubled data-center revenue and still fell 5-8%, because its 2026 rally had already priced in more.

3. Everything rallied together, which is itself unusual. Stocks, gold and Bitcoin all rose on the same catalyst this week — a weak jobs report read as dovish for the Fed — rather than the more typical pattern of risk assets and the safe haven moving in opposite directions.

4. Iran's Hormuz negotiations produced a document, not a deal. The draft plan banning US and Israeli ships is a harder opening position than the "progress" flagged two weeks ago, and oil's muted reaction suggests traders are pricing it as a negotiating stance rather than a breakthrough or a breakdown.

5. Europe's rally has gone from a single record close to a genuine trend. A fourth consecutive weekly gain for the Stoxx 600, a first-ever DAX level above 26,100, and Stoxx 600 earnings growth above 22% together describe a market re-rating rather than a one-week bounce.

6. Washington's crypto clock finally moved, if only procedurally. The CLARITY Act's cloture filing is the first concrete step toward an actual floor vote this briefing has reported in weeks — though the 60-vote threshold and the seven-Democrat requirement mean September's outcome is still genuinely uncertain.

The week's whiplash — hawkish Fed shock one week, dovish jobs-report relief the next — leaves markets at records but on thinner conviction than the headline numbers suggest. Wednesday's CPI print is the next test of whether Friday's read holds or reverses, and Applied Materials' and Coherent's 12-13 August earnings will show whether AMD's "beat but not enough" verdict was a one-off or a new pattern for the rest of the chip-earnings calendar.
