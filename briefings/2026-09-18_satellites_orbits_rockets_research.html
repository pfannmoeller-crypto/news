What flies where, and why: satellites, orbits and the rockets that carry them

This is a reference sheet Stefan asked for: which orbits exist, what satellites do in each, what a satellite must be built to do to survive and work there, and which rocket takes it there. It has no argument to make and no author to agree or disagree with — it is a set of figures, each carrying its own source and its own confidence score.

The unit of the piece is the satellite, followed through four questions in order: what should it do → where must it be to do that → what must it be able to do, physically, once it is there → how does it get there. Three launch blocks carry almost all of the traffic — SpaceX, the US government (NASA and national-security launch customers, mainly on SLS and defence missions), and China (CASC's Long March family plus a fast-growing commercial tier) — with Europe (Ariane 6, ESA) and everyone else (Japan, Russia, India, and the new commercial entrants) each getting one line.

Every number below carries a source and a confidence score (C, out of 5). Counts of active satellites and launch tallies are dated, because they move week to week — sometimes day to day.

The map

One paragraph, one small table, and the one fact that explains almost everything else in this piece: going higher is not what costs a rocket most of its fuel — going fast enough to stay up is.

Everything that orbits Earth sits in one of six broad bands. Closest in is low Earth orbit (LEO) — anywhere from about 200 to 2,000 km up — home to the International Space Station, most broadband satellite fleets and almost all Earth-imaging spacecraft. A special, near-polar slice of LEO is sun-synchronous orbit (SSO), tuned so a satellite crosses every point on Earth at the same local solar time — the imaging workhorse orbit. Further out is medium Earth orbit (MEO), roughly 19,000–23,000 km up, where the navigation constellations — GPS, Europe's Galileo, Russia's GLONASS, China's BeiDou — live. At 35,786 km sits geostationary orbit (GEO), the one altitude where a satellite's orbital period exactly matches Earth's rotation, so it appears to hang motionless over one spot — the address for most television and fixed-broadband satellites and for weather satellites that need to stare at one hemisphere continuously. Well above that, a minority of spacecraft use highly elliptical Molniya or Tundra orbits, which dwell for hours over high latitudes GEO cannot usefully cover. And beyond Earth orbit entirely, a handful of science missions sit at the Sun–Earth Lagrange points (L1 and L2, roughly 1.5 million km out) or, for lunar missions, in cislunar space around the Moon.

What it costs to get there is the more useful number, and it is not a story about height. Reaching low Earth orbit from the ground costs a rocket roughly 9.3–10 km/s of delta-v — the change in speed a manoeuvre costs, the standard currency of spaceflight — of which 1.5–2 km/s is lost fighting gravity and air drag on the way up. Moving from a low orbit up to a geostationary transfer orbit (GTO, an elliptical "on-ramp" orbit used to reach geostationary orbit) costs a further 2.3–2.4 km/s; the last step, circularising at GEO itself, is roughly 1.5 km/s more, though sources disagree on the exact figure. Reaching the Moon (trans-lunar injection, TLI — the burn that sends a spacecraft toward the Moon) from LEO costs about 3.2 km/s, and a Mars transfer costs 3.6–4.3 km/s depending on the launch window. Altitude, in other words, is not the hard part; speed is — a satellite in a 200 km orbit is already travelling at roughly 7.8 km/s, and staying up simply means going fast enough, sideways, that the curve of your fall matches the curve of the Earth.

Where does space begin? By international convention, the Kármán line is set at 100 km altitude; the United States has used 50 miles (about 80 km) as its own working definition since 2005, which is why some sub-orbital flights are called "spaceflights" in the US and not everywhere else.

What the altitude does to a satellite

Drag and lifetime. Left uncontrolled, a satellite at 200 km re-enters within days to weeks; at 400 km, within about five years; at 600 km and above, often more than 25 years; at 800 km, centuries. In medium Earth orbit and geostationary orbit, decay is effectively permanent on any human timescale, which is exactly why those two altitudes need dedicated disposal orbits rather than just waiting for the atmosphere to do the work. The International Space Station loses roughly 2 km of altitude a month to residual drag and is reboosted several times a year to compensate.

Eclipse. A satellite in an ISS-type low orbit spends roughly 35 minutes of its roughly 93-minute orbit in Earth's shadow — no sunlight, running on battery. Geostationary satellites see no eclipse at all for most of the year, but around each equinox they pass through roughly 44-day seasons in which the shadow returns for up to about 72 minutes a day. Dawn–dusk sun-synchronous orbits, chosen deliberately, see little or no eclipse at all, which is one reason imaging satellites like this orbit.

Radiation. NASA's total-ionising-dose model (5 mm of aluminium shielding, one year) gives a stark ladder: a low equatorial orbit at 500 km receives 10 rad or less a year; an ISS-type 500 km/51° orbit receives about 430 rad; a polar 500 km orbit, 333 rad; geostationary orbit, about 5,930 rad; and a geostationary transfer orbit — the elliptical path (roughly 500×38,000 km) a satellite spends days crossing on its way up to GEO, passing twice a day through the Van Allen belts (two doughnut-shaped bands of high-energy particles trapped by Earth's magnetic field) — about 59,600 rad. That is ten times the dose a satellite receives once it settles into geostationary orbit itself; the inner Van Allen belt starts around 1,000 km and the outer one runs roughly 13,000–60,000 km, which is exactly where the navigation satellites have to sit.

Station-keeping. Low-orbit constellations run electric thrusters more or less continuously just to hold altitude against drag. A geostationary satellite needs roughly 50 m/s of delta-v a year for north–south correction alone, and is generally required to stay inside a box just 2–3° of longitude wide. Halo orbits around the L1 and L2 Lagrange points are inherently unstable and need continuous small corrections to avoid drifting away.

Rules and debris

Since 29 September 2022, the US Federal Communications Commission (FCC) requires US-licensed low-Earth-orbit satellites to deorbit within five years of the end of their mission, replacing the long-standing 25-year international guideline set by the Inter-Agency Space Debris Coordination Committee (IADC) and the UN — a five-fold tightening, for US operators only. Geostationary satellites, which cannot practically deorbit, are instead raised into a graveyard orbit roughly 200–300 km above the GEO belt at end of life.

ESA's Space Environment Report 2025 counts roughly 40,000 tracked objects in orbit, of which about 11,000 are active payloads; it estimates more than 1.2 million objects larger than 1 cm and over 50,000 larger than 10 cm. Jonathan McDowell's end-2025 tally, independently, counts 32,207 catalogued objects weighing 16,185 tonnes in total.

Starlink's own collision-avoidance manoeuvres, reported every six months in its FCC filings, have been rising fast: more than 25,000 in the December 2022–May 2023 window, climbing to 144,404 in December 2024–May 2025. Later figures of 207,152 and 355,000 have been reported in the press but could not be pinned to a specific reporting window this run, so they are marked at low confidence. On the disposal side, Starlink deorbited 260 satellites (176 first-generation, 84 second-generation) between December 2025 and May 2026, versus roughly 500 in the previous six-month window and 406 across the whole of 2024.

The census — how many satellites are up there

As of 17 September 2026, Jonathan McDowell's planet4589.org census — the field's standard independent tracker — counts 16,810 active satellites. A June 2026 figure of 15,711, citing McDowell-derived data via SpaceDaily, and an end-2025 figure of 14,132 from McDowell's own "Space Activities in 2025" report sit earlier on the same growth curve — these three numbers disagree only because the fleet is growing quickly, not because they disagree about anything. The Union of Concerned Scientists' long-cited satellite database, by contrast, has been frozen at 7,560 (as of May 2023) and should not be treated as current.

Of the 16,810, Starlink alone accounts for 11,114 active satellites — 66% of everything active in orbit — with 10,801 launched and 1,391 re-entered by 25 December 2025. Behind it: OneWeb/Eutelsat at 652 (October 2024); Amazon's Leo/Kuiper constellation at roughly 392–398 launched (July–September 2026); and China's two mega-constellations, Guowang at 186 and Qianfan at 238 launched (17 September 2026). A separate, lower-confidence 2023 aggregator estimate puts military and intelligence satellites at 247 for the US, 157 for China, 110 for Russia and 17 for France — 613 in total.

Part 2 — what satellites do there

One block per job, in the orbit built for it, each with the "why this orbit" logic, the current numbers, and its own check.

Broadband, from low Earth orbit

Why LEO: latency scales with distance. A round trip to a satellite around 500 km up and back takes roughly 25–40 milliseconds; the same round trip via a geostationary satellite 35,786 km away takes about 500–600 milliseconds — the difference between a phone call that feels normal and one with an awkward half-second pause, and the reason video calls, online gaming and remote desktops work acceptably over LEO broadband but were always painful over GEO. The price of that low latency is scale: because each satellite covers only a small, fast-moving patch of ground, you need thousands of them where one geostationary satellite would do.

Starlink runs 11,114 active satellites and reported 12 million subscribers as of June 2026. It has flown laser inter-satellite links since 2020 — three optical terminals per satellite, according to trade reporting, now said to run "up to 200 Gbps per link"; an earlier design figure of 100 Gbps per link is also reported and the two do not obviously refer to the same hardware generation, so both are kept here as a live conflict. A separate "mini" laser terminal built for third-party satellites demonstrated 25 Gbps over 4,000 km after launching in August 2025. Competing constellations: OneWeb/Eutelsat at 652 satellites, Amazon's Leo constellation at roughly 395, and China's Guowang and Qianfan combined at roughly 424.

Television and fixed broadband, from geostationary orbit

Why GEO persists: a geostationary satellite holds still in the sky as seen from the ground, so a single fixed dish — no tracking motor, no hand-over between satellites — can serve millions of homes at once. Viasat's own materials describe its Viasat-3-class satellites as designed for roughly 1 terabit per second of throughput each. Fleet sizes: SES and Intelsat each operate more than 50 satellites, per trade aggregation.

Trade press has been reporting a real decline in new GEO orders — including SES's cancellation of two already-paid Intelsat satellites, reported in 2026 — but this run found no primary annual-orders series to put a number on the trend. That figure is marked UNVERIFIED rather than estimated.

Navigation, from medium Earth orbit

Why ~20,000 km: a position fix needs four or more satellites visible from any point on Earth at once, and roughly a 12-hour orbit repeats the same ground track every day, which simplifies both the constellation design and the receiver's job; the altitude is a balance between signal strength (closer would be stronger) and how much sky each satellite covers (higher covers more).

GPS runs 31 operational satellites (gps.gov, a dated page); Europe's Galileo, 28 operational as of September 2026; China's BeiDou, 35 BeiDou-3 satellites plus 15 older BeiDou-2 satellites, arranged in three layers — 24 medium-orbit, 3 geostationary and 3 inclined geosynchronous; Russia's GLONASS, roughly 24–26. On accuracy: civilian GPS typically resolves to a few metres; Galileo's High Accuracy Service, per ESA, resolves to 20 cm.

Earth observation, from sun-synchronous orbit

Why sun-synchronous: the orbit precesses — its plane rotates — by about one degree a day, exactly matching Earth's motion around the Sun, so the satellite crosses every point on the ground at the same local solar time on every pass (Sentinel-2 crosses at 10:30, descending). Constant lighting makes images directly comparable year over year, and the near-polar inclination gives global coverage.

ESA's Sentinel-2 flies at 786 km with 10/20/60 m resolution bands and a 5-day revisit using two satellites. Maxar's WorldView Legion resolves to 30 cm using six satellites. In radar imaging (SAR — synthetic-aperture radar, which images through cloud and at night by bouncing its own radio signal off the ground), ICEYE runs roughly 14 satellites, Capella roughly 7, and Umbra roughly 2, per a single trade source. China's Jilin-1 constellation runs "100-plus" satellites, alongside the separate Gaofen series.

Weather — why it needs both GEO and polar orbit

A geostationary satellite gives an unblinking view of one hemisphere, which is what storm tracking needs — GOES-19 became "GOES-East" on 7 April 2025 after launching on a Falcon Heavy on 25 June 2024, joining Europe's Meteosat Third Generation, Japan's Himawari-9 and China's Fengyun-4. Polar, sun-synchronous satellites (JPSS-2, Europe's Metop, China's Fengyun-3) instead cover the whole globe roughly twice a day and can sound the atmosphere vertically as they pass. GOES-R's imager carries 16 spectral bands, resolves to 0.5 km in the visible, and produces a full-disk image every 5–15 minutes, alongside a dedicated lightning mapper.

Science and crewed spaceflight

Hubble sits in low Earth orbit (~535 km) because the Space Shuttle had to be able to reach it for servicing. The James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) sits instead at the L2 Lagrange point because a sun shield facing one direction there gives it permanent, uninterrupted cold and dark — essential for an infrared telescope. Europe's Gaia and Euclid missions sit at L2 for the same reason. Chandra, NASA's X-ray observatory, instead uses a high elliptical orbit specifically to climb above the Van Allen belts for long, uninterrupted exposures.

The International Space Station masses roughly 420 tonnes (NASA also quotes "450,000 kg" including visiting vehicles), carries up to seven crew, orbits at roughly 400–420 km, and is scheduled for a planned deorbit at the end of 2030 using a dedicated SpaceX deorbit vehicle under a NASA contract signed in June 2024 worth roughly $843 million. China's Tiangong station masses roughly 100 tonnes for its core module alone — higher figures sometimes quoted include docked visiting craft, which is a definitional difference rather than a factual dispute — carries a crew of 3 (6 during handovers), and orbits at 386–392 km. A private US entrant, Vast's Haven-1 module, masses 14 tonnes and is targeting a 2026 launch on a Falcon 9.

Part 3 — what a satellite must do

Seven systems every satellite needs regardless of mission, then three real satellites laid across the same rows to see how differently those seven systems get built.

The seven systems

1. Power. Triple-junction solar cells run roughly 30% efficient, up to 34% in the best cases. A 3U cubesat (a standardised small-satellite format, roughly the size of three stacked bread loaves) draws 2–50 watts; a GOES-R weather satellite draws more than 4 kilowatts at end of life; large geostationary communications satellites draw 10–20 kilowatts. Lithium-ion batteries carry the load through eclipse.

2. Propulsion. Chemical hydrazine thrusters produce 0.5–4 newtons of thrust at a specific impulse (a measure of fuel efficiency — how much thrust a given amount of propellant produces) of 150–250 seconds. Electric propulsion trades thrust for efficiency: Hall-effect thrusters produce 10–50 millinewtons at 1,000–2,000 seconds; ion thrusters, 1–10 millinewtons at 1,000–3,500 seconds; cold-gas thrusters (simplest, weakest) run only 65–70 seconds. Starlink's own argon Hall thruster is reported at 170 millinewtons, 2,500 seconds, 4.2 kW and 2.1 kg. Raising a satellite electrically from a transfer orbit up to geostationary orbit takes roughly 4–6 months.

3. Attitude control (keeping the satellite pointed correctly) uses star trackers, sun sensors and gyroscopes to sense orientation, and reaction wheels, magnetorquers (which push against Earth's magnetic field) and small thrusters to correct it. A typical communications satellite needs to point to within about 0.1°; Earth-observation satellites need arcsecond-level precision. JWST needs to hold roughly 1 arcsecond of pointing from its main bus, refined to 2 milliarcseconds by a dedicated fine-steering mirror.

4. Thermal control — radiators, heat pipes, multilayer insulation and heaters — manages surfaces that, in low Earth orbit, can swing roughly between −150°C and +120°C as the satellite passes in and out of sunlight.

5. Radiation and reliability — covered in Part 1's dose table (LEO ≤10–430 rad/year, GEO ~5,930 rad/year, transfer orbit ~59,600 rad/year) — drives how "rad-hard" (radiation-hardened) a satellite's electronics need to be, and feeds directly into design life: a LEO broadband satellite is typically designed for roughly five years (Starlink's own figure for this could not be sourced this run), GOES-R for 15 years (10 operational plus 5 in storage as a spare), and GPS III for 15 years.

6. Communications — radio bands L, S, C, X, Ku, Ka and V, plus optical laser links (see Part 2); phased-array antennas; telemetry and command typically in S-band.

7. Payload — the part that actually does the mission's job: transponders or phased arrays for communications; atomic clocks for navigation (GPS III's on-board clock count could not be sourced this run); imaging optics, where aperture size sets resolution, for Earth observation; SAR antennas for radar imaging; imagers and atmospheric sounders for weather (GOES's imager: 16 bands, as above).

End of life ties back to Part 1's rules: the FCC's five-year deorbit rule for US-licensed LEO satellites, a graveyard orbit above GEO, passivation (venting remaining propellant, discharging batteries so nothing can explode later) and, increasingly, "demisable" design so the satellite is engineered to burn up completely on re-entry.

What the table says. Same seven systems, three different answers to "how do you build this." The low-orbit broadband satellite is cheap by satellite standards, short-lived by design, and built in the thousands — mass production, not longevity. The geostationary weather or communications satellite is heavy, engineered for a 15-year design life, radiation-hardened for a much harsher dose, and built one at a time. The navigation satellite is, in a sense, a clock with wings: its entire job is to keep extremely precise time in the outer Van Allen belt and broadcast that time down, which is why it too gets a 15-year design life despite sitting in a much harsher radiation environment than a broadband satellite in LEO.

Part 4 — the ride

Which rocket a satellite gets depends on more than its mass.

The matching rule

A satellite's ride is decided by mass, target orbit, inclination (which depends on the launch site's latitude), schedule, insurance, and law, roughly in that order of how binding each constraint is. Law is the least visible constraint and one of the hardest: US export-control rules (ITAR — the International Traffic in Arms Regulations) mean a Chinese payload effectively cannot fly on a US rocket, and Chinese policy runs the same restriction in the other direction. European institutional payloads fly on Ariane 6 or Vega-C by policy preference; Europe's own Galileo navigation satellites flying on a Falcon 9 in 2024 was a genuine exception, granted under what is reported as a rare waiver.

Stepping back

Where the numbers are soft

  • Chinese prices. No launch price for any Long March variant, or for any Chinese commercial rocket beyond a handful of estimates, has been published; only counts are public, via state media and independent trackers.
  • SpaceX specifications beyond two documents. SpaceX's own capabilities PDF and space-safety page are treated as primary (5/5); nearly everything else about Starlink — subscriber counts, laser-link speeds, per-satellite cost — comes from trade press at 3/5 or below.
  • Textbook figures not re-confirmed. Eclipse minutes, station-keeping delta-v, temperature swings, and general attitude-control pointing requirements are standard aerospace-engineering figures that this run did not trace back to a primary source; they sit at 2–3/5 and are marked as textbook throughout.
  • Counts move weekly. Every satellite count and launch tally in this piece carries a date for a reason — these numbers are not static facts but snapshots of something that changes, in Starlink's case, roughly daily.
  • Wikipedia as cross-check, never as sole source. Wikipedia articles were used throughout to cross-check or fill gaps (typically 3–4/5) but never as the only source behind a load-bearing figure in this piece.

What could not be found

  • Chinese launch prices, for any rocket family
  • Starlink satellite power output, official design life, and per-satellite cost
  • GPS III's on-board atomic-clock count and satellite cost (the commonly quoted "$577 m" figure)
  • An annual series for GEO comsat orders, beyond a trade-press decline narrative and one named cancellation
  • SpaceX's share of total mass launched to orbit (as distinct from its share of launch attempts)
  • India's LVM3 and PSLV launcher specifications and pricing, this run
  • Several general aerospace-engineering figures (eclipse duration, station-keeping delta-v, thermal swings, pointing requirements) that are textbook-standard but were not re-confirmed against a primary source

How to read the counts

Almost every satellite and launch count in this piece traces back to one lineage — Jonathan McDowell's independent tracking at planet4589.org — sampled at different dates. Where three figures for "active satellites" appear at 16,810, 15,711 and 14,132, that is the same count growing over roughly nine months, not three trackers disagreeing about the same moment. Read any pair of counts in this piece with their dates attached, and treat a difference as growth first, disagreement second, unless the note beside it says otherwise.
