LEADASF / PORK6 Jul 2026 · widened window

African swine fever pushes the EU pork benchmark down 36% as Spain's export bans pile up inventory

The EU Pork Marker, S&P Global Platts' benchmark assessment, was priced at €2,277 per metric tonne on 6 July 2026 — down 36.4% from October 2025 levels. The drop follows African swine fever (ASF, a highly contagious viral disease of pigs with no vaccine, fatal to infected animals and triggering trade restrictions wherever it is confirmed) re-emerging in Spain, which logged 33 wild-boar outbreaks in the first quarter of 2026 alone. Key Asian buyers imposed export bans in response, swelling European belly inventories even as EU output keeps contracting on cost and disease pressure.

Context & background. Spain had been ASF-free for decades before new detections emerged late in 2025; the 33 Q1-2026 outbreaks logged there sit against a broader European picture where wild-boar case counts are actually down 16% year-on-year (3,760 vs 4,481), meaning Spain's flare-up is a country-specific shock inside an otherwise easing continental trend — which is part of why the price reaction has been so sharp relative to the aggregate disease count.

The details. Platts' €2,277/MT assessment marks the steepest drawdown this briefing has tracked for any single protein benchmark: a 36.4% fall in nine months. The mechanism is trade policy, not just disease — key Asian import markets suspend purchases from affected regions as a standard biosecurity response, and Spain's belly and processed-pork inventory has nowhere to go domestically or via its usual export lanes, pushing spot prices below production cost even as EU-wide output holds roughly flat.

Why it still matters. Pork is a baseline rendering input for pet food formulations across Europe; a sustained discount below production cost usually shows up first in rendered by-product and meal pricing before it reaches finished-feed contracts. Watch for whether Spain's export bans lift once the outbreak is contained, whether other EU producers see a compensating price lift as Spanish supply gets diverted domestically, and whether this shows up in EU rendered-meal pricing in the next few editions.

RENDERED MEAL2026 range · aggregator data

US commodity poultry meal trades near $550–750 a tonne as EU rendered protein runs roughly double

Commodity-grade poultry meal (48–50% protein, 10–12% ash) is trading at USD 550–750 per metric tonne (MT) FOB Midwest rendering plants in the US in 2026, per market-pricing trackers ChemTrade Asia and Feed Ingredients Asia. EU commodity-grade poultry meal (58–60% protein) trades higher, at EUR 800–1,200/MT, reflecting different protein specifications and feedstock availability. Globally, the commodity-grade rendered meal corridor (poultry, meat-and-bone) runs USD 900–1,400/MT, with the low end reflecting large-volume long-term contracts and the high end spot purchases with minimal certification; hydrolyzed and functional protein variants command a 40–80% premium over standard meal.

Context & background. Poultry-based meals (chicken and turkey) account for an estimated 55–60% of rendered-protein volume consumed in US pet food and 45–50% in the EU, making this the single largest rendered input by tonnage for most formulators — the baseline this briefing's expanded protein coverage below sits alongside, not instead of.

The details. The roughly 45–60% price gap between US and EU commodity meal reflects both a protein-content difference (48–50% US spec vs 58–60% EU spec) and tighter EU feedstock availability; buyers on long-term contracts are seeing the low end of each range, while spot buyers without full certification are paying toward the top. The 40–80% premium for hydrolyzed and functional proteins — used where digestibility or palatability specifications are tighter — is a wide enough band that sourcing terms matter as much as the headline index.

Why it still matters. These figures come from pricing-index aggregator sites rather than a named rendering company or trade body, so treat the ranges as directional rather than a quotable spot price; the next public data point worth checking is whether either tracker publishes a narrower monthly index, or whether a named renderer (Darling Ingredients, SARIA) discloses comparable figures.

METAL / TARIFFS4–11 Aug 2026

US aluminum premium hits a record $2,182 a tonne as Section 232 tariffs bite

The US Midwest Premium — the regional surcharge US buyers pay over the London Metal Exchange (LME) benchmark price for physical delivery — surged to a record $2,182 per tonne, pushing all-in US aluminum prices above $5,340/tonne. Section 232 tariffs (a US national-security trade law provision) impose a 50% import duty on aluminum, layered with a separate 10% "global tariff," and have effectively decoupled the domestic aluminum market from world prices. The Can Manufacturers Institute's president said the industry expects "greater price increases in 2026 than we saw last year."

Context & background. A US Supreme Court ruling on 20 February struck down a separate set of broad tariff powers under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act, but explicitly left the Section 232 metal tariffs untouched — meaning the ruling that looked like it might ease packaging-input costs did not reach the aluminum supply chain at all.

The details. The Midwest Premium had already broken $1/lb in late January before reaching this record; university research (University of Cincinnati) found canned foods among the sharpest year-end-2025 grocery price risers, a direct read-through of exactly this cost pressure reaching finished products. Wet pet food in metal cans and trays sits in the same aluminum-cost exposure as human canned food.

Why it still matters. With Section 232 duties untouched by the Supreme Court ruling, the near-term price path depends on whether the Iran/Strait-of-Hormuz tensions cited alongside the tariff story (see the Energy section below) ease, and whether any packaging buyer discloses aluminum-driven cost pass-through in pet food specifically — nothing public on that yet.

GAS / TTF11 Aug 2026

European gas holds near €58/MWh, up 80% year-on-year, as traders price in a tight winter refill

TTF (Title Transfer Facility, the Dutch gas-trading hub whose front-month price is Europe's standard wholesale gas benchmark) settled at €58.44/MWh on 11 August 2026 — down 3.9% on the day, but still up 13.6% over the past month and roughly 80% above year-ago levels. The front-month contract had already jumped 30% since end-June to €59.44/MWh by 31 July. Commerzbank raised its year-end forecast to €50/MWh from €45/MWh, while Uniper expects prices to hold in a €50–60/MWh band for as long as the Strait of Hormuz remains closed to shipping.

Analysis

Four reads from a cycle where two commodities moved sharply in opposite directions

  1. Aluminum and pork are the cleanest opposite-direction story this briefing has carried. One is a policy-driven cost spike (Section 232 tariffs decoupling US aluminum from world prices), the other a disease-driven price collapse (African swine fever pushing Spanish and EU pork down 36%) — both genuine, both public, and both landing in the same week for entirely unrelated reasons. That is a reminder that "input cost pressure" in pet food is rarely a single direction across all clusters at once.
  2. The restructure to three clusters plus an industry side genuinely surfaced more than the old four-section format found in the prior two editions. Where 10 August reported two of four sections as empty, this edition's three input clusters and industry side all found real material — though the fishmeal card is honest about being four months stale rather than fresh, and the M&A card is explicitly a trend read rather than a new transaction.
  3. Packaging's two stories share a mechanism worth watching together. Aluminum tariffs and a 32% resin-cost rise are both landing on packaging buyers in the same year that American Packaging Corp is committing $30 million to new pouch capacity — a bet that volume growth outruns input costs, not evidence that it has yet.
  4. Retail moves being the one genuine gap, on top of a widened window for two other sections, is worth naming as a pattern rather than smoothing over. Three editions running now, brand and retail-level consolidation stories have clustered in bursts (three items on 10 August, zero this cycle) rather than arriving steadily — this beat's news flow looks lumpy by nature, not by search effort.
