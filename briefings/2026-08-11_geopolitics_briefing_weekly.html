Deep strike10 Aug

A Ukrainian drone strike on a refinery deep inside Tatarstan kills 13 — among the furthest such strikes of the war

Ukrainian drones struck the Nizhnekamsk refinery complex in Russia's Tatarstan republic on 10 August, killing 13 people including a child and wounding 75, according to NBC News reporting on the Ukrainian military's own account of the strike. The target sits roughly 1,000–1,300 kilometres from the Ukrainian border, making it one of the furthest confirmed penetrations of Kyiv's deep-strike campaign against Russian energy infrastructure. The same 48 hours also saw Ukrainian strikes on the Ilsky and Syzran refineries closer to the front, with Ilsky debris sparking a fire that injured five people on 8 August.

Context & background. This briefing has tracked Ukraine's energy-infrastructure campaign for weeks — the Wildberries warehouse strikes and the 3 August refinery hits closer to the border — but Nizhnekamsk is a step change in reach rather than intensity. Tatarstan sits deep in Russia's interior, well beyond the belt of border and near-border regions (Krasnodar, Belgorod, Samara) that have absorbed most prior strikes this briefing has logged.

The details. Ukraine's general staff described the strike as targeting an oil refinery directly rather than reporting stray debris damage, distinguishing it from the Arkhipo-Osipovka casualties recorded in the 3 August edition, where deaths came from intercepted-drone fallout. The Ilsky and Syzran strikes the same week show the campaign running on two tracks simultaneously — sustained pressure on refineries within a few hundred kilometres of Ukraine, plus occasional strikes at extreme range designed to demonstrate that no facility is fully out of reach. NBC News's figure of 13 dead and 75 wounded at Nizhnekamsk had not, at the time of this research, been independently corroborated by a second Western outlet beyond the Ukrainian military's own account relayed through NBC.

Why it still matters. A strike this deep inside Russia signals either an improved long-range drone capability, a degraded Russian air-defence picture in the interior, or both — any of which would be a meaningful shift in the war's technical balance rather than just another data point in an ongoing campaign. What would change this reading: independent confirmation of the casualty figures, Russian acknowledgment of the strike's depth, or evidence the flight path routed through a third country rather than Ukrainian territory directly.

Air war11 Aug

Russian missiles and guided bombs kill six in Zaporizhzhia and hit Kyiv near a children's hospital

Russian forces struck Zaporizhzhia with ballistic missiles and guided aerial bombs overnight into 11 August, killing six people and wounding at least 20 (regional governor Ivan Fedorov put the initial toll at five, since revised upward), damaging four apartment buildings and several non-residential structures. Kyiv was hit the same night: a missile crashed near a children's hospital in the Shevchenkivskyi district without casualties, and a separate strike sparked a warehouse fire that injured one person. Russia's defence ministry said the overnight strikes targeted "defence industry facilities and transport and logistics centres" in both cities.

Zelensky says Russia is "preparing for escalation, not peace," citing North Korean equipment and rising missile output

President Volodymyr Zelensky said on 11 August that Russia is gearing up for further escalation rather than a negotiated peace, pointing to the transfer of North Korean equipment, accelerating ballistic-missile production and what he described as Russian plans for a further mobilisation wave. Ukrainian military intelligence separately reported that Russia has already met its annual production targets for Zircon and Oniks anti-ship/cruise missiles seven months into the year, with 33 Zircons and 60 Oniks built through July.

Iran / Hormuz — coordinates agreed, a joint statement drafted, but the strait still isn't reopening

2 items
Diplomacy5–9 Aug

Iran and Oman agree on the actual coordinates of a Hormuz shipping corridor — a joint statement is now in final drafting

Iran and Oman have moved from negotiating a framework to agreeing the concrete geographic coordinates of a proposed safe shipping route through the Strait of Hormuz, Iranian Foreign Ministry spokesman Esmaeil Baghaei confirmed, with a joint statement covering technical, legal, security and environmental points now under review in final drafting. Parts of the new inbound and outbound corridors would run through Iranian waters — a departure from the decades-old system in which commercial traffic used Omani waters — alongside a proposed joint coordination centre to manage traffic and collect vessel information. Iranian Deputy Foreign Minister Kazem Gharibabadi cautioned the framework, even once finalised, "would not automatically reopen the waterway," since Tehran continues to tie full normalisation to US concessions.

Context & background. Last week's edition described the emerging deal at the level of a fee dispute — Iran wanting 5–7% on cargo value, Oman countering with roughly 3% — with a Eurasia Group analyst predicting Washington would accept Iranian control "without explicitly acknowledging" it. This week's coordinates agreement is the concrete technical step that turns that prediction into an actual document moving toward signature.

The details. The routing detail matters more than it sounds: corridors running through Iranian rather than exclusively Omani waters is precisely the "de facto Iranian control" outcome flagged last week, now expressed as a specific, mappable fact rather than a described possibility. CNN's analysis frames the emerging arrangement as one Washington did not design and does not fully want, since a US official has separately insisted any temporary routes carry "no approvals or permissions and no tolls or charges" — a position increasingly at odds with an agreement Washington is not even a party to negotiating.

Why it still matters. Gharibabadi's caveat that the framework "would not automatically reopen the waterway" is the load-bearing sentence in this story: a technically complete shipping-corridor agreement can exist for weeks without producing a single additional tanker transit if Iran's broader conditions — compensation, an end to the naval blockade, releases of frozen assets — remain unmet. What would change this reading: publication of the actual joint statement, any US comment reacting to the Iranian-waters routing, or a measurable rise in tanker transit volumes.

House Republicans and Democrats file a companion Graham Act bill even while the chamber is still on recess

Republican Rep. Michael McCaul (Texas) and Democratic Rep. Steny Hoyer (Maryland) introduced the House companion to the Lindsey O. Graham Sanctioning Russia and Iran Act of 2026 on Monday, 10 August, alongside 16 additional bipartisan co-sponsors — even though the House remains in its August recess and does not return to session until September. The bill mirrors the Senate-passed text: tariffs of up to 100% on the top five buyers or facilitators of Russian oil and gas, sanctions on Putin's inner circle and Russian financial institutions, and an extension of Iran sanctions through 2031. Politico-style reporting on the bill notes some House members remain wary that new tariff-authority delegation to Trump could raise consumer costs and expose Republicans to political blowback.

Follow-up10 Aug

Bulgaria summons Ukraine's ambassador; both sides agree to keep coordinating on the drone-explosion inquiry

Two days after the 8 August drone explosion near a Trans-Balkan gas-pipeline compressor station that this briefing traced to a likely Ukrainian "Maya" decoy drone, Bulgaria summoned Ukraine's ambassador, Olesia Ilashchuk, who met Bulgarian Foreign Minister Velislava Petrova-Chamova on 10 August. Both sides agreed to maintain ongoing coordination to determine the incident's causes; Kyiv has reiterated it did not intentionally direct any asset toward Bulgaria and has offered investigative cooperation.

Context & background. The summons and follow-up meeting are the diplomatic mechanics that naturally follow such an incident between partners, rather than a sign of a deeper rupture.

Russia internalupdated 4–11 Aug

The Duma election countdown wakes up: a formal three-day vote, a Kremlin pressure campaign on Yabloko, and an anti-war party registering nationwide

After weeks of silence, Russia's State Duma election campaign produced real developments: the vote is formally confirmed as a three-day poll from 18 to 20 September, electing all 450 deputies alongside eight regional gubernatorial races and 39 regional parliaments. The Kremlin is intensifying pressure on the systemic opposition, primarily the anti-war-leaning Yabloko party and the Communist Party (KPRF), while the Dawn (Rassvet) party led by former presidential candidate Ekaterina Duntsova says it will contest every single-member district on a platform of "peace, progress, and freedom." Central Election Commission chair Ella Pamfilova separately warned that internet disruptions could occur during the vote, telling citizens "not to complain" and attributing any restrictions to security concerns.

lightbulbEditor's view

The quietest arcs made the loudest news this week — and Hormuz just got a lot more concrete

1. Iran and Oman agreeing actual coordinates, not just a fee framework, is the week's most consequential single fact. A shipping corridor running through Iranian rather than exclusively Omani waters is the "de facto Iranian control" this briefing predicted last week, now expressed as a mappable, near-signable document — even though Iran's own deputy foreign minister says it won't automatically reopen the strait.

2. Ukraine's Nizhnekamsk strike is a range statement as much as a casualty event. A refinery roughly 1,000–1,300 kilometres inside Russia being hit successfully says something about Ukraine's evolving deep-strike reach that a single-outlet, not-yet-independently-corroborated casualty count (13 dead, 75 wounded) can't fully capture on its own.

3. Two arcs this briefing had marked dormant — the Duma race and the House sanctions push — produced genuine, dated news in the same window. Neither development changes the underlying calendar (the vote is still six weeks out, the House still doesn't return until September), but both show that "quiet" arcs can restart abruptly rather than staying quiet indefinitely.

4. Zelensky's "escalation, not peace" framing and the still-unconfirmed Witkoff/Kushner timeline sit in tension. If Kyiv's own intelligence read is accurate — accelerating missile production, North Korean equipment, mobilisation planning — it undercuts the premise that a Kyiv-then-Moscow envoy visit, whenever it actually happens, is heading toward a near-term ceasefire.

5. Pickaxe Mountain's continued silence is worth taking at face value rather than reading into. An undated uranium-extraction report and month-old intelligence characterisations of Mojtaba Khamenei's nuclear leanings both circulate widely online without a fresh news hook — exactly the kind of stale material this briefing's honesty rule exists to keep out of the "new this week" column.

Wildcard to watch into next week: whether the Iran-Oman joint statement is actually published and signed, and whether the Witkoff/Kushner "7–10 days" window produces an actual visit rather than a fourth revised timeline.
