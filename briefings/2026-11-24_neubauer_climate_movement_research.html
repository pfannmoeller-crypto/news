Key takeaways

  • The “biggest mistake” is narrower than the title. Neubauer does not accept Ronzheimer’s thesis, as he put it, that the movement’s moralism drove polarisation; many actors were involved, she says. The mistake she admits, speaking for herself, is having been “naive” in trusting conservative pledges such as the CDU’s party-conference commitment to the Paris goals.
  • Her core argument is economic, not moral. Germany’s fossil import bill, heat damage and dependence on Putin, Trump and the Strait of Hormuz make climate policy a matter of cost and sovereignty. Her headline figures largely hold: the import bill was €72bn in 2025 (KfW), and the June 2026 heatwave cost about €6.3bn (Prognos).
  • Three figures need qualification. Germany emits about 1.5% of global fossil CO2 rather than her 2%; the “almost €1bn per heat day” applies only to days above 35°C (€431m for a day above 30°C); the solar job losses are real, but she leaves out that cheap Chinese modules drove them too.
  • On COP31 she blames both sides. Oil states led by Saudi Arabia and Russia blocked, but Germany and the EU, she says, did not deliver what they should have and were not prepared; Merz stayed away. The checks confirm the blockade and his absence; whether Europe could have changed the outcome is a judgment.
  • On Greta Thunberg she defers a verdict. She says they are no longer close, calls the split a division of labour, declines to judge Thunberg’s flotilla companion Thiago Ávila, who praised Hezbollah’s leader, until she has looked into it, and then says keeping away from terrorists and their friends is “self-evident”.
Part I

The conversation

What Neubauer says, in the order of the interview, with the host’s challenges and a check box wherever a factual claim carries the argument. Section titles are ours.

Who is speaking

Luisa Neubauer has been the best-known face of Fridays for Future Germany since 2019. She is a member of the Greens and describes herself as an author by profession and an activist for most of her working time: talks at schools and universities, visits to companies such as the steelmaker Salzgitter, work with unions and works councils. She had just returned from COP31 in Antalya.

Paul Ronzheimer is a senior journalist at Bild (Axel Springer) and hosts the podcast. He takes the sceptic’s role: the movement’s tone, people’s job fears, the cost of climate policy, and Greta Thunberg’s company. The two know each other from panel debates and use the informal “du”.

The interview in one paragraph

Ronzheimer’s question is whether climate protection still has a place in a Germany worried about jobs, the economy and war, and whether the movement itself helped bring about the backlash. Neubauer’s answer: the movement proved that activism works; Germany’s economic trouble comes from wrong industrial decisions, not from climate rules; climate protection is the cheapest way to energy security; and on climate Germany makes itself small, as at COP31. The last third turns to her political ambitions and to Greta Thunberg.

1

Activist, author, and the AfD question 1:19

Ronzheimer notes that she now speaks on democracy, the AfD and migration, not only climate. Neubauer says reach brings a responsibility to take positions, though nobody is obliged to; and some topics are tied directly to climate. A party that questions the free democratic order also threatens what climate protection depends on: that laws and court rulings are respected.

Does that scare people away from climate protest? She calls it “a very good point” and a big strategic question. Fridays for Future wants protest with low barriers, where nobody has to tick three other boxes first. But protest without a position is pointless:

“It would be nonsense to make a protest that wants to please everyone. Then we have a flea market.” — Neubauer (our translation)

On the AfD she separates the party from its voters. The party, which she says denies climate science “most offensively”, is not welcome at climate demos. AfD voters who come out of curiosity are, in her words, almost to be encouraged: if the aim is to win AfD voters back, one has to count on some who once voted for it changing their minds. Someone arriving with an AfD flag would “rightly” be told that the demo stands for the opposite.

2

What the movement achieved 8:37

Asked for the biggest concrete success of 2019–21, she first names an indirect one: proof that activism works, that an unpopular topic like climate can win elections and that all democratic parties can be pushed to compete on it. She recalls sitting next to Friedrich Merz on Markus Lanz’s talk show while he explained how markets would protect the climate. In hindsight, she says, one can call that hot air, or see it as what public pressure achieved.

She adds that these were the years when the AfD’s approval was historically low and it had little to say. Then the concrete list: in 2016 a coal exit was unthinkable; now Germany is exiting coal, the Constitutional Court has ruled on climate protection, renewables are spreading worldwide, and electric cars, once mocked (she cites a newspaper editor who said they had “no soul”), are on the roads.

Check 1 · AfD “historically low” in 2019–21 · C2/5Partly holds. The AfD fell from 12.6% (2017) to 10.3% in the 2021 federal election, and polled roughly 9–15% in those years. That is low compared with today, but may not be a historic low: by our recollection it polled similar or lower values in 2013–15. A full poll series was not retrieved.
Check 2 · Constitutional Court · C3/5Holds as a loose paraphrase. On 24 March 2021 (1 BvR 2656/18) the court found the Climate Protection Act partly unconstitutional because it pushed the burden of cuts onto the years after 2030 and so restricted future freedoms. Her wording, a right “to grow old on a healthy planet”, is not in the ruling. The coal exit is law, with 2038 as the latest end date.

Her hedge: Merz has since proved “ecologically quite flexible”, and the success “can also happen again at any time”, which she offers as the lasting lesson.

3

The mistake she admits 13:09

Ronzheimer puts his thesis, saying he is exaggerating on purpose: with its dogma, “we are the good ones”, did the movement scare people off and share responsibility for the radicalisation of the social climate, on both sides? Neubauer answers first with irony, thanking him for ascribing so much power to the climate movement, then says many actors were involved in the economic mistakes and the shift in society. Protest, she adds, is pointless if it never dares to be uncomfortable.

She describes the 2019 mood as a Germany rolling up its sleeves: solar, heat pumps and wind turbines “made in Germany”, the renewable energy law (EEG) as an export hit, and an early switch to electric cars to keep the export lead. Conservative institutions joined because, Ronzheimer suggests, they wanted to belong. She answers that motives matter less than what happens next.

When he presses whether the movement failed to bring the whole of society along, she names the mistake, speaking for herself:

“We were, or I was, I would say naive in the good sense, but in hindsight I thought there was more to rely on, for example a CDU party-conference resolution that commits to the Paris climate goal.” — Neubauer, speaking only for herself (our translation)

Her reason for staying trusting: she does not want to become cynical. If a conservative club commits to climate action, she wants to be the person who says “awesome, I’m in”, because her job is to invite people to change their perspective, and that requires letting them move.

Note: this is a mistake of trust in partners, not the mistake of tone Ronzheimer asked about. She does not accept the thesis as put; she does not deny that the movement scared some people off either.

4

Germany’s missed chance 18:47

To the question whether job fears outweigh climate concern, she answers with industrial policy. Germany had a huge chance to profit from a global transition and “blew the first opportunity”: the car industry chose to deceive the world with the diesel scandal instead of building the best, most affordable electric cars. Economists had warned for decades; now electric cars come from China, heavily subsidised by the Chinese state.

It had happened before with solar, she says: more than 100,000 jobs in renewables were lost because a government, “also under Merkel”, massively blocked the energy transition. Now there is a new chance, and she names Salzgitter’s plan for green steel. Ronzheimer points to Hans-Werner Sinn, a recent guest, who sees the combustion engine very differently.

Check 3 · 100,000 renewables jobs · C3/5Number holds, cause is one-sided. She spoke of renewables; the losses were concentrated in solar. Solar employment fell from about 156,700 (2011) to about 44,300 (2016), some 112,000 jobs. The CDU/FDP government cut feed-in tariffs sharply in 2010 and 2012, but cheap Chinese modules and insolvencies (Q-Cells, Solon, Sovello) drove the collapse at the same time. “Massively blocked” is her political framing.
Check 4 · Salzgitter green steel · C4/5Holds. Salzgitter’s SALCOS project continues; the expansion stage was delayed by about three years in September 2025, and the federal economics ministry raised its funding by about €322m in February 2026.
5

COP31: “collective failure” 21:41

Ronzheimer summarises: Germany and the EU rejected the final declaration because it did not explicitly mention moving away from coal, oil and gas; on Instagram she called it a collective failure. Whom does she blame?

She starts with what a climate conference looks like from inside: some 190 states agreeing this is the biggest crisis humanity has faced, and delegates from Nepal who had just survived another deadly flood, made worse, she says, because US aid had been cut and monitoring systems in the mountains were gone. Then the blame: the oil states, “above all Saudi Arabia, but also Russia”, tried to undermine the process and reverse the decision taken in Dubai three years earlier to transition away from coal, oil and gas. But Germany and the EU did not deliver either:

“It would be too short to say it was only the evil oil states or Russia. One has to acknowledge that Germany and the EU did not deliver what they should have delivered.” — Neubauer (our translation)

Walking out was right, she says, but “an emergency solution to the emergency solution”.

Check 5 · COP31 outcome · C4/5Holds, and the outcome was worse than “no roadmap”. COP31 (Antalya, 9–20 Nov) ran into 22 Nov and ended without any adopted final text: President Murat Kurum declared there was no consensus on the “Antalya Pact”. The hosts had dropped the phrase “transitioning away from fossil fuels” after objections from Saudi-led oil exporters and Russia. The EU, speaking through Irish minister Darragh O’Brien, refused a text that did not mention fossil fuels; Marshall Islands, Panama, Norway, Vanuatu and Belize also rejected it. Germany’s environment minister Carsten Schneider called the draft “not acceptable for Germany” and blamed countries that “make money with oil and gas”. COP28’s 2023 decision did call for “transitioning away from fossil fuels in energy systems”.

Ronzheimer reads a quote attributed to Ralf Fücks, a Green party member: stop blaming the fossil lobby; even in Germany, after 25 years of Energiewende and hundreds of billions in subsidies, oil, gas and coal are still 75% of energy use; global CO2 emissions hit a record in 2025; sun and wind will not be the last word. Does the world not need fossil energy for many people to live better?

She agrees that electrification is the goal: hundreds of millions in sub-Saharan Africa have no grid connection. But those regions are often where soils are drying out and where adaptation is impossible; parts of Pakistan already see people leave villages in summer heat, and Europe had about 50,000 heat deaths. Offering poor countries “one more round” of fossil energy is a double trap: oil states and fossil companies come with gas-field deals that deepen debt. Germany should make the counter-offer: solar power for sub-Saharan Africa on equal terms, without new debt spirals.

Check 6 · The Fücks figures · C3/5Mixed; quote not found. We could not find the quote’s original. Fossil fuels were about 69.5% of Germany’s primary energy use in 2025 (AG Energiebilanzen). The 75% fits only a different base, such as gross final energy, where renewables supply roughly a fifth to a quarter (our inference). Record global emissions hold: fossil CO2 reached about 38.1bn tonnes in 2025, up 1.1% (Global Carbon Budget).
Check 7 · Nepal flood and US aid · C3/5Event holds, causation is argued, not shown. On 26 Aug 2026 an ice-rock avalanche in Tibet sent a flood down the Rasuwa/Trishuli valley; the WMO reported 359 bodies recovered the next day, and later tolls run higher. River gauges were washed away, and USAID’s SERVIR early-warning programme with NASA ended in January 2025. Some aid experts blame the cuts; fact-checkers could not establish that they caused the deaths.
Check 8 · 50,000 heat deaths · C4/5Holds for urban Europe. A study led by Imperial College and LSHTM estimates more than 50,000 heat-related deaths in summer 2026 across 854 cities in 30 countries, about two thirds attributable to climate change.
6

Why climate, when people fear for their jobs 32:00

Ronzheimer’s central question: many people worry about their jobs, the economy or a war in Europe. Why should climate be at the top of the list for them? Her first answer is that it need not be:

“My activism does not aim at everyone worrying about the climate crisis every day. It aims at people being able to rely on the government not switching course and opening the fossil age while they look away for a second.” — Neubauer (our translation)

Climate targets are also a constitutional matter, she adds; it is a drama that protest is needed for such basic government action. He presses on money: with a limited budget for defence and the economy, why spend on climate? Because Germany cannot afford not to: a single heat day this summer cost the economy almost a billion euros, the longest heatwave over €6bn. The question has to start with what doing nothing costs.

Check 9 · Heat costs · C5/5Holds, with a qualifier. Prognos estimates the heatwave of 17–30 June 2026 cost Germany about €6.3bn, mostly lost productivity, and calls this a lower bound. A day above 35°C costs about €984m, so “almost a billion” is right for very hot days; a day above 30°C costs about €431m.

Ronzheimer’s next objection is the free-rider problem: even if Germany does everything, the heat comes anyway if other countries do not take part. Neubauer gives two lines of reasoning. First, Germany pays, “say, between 70 and 130 billion euros a year”, for oil and gas imports that it then burns, a business model she calls hara-kiri, all the more after the Strait of Hormuz crisis. Even someone who did not care about climate at all should want a home-grown energy supply, because nothing is cheaper or more sovereign than renewables, given Putin and Trump.

Check 10 · Fossil import bill · C3/5Mostly holds. KfW puts Germany’s net import bill for oil, gas and hard coal at €72bn in 2025 (average €78bn since 2008), more than double the 2019 level in 2022, and €83–96bn in its 2026 scenarios. The €130bn upper end fits the 2022 peak but was not confirmed. The Hormuz reference is real: after the US–Israeli war on Iran began around 1 March 2026, Iran disrupted shipping through the strait and Brent rose from about $70 to near $120.

Second, shares: Germany contributes about 2% of global emissions, the whole African continent about 4%. China is moving to renewables; Lula just won “in a certain way a climate election” in Brazil; US Democrats won with explicit renewables advertising. When Ronzheimer calls that a bold claim, she concedes that neither won only because of climate, but both campaigned against fossil business models. What she calls one of the most remarkable points after all her years in the debate: Germany asserts itself on Ukraine, in Asia and at every summit as a country of 80 million that matters, but on climate “we make ourselves small”.

Check 11 · Emission shares · C3/5Germany rounded up; Africa holds. Germany emitted about 1.5% of global fossil CO2 in 2025 (Our World in Data; Global Carbon Budget). Africa’s share is just under 4%.
Check 12 · Elections and the UK speech · C3/5Facts hold; “climate election” is her reading. Lula won a fourth term in the run-off on 25 Oct 2026. In the US midterms of 3 Nov 2026 Democrats won the House on energy-cost and affordability messaging. Her side remark that the UK prime minister called climate a security issue at the COP holds: Andy Burnham said on 12 Nov 2026 that “climate is a national security issue” (C5).
Check 13 · The chancellor at COP31 · C4/5Holds. Both refer to Merz staying away. He did not travel to COP31; a government spokesperson cited a very full calendar. He had attended the leaders’ summit at COP30 in Belém in November 2025.
7

Jobs or climate? 40:05

Ronzheimer argues that the chancellor’s absence mirrors society: people say the last ten years went too far, and industry suffers from high energy prices and investment costs. Then a direct question: if she had the power, would she weaken climate rules if that secured important industrial jobs?

She does not answer yes or no. She would want to promise workers jobs with a long-term future, not jobs built on “technologies of the day before yesterday that are failing on the world market”. Blaming climate rules for the job crisis is, in her view, an analysis that does not hold, because people worldwide are buying electric cars anyway. The real choice, she argues, is not between climate rules and no problems: oil prices rise because of dependence on “fossil warmongers”, not because of climate policy, and climate damage such as a Rhine without water in summer hurts industry regardless.

Her concession: she finds it a disaster that so many people in a rich country must worry about their jobs, and says the movement works closely with unions and works councils.

8

Will she go into politics? 44:37

Ronzheimer hears anger at current politics and asks why she has not gone into politics herself. Her answer is “unfinished”: so far activism outside parties has been the most effective lever, but that has been “shaken” because many good people are no longer in positions of power. She keeps the option open without planning anything, says she did not consider running at the last federal election although she was asked, and would let Ronzheimer know first. Party membership, she says, is “not a marriage but a rough agreement”.

On Berlin, where the Left party won the September 2026 election and is negotiating with the Greens and SPD amid criticism over antisemitism, she says: if the Greens’ and SPD’s conditions are negotiated well, “let’s go”.

Check 14 · Berlin · C3/5Holds as of the last report. In the Berlin election of 20 Sep 2026 the Left party won 25.7%, ahead of the CDU (18.8%), AfD (16.3%), Greens (14.3%) and SPD (12.1%). The three agreed on exploratory talks; antisemitism allegations against individual Left party members overshadow them. The status after early November was not verified.
9

Greta Thunberg 51:16

A documentary clip shows Thunberg laughing on hearing that Fridays for Future Germany still demonstrates; she also says she wanted to abolish capitalism “from day one”. Neubauer describes a division of labour: voices like Thunberg’s name injustice at every level; German Fridays for Future works closer to concrete solutions, alliances and negotiation. The two are “not as close as we once were”. Every kind of protest is needed, she says.

Does she want to abolish capitalism? She wants economic logics that do not destroy children’s future and the basis of life, and an end to the “fossil capitalism” and “exploitation logic” that, in a coming super El Niño year, puts millions at risk. Pressed on whether she wants to abolish the market economy, she says the most urgent problems come first, points to companies that already work sustainably, and rejects the idea that the state should take economic decisions.

Ronzheimer then names Thiago Ávila, who sailed with Thunberg towards Gaza in 2025 after attending the funeral of Hezbollah leader Hassan Nasrallah and calling him inspiring. Neubauer first declines to discuss individual people and suggests he invite Thunberg. She says Thunberg has campaigned for Palestinians at a high personal price, that criticism of her company is justified, that “of course one must keep away from terrorists or the friends of terrorists”, and that she will look at the case and comment afterwards. She then widens the frame: tens of thousands killed in Gaza, a German media debate that cared too little about Palestinian lives, the UN criticising Germany for restricting pro-Palestinian protest. She says the climate movement, herself included, spoke too late and too unclearly on injustice in Palestine, and that German solidarity born of its history with Jews must not be selective.

She adds that she does not want to relativise or play anything down, and that years ago she stressed how important it is to speak about 7 October and to stand up for the protection of Jewish life. Ronzheimer insists that empathy for Gaza must come with clarity about 7 October, which, he says, Thunberg has not shown. Neubauer agrees that it matters how one stands up for a cause, and that one should keep away from people who do not get it right.

Check 15 · Ávila and the documentary · C3/5Holds. Ávila was aboard the Madleen with Thunberg in June 2025; he attended Nasrallah’s funeral in February 2025 and called him inspiring (reported by the Jewish Chronicle and others; he posted about the funeral himself). The clip comes from Hubertus Koch’s documentary on the criminalisation of activism; fact-checkers say Thunberg was reacting to Fridays for Future Germany having distanced itself from her. The “capitalism from day one” line was not verified.
Check 16 · The UN references · C4/5Holds, with precision. UN human-rights experts urged Germany on 16 Oct 2025 to end its crackdown on pro-Palestinian protests. The finding of genocide came on 16 Sep 2025 from the UN-mandated Independent International Commission of Inquiry, not from the Security Council or a court; the ICJ case brought by South Africa is still pending.

A · “Climate policy is economic and security policy” primary texts and reports · C3–4

Ottmar Edenhofer (Potsdam Institute), Claudia Kemfert (DIW Berlin), Bertelsmann Sustainability Barometer

Edenhofer wrote in July 2026 that Germany’s economic problems “have nothing to do with climate protection” and lie in low productivity, weak innovation and scarce venture capital; framing it as climate or prosperity helps populists. Kemfert argues that holding on to fossil energy, not the Energiewende, is what is expensive. In the 2026 Bertelsmann survey 59% say climate investment must happen now to lower long-term costs.

Difference from Neubauer: little on substance. Edenhofer, unlike the movement, prefers carbon pricing and the EU’s carbon border levy to bans and fixed dates, and says the CDU never internalised climate protection, which matches her “naive” admission.

B · “Right goal, wrong instruments, real costs” interviews and essays · C3/5

Veronika Grimm (German Council of Economic Experts), Ralf Fücks (Zentrum Liberale Moderne)

Grimm warns that electricity prices will stay high for a long time and that renewables are not “cheap” once grids, storage and backup are counted, which questions Neubauer’s “nothing is cheaper”. Fücks, as quoted by Ronzheimer, argues that blaming the fossil lobby avoids the harder truth that renewables have barely displaced fossil fuels globally and that a growing world will need more than sun and wind.

Difference from Neubauer: shares the goal, disputes the cost story and the explanation for COP failure. Neubauer locates the obstacle in interests and weak diplomacy; this camp locates it in physics, cost and global energy demand.

C · “Germany as wrong-way driver” op-eds · C3/5

Hans-Werner Sinn (former ifo president)

Sinn calls Germany a “Geisterfahrer” (wrong-way driver) and argues that the EU combustion-engine ban can even accelerate climate change: fuel Europe does not burn becomes cheaper and is burned elsewhere. This is the economic version of Ronzheimer’s question about countries that do not take part.

Difference from Neubauer: fundamental on the leakage question. Neubauer’s answer, that renewables pay off even without any climate benefit, is precisely the claim this camp disputes.

D · “The movement has lost the public” polls and studies · C3–4

Ronzheimer’s questions; infratest dimap; Bertelsmann/RIFS; YouGov. Counter-evidence: Maria Waldinger (ifo); Öko-Institut

The polls support part of Ronzheimer’s frame. According to Edenhofer, the share naming climate among their top three issues fell from about 34% (2021) to about 16%. Bertelsmann finds 80% still concerned but support for the Energiewende down to 60% from 69% five years earlier; infratest dimap found Fridays for Future left little lasting mark on attitudes. Against the backlash thesis: an ifo study attributes about 7% of the Greens’ vote gains in 2019–21 to the protests, and an August 2026 Öko-Institut study finds Bild’s “Heizungshammer” campaign shifted the 2023–24 heating law, which points to media, not activists, as a driver of backlash.

Difference from Neubauer: on the cause. Both sides accept that climate lost priority. Whether the movement’s tone, the cost of specific laws, or campaigning media drove it is not settled by the evidence found here; no study linking the protests to AfD gains was found.

E · “Clear separation from Thunberg” primary statements, 2023 · C4/5

Josef Schuster (Central Council of Jews in Germany)

In October 2023 Schuster asked Fridays for Future Germany to cut ties with the international movement and change its name, and called Thunberg “naive or even antisemitic”. Neubauer then refused the name change as symbolic politics but said trust was badly damaged and joint work on hold.

Difference from Neubauer: the two occasions are not directly comparable: 2023 was about Thunberg’s statements after 7 October, 2026 about her flotilla companion. In 2026 Neubauer defers a judgment on Ávila and adds a self-criticism that the movement spoke too late on Gaza. No reaction to the new remarks was found.

Where they really disagree

  • 1 · What caused Germany’s industrial weakness? Wrong corporate choices and China (Neubauer, Edenhofer) or energy costs and regulation (Sinn, Grimm). Both are partly supported; the weights are disputed.
  • 2 · Are renewables the cheapest option for the system? At the generation level largely yes; once grids and backup are included, contested (Grimm).
  • 3 · Does German action change the global outcome? Neubauer: yes, through the EU, technology and diplomacy. Sinn: leakage cancels much of it.
  • 4 · Why did public support fall? Movement tone, cost of specific laws, war and inflation, or media campaigns. The polls show the fall, not its cause.
  • 5 · Who failed at COP31? The government blames the oil states; Neubauer says Germany and the EU did not deliver what they should have; observers such as Niklas Höhne and ZDF’s correspondent add that Europe brought little money to negotiate with.

Who is speaking, and with what stake

Neubauer is an activist, Green party member and author; her standing depends on climate staying a public issue, and she argues as an advocate, not a neutral analyst. Ronzheimer works for Bild, whose “Heizungshammer” campaign is documented by the Öko-Institut as having shaped the climate debate; readers may weigh that, though we found no evidence that it shaped his questions, which are standard sceptic’s questions. His own premises (“dogma”, the movement’s share in radicalisation) are as contestable as hers and were not checked here. Neubauer once hints at the house, calling hyping someone up “a Springer concept”. The compiler is an Anthropic model with no stake in German climate policy.

◆

The big picture

Our synthesis of the positions above, not a forecast. No recommendations.

The interview shows a shift in the climate argument in Germany. In 2019 the movement argued from science and justice. In 2026 Neubauer argues mainly from cost and sovereignty: import bills, heat damage, Hormuz, Putin, Trump. That is the ground on which her critics also stand, and most of her figures survive the check on that ground, with roundings in her favour.

Where the argument stays open is causation. That climate lost priority is measured; why is not. That German industry is weak is measured; how much of it is energy policy and how much corporate strategy and Chinese competition is not. That COP31 failed is fact; whether a more ambitious Europe could have prevented it is a judgment.

On Thunberg she separates motive from company: she praises Thunberg’s commitment to Palestinians, calls criticism of her companions justified, defers a judgment on Ávila until she has looked into the case, and calls keeping away from terrorists and their friends self-evident. Her position on the specific case is not yet settled; she has promised to say more.
