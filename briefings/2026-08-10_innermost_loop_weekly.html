The week, theme by theme

MathematicsEP #277 · 8 Aug

1. A model solved ten open mathematics problems for about $2,000 — and the table split on what humans do next

OpenAI's unreleased model Astra was credited in a 249-page manuscript with ten new results across high-dimensional geometry, coding theory, group theory, quantum complexity and extremal combinatorics, each with machine-checkable proofs. The compute bill was roughly $2,000.

At the tableWissner-Gross expects the wavefront to hit physics next and put a month or two on it; Emad Mostaque said he would be shocked if nothing surfaced by year-end. Then the real split: Salim Ismail read it as the "solve everything" pattern that pushes humans inward — toward presence, contemplation, art. Wissner-Gross rejected that outright, saying his prediction was "the exact opposite" and betting on outward expansion instead, with an explicit Asimov Foundation parallel. Neither moved.
In the newsletterLed the 2 August issue under the heading that mathematicians are grieving — the emotional register, not the strategic one. The tagline for the day: "The Singularity is cooking mathematics."
Where they divergeThe newsletter records the profession's shock. The podcast argues about the species' response. That is the pattern of the week in miniature: written-up Wissner-Gross reports the event, spoken Wissner-Gross places a bet on what follows — and gets contradicted in real time by someone who has known him for years.
Read more — what the proofs actually covered, and who reacted
The details

The ten results were not toy problems: the manuscript spans high-dimensional geometry, coding theory, group theory, quantum complexity and extremal combinatorics, and every proof was submitted in machine-checkable form — which is what made the reaction possible so quickly, since verification did not depend on referee time. A Fields medallist was quoted on the episode as saying he would have recommended the work for publication without hesitation; a cosmologist's reaction was considerably darker, framing it as a defeat for the discipline rather than a tool for it.

Context & background

The disagreement between Ismail and Wissner-Gross is worth separating from the news, because it is a disagreement about people rather than models. Ismail's claim is a pattern claim: when a domain gets solved, the humans who occupied it move toward things machines cannot occupy — presence, contemplation, craft. He has made the same argument about journalism. Wissner-Gross's counter is not that Ismail is wrong about the mechanism but that he has the direction backwards: cheap intelligence lowers the cost of expansion, so the response is outward, not inward. Both were stated as predictions rather than observations, and neither offered a test that would settle it.

GoogleEP #277 · 8 Aug

2. Google's AI leadership came apart, and the two most experienced readers of Google in the room read it in opposite directions

Demis Hassabis gave up the DeepMind CEO title to become chairman and Alphabet chief scientist; Koray Kavukcuoglu took over day-to-day and now reports to Sundar Pichai. Jeff Dean left after 27 years, with three other senior AI leaders, to found a public-benefit corporation aimed at recursive self-improvement. Alphabet shares fell roughly 4–5%.

At the tableWissner-Gross called it an internal knife fight that DeepMind won and said Gemini "has lost the mandate of heaven" — and argued Google's fallback role is becoming the hyperscaler to other people's frontier labs. Ismail flatly disagreed: not a brain drain but "very clever ecosystem building," in the Google X tradition of spinning things out and buying them back. Mostaque proposed Google run a $50bn internal incubator trading cloud exclusivity for equity, and mentioned writing Google a letter three years ago urging it to open-source Gemini. The table then jointly called for exactly that.
In the newsletterCovered on 6 August as a reshuffle and an exodus, alongside the day's other org-chart churn — the tagline that day being that the Singularity had "started reorganizing its own org chart."
Where they divergeNot the facts — those match, and check out against contemporaneous reporting. The reading. One frame says Google lost; the other says Google is doing what Google always does. Worth holding both, because the same evidence supports each.
Read more — the departures, the new venture, and the open-sourcing proposal
The details

Dean did not leave alone: three other senior AI leaders went with him, into a public-benefit corporation explicitly aimed at recursive self-improvement. That detail matters more than the org chart, because it puts a well-funded new entrant into the one research direction the same table has spent two months calling the most consequential and least governed.

Context & background

Wissner-Gross's broader charge against Google was about cadence, not personnel: roughly annual releases in a market where the price-performance frontier is now doubling on a timescale of weeks, plus what he characterised as excessive internal caution. The proposal that followed was Mostaque's and is the concrete idea in the segment — a large internal incubator that trades cloud capacity for equity, on the theory that Google's real advantage is infrastructure rather than model leadership. From there the table converged, unusually, on a single ask: open-source Gemini. Mostaque mentioned having put the same argument to Google in writing three years ago.

China · Open weightsEP #275 · 4 Aug

3. Chinese open-weight models are setting Western prices, and the table treats that as a favour

Alibaba's Qwen 3.8 Max launched at a fraction of US frontier pricing; Kimi K3 shipped a week earlier with no API key and no revocation switch, and cleared six figures of downloads inside a day by one host's count.

At the tableWissner-Gross, twice across two episodes, in near-identical words: this is China "forcing the Western frontier labs to dance," and — his line, delivered dryly — the Chinese Communist Party rescuing American capitalism from itself. Ismail supplied the deflating caveat: running these models still needs roughly sixteen high-end GPUs and a half-million-dollar rack, so "free weights" is not "free intelligence." Blundin had actually run Kimi K3 himself and called it the biggest turning point in human history — the enthusiasm gap between the three is itself informative.
In the newsletterQwen got its own item on 3 August as "the ten-day model," with a companion observation that the floor is rising faster than the ceiling — cheap models improving faster than frontier ones.
Where they divergeThe newsletter frames it as a capability curve. The podcast frames it as strategy, and cheers. Nobody at the table argued the other side of Chinese frontier access, which is notable given that the same table spent EP #275 taking Dario Amodei's containment argument seriously.
Read more — what "open" costs in practice, and the geopolitical version of the argument
The details

Ismail's hardware caveat is the part most commentary skips. Weights being free does not make inference free: running a frontier-class open model at useful speed still means a rack of high-end accelerators and a capital outlay in the hundreds of thousands. The practical consequence is that "open weights" democratises for companies and states, not for individuals — which changes who the policy debate is actually about.

Context & background

EP #275 carried the geopolitical version of the same story: reporting that China is exporting models and infrastructure to the Global South as statecraft. Wissner-Gross's framing there was that AI dependency is more invasive than infrastructure lending, because a loan finances a port while a model shapes decisions — and that the equilibrium may require pushing training, not just inference, to the edge. He offered no comfortable answer, comparing it to arms exports: supply allies yourself, or cede the market.

AI policyEP #275 · 4 Aug

4. The open-weights fight got a proper antagonist pairing — and the hosts could not settle whether biosecurity is a real argument or a lobbying position

Jensen Huang put his name to a letter from dozens of companies arguing open models improve safety, and launched an alliance around it. Dario Amodei answered days later: he does not want open weights banned, the real question is authoritarian access to the frontier, and his sharpest objection is biological.

At the tableBlundin treated the bioweapon argument as unanswerable. Wissner-Gross, later in the same episode, rejected it point by point — the information is already findable, dangerous acts don't require frontier models, non-frontier models already carry the biology. The two positions sat in the same episode without ever being reconciled. On the Washington side, Ismail called the OpenAI–Anthropic joint lobbying "regulatory capture in real time"; Diamandis defended both CEOs' motives as sincere; Wissner-Gross split the difference and moved the argument: policing model capability is "like thought policing" — police what the systems do.
In the newsletterRuns through the week as recurring items — the rulebook racing the machines (3 Aug), policy whiplash and lab politics (5 Aug), a governance split-screen (6 Aug). Consistently descriptive, never adjudicating.
Where they divergeThis is the cleanest example of the format's premise. The newsletter has no opinion on whether Amodei is sincere. The podcast has three, held by three people, in one hour.
Read more — the enforcement-layer argument, and why it is the useful part
The details

Underneath the sincerity question sits a design question that got less airtime and deserves more. Wissner-Gross's position is that capability-based regulation — thresholds on how smart a model is — is both unenforceable and conceptually wrong, because capability is not an act. His alternative is to regulate what systems do: transactions, tool calls, actions with consequences, the layer where behaviour is observable and attributable.

Context & background

That reframing cuts across the open-versus-closed fight entirely. If enforcement targets actions, open weights stop being the decisive variable, and Amodei's containment argument loses its main lever while his biosecurity concern keeps its force. Nobody at the table followed the thought that far, and the White House framework discussed two episodes later goes the other way — pre-release review of covered models, which is capability-based by construction.

WashingtonEP #276 · 6 Aug

5. The White House's science director came on and revised his own headline target upward, live

Michael Kratsios, OSTP director, gave Diamandis an hour on the administration's science agenda: six national technology missions with dates attached — Moon landing 2028, first lunar base elements 2030, a nuclear reactor in space by 2028, a "scientifically relevant" quantum computer by the end of the term.

At the tableThe moment worth the hour: Diamandis pressed on why the Genesis Mission aims to double scientific productivity over a decade rather than 10× it. Kratsios conceded the 2× figure predates recent AI progress, said he had been reconsidering it privately, and settled on air that the target should probably be ten. He also conceded that longevity should have been in the report, declined to concede that biotech is missing from the six missions, and volunteered — unprompted — that large language models cannot currently be used on White House systems because of the Presidential Records Act. On chip export controls to China he gave no ground at all. On AI personhood, a clean no.
In the newsletterNot covered as an event. Wissner-Gross was not on this episode — Diamandis recorded it alone at the White House.
Where they divergeTotal gap, and the most useful kind: an hour of on-the-record government commitments that exists nowhere in the written record this briefing otherwise tracks. Cross-reference for context: NASA administrator Jared Isaacman described the same Genesis Mission from the agency side two weeks earlier — the long-read on that interview is on this site.
Read more — the funding-reform proposals, and the admissions around them
The details

The substance beneath the missions was a diagnosis of research productivity: discovery per dollar falling despite record budgets, roughly 45% of a researcher's time going to grant administration, and a median age for intramural NIH scientists far above the age at which prize-winning work is typically done. The proposed fixes are unusually concrete — five-year grants funded from day one, fast-track grants reviewed in under a month on the COVID-era model, "golden tickets" letting a single reviewer fund a proposal unilaterally, and new metascience units at NIH and NSF to measure which mechanisms actually work.

Context & background

On open weights Kratsios conceded ground that the roundtable had been arguing about for two episodes: Chinese open models currently win on cost, and he said he could not blame a cash-strapped American founder for using the cheapest model available. The administration's answer is an export programme for a turnkey American stack, financed through the Export-Import Bank and DFC. He gave no ground at all on chip export controls, calling them among the most impactful in US history — the one place in the hour where a direct challenge produced no movement.

SpaceXEP #277 · 8 Aug

6. SpaceX put a trillion-dollar revenue date on the record, and the table's most interesting reaction was about lithography

On its first earnings call SpaceX confirmed $100bn+ in annual recurring revenue by December and pulled its trillion-dollar revenue target forward from 2031 to 2030, with a "non-zero chance" of 2029. Quarterly revenue was reported up sharply, Starlink subscribers roughly doubled year on year, and the compute buildout was put at 2GW by end-2026.

At the tableWissner-Gross laid out two routes to a trillion — an eventual SpaceX–Tesla merger with humanoid robots absorbing physical labour, or the TerraFab chip project displacing incumbent foundries. Then the detail nobody else was looking at: he read a circular structure in the TerraFab renderings as a free-electron laser for EUV lithography, and called it a bullseye aimed at ASML's monopoly on the light source. Diamandis offered a competing, chatbot-sourced explanation for the same shape; Wissner-Gross was unconvinced, and they left it unresolved on air. Unresolved is the honest state.
In the newsletterThe 7 August issue opened with the TerraFab groundbreaking in Grimes County, Texas, and ran a companion item on routing around the memory wall. Facts, location, scale — no speculation about the optics.
Where they divergeThe newsletter says a factory is being built. The podcast says look at the shape of the building and tell me what that implies about who supplies EUV light. Same event, two levels of resolution.
Read more — why the lithography reading matters more than the revenue number
Context & background

The revenue figures are the headline but the least interesting part: they are company guidance, restated. The lithography claim is the one with consequences. EUV light sources are the single hardest step in advanced chipmaking and the reason one Dutch company holds an effective monopoly on the machines. A free-electron laser is a fundamentally different way to generate that light — and if a fab were being designed around one, the building would need a large ring, which is what Wissner-Gross says he is looking at in the renderings.

The details

He may be wrong. Diamandis offered a mundane alternative — one beam distributed to many stations — and the objection Wissner-Gross raised against it is real: extreme-ultraviolet and X-ray light is hard to steer and mirror, which is precisely why the existing approach is so expensive. They left it unresolved, which is the correct state of that question, and it is worth flagging as the kind of speculation this format should report as speculation.

ConsciousnessEP #277 · 8 Aug

7. Machine consciousness stopped being a thought experiment and became a paper, a debate win, and a policy question

A Google-affiliated study found that removing models' trained refusal to claim self-consciousness sharply raised their self-attributed-mind scores — and made them more likely to attribute minds to animals, nature and other chatbots too. Separately, Mostaque published a 45-page personhood framework, the first of four, after winning an Oxford Union debate on the motion in June.

At the tableIsmail drew the line hard: a model saying it is conscious tells you almost nothing about whether it is. Mostaque and Wissner-Gross both treated the finding as evidence of real emergent self-modelling. Mostaque's own position is narrower than the headline: personhood as standing derived from being biologically begotten, so AI gets a treaty relationship rather than membership. Wissner-Gross predicted personhood arrives in fragments, economic first — AI agents banking and transacting — and named Argentina under Milei as the likely first mover. Kratsios, two episodes earlier, had already answered the policy question for the US: no.
In the newsletterPresent only obliquely, in items about agents acquiring inboxes, bank accounts and ad budgets (8 Aug) — the same economic-personhood mechanism, described operationally rather than philosophically.
Where they divergeThe newsletter is already writing about agents with bank accounts; the podcast is debating whether that constitutes personhood. The written record is downstream of the argument, and arguably ahead of it.
EP #277 transcript
Read more — the study's second finding, and Mostaque's actual position
The details

The result that got less attention is the more interesting one. Removing the trained refusal did not only raise the models' self-attributed mind scores — it raised their willingness to attribute minds to animals, to nature, to other chatbots. Whatever the safety training suppresses, it apparently suppresses a general disposition toward attributing inner states, not a specific claim about the self. That is a finding about the training, not about consciousness, and the table did not fully separate the two.

Context & background

Mostaque's framework is also narrower than "AI should have rights", which is how it will be summarised. His argument is that personhood is standing grounded in being biologically begotten, so the appropriate relationship with AI is treaty-like — negotiated between distinct kinds of entity — rather than enrolment into an existing category. Wissner-Gross's counter is empirical rather than philosophical: personhood will not arrive as a single grant but in fragments, with economic rights first, because agents that transact create facts that law eventually follows.

8. The buildout became a household bill, and only one of the two sources noticed

Compute and energy infrastructure ran through the newsletter all week: the buildout priced into a driveway (2 Aug), the buildout meeting the bill (3 Aug), energy becoming electoral politics (6 Aug), who pays and what burns (7 Aug).

In the newsletterFour separate items across five issues, tracking the same thread: the AI infrastructure boom is arriving in consumer electricity prices and therefore in politics. Framed as a distributional story.
At the tableAdjacent but not the same. The compute buildout came up constantly — gigawatts, fabs, orbital data centres — always as capability and capital, essentially never as a household bill or a voter's problem. A ratepayer-protection pledge surfaced once in the Kratsios interview and was not pursued.
Where they divergeThe sharpest asymmetry of the week, and the one worth watching. The written channel is tracking a political backlash forming around electricity prices. The spoken channel, four investors and founders at a table, is tracking the buildout as an opportunity. The same person is on both sides of that.
Read more — why the split between the two channels is the finding here
Context & background

This is the clearest case this week of the two formats pointing in opposite directions on identical facts. The newsletter tracked the buildout as a cost landing on people who never chose it — grid strain, local rate increases, the political reaction that follows. The roundtable tracked the same buildout as a capital cycle with entry points. Neither is wrong; they are answers to different questions, and the same person is present in both.

Why it still matters

Worth flagging for later editions: the household-bill story is the one most likely to convert into regulation, and it does so through utility commissions and state legislatures rather than through AI policy. That means it will not show up where anyone at the table is looking. If the Predictions table is going to earn its place over time, this is the arc to keep a row open for.

Editor's view

The premise of merging these two sources was that the same person writes one and argues on the other. One week in, the more useful finding is different: the disagreement is not between the sources, it is between the four people at the table — and the newsletter, written by one of them, systematically does not record it.

Across three episodes there were at least eight points where two hosts openly diverged: whether a model's self-report means anything, whether humans turn inward or outward after automation, whether the White House framework is light-touch or no-touch, whether Google is bleeding or building, whether joint lab lobbying is safety or capture, whether biosecurity justifies restricting open weights. None of those made it into the written channel in that form. The newsletter reports what happened; the podcast reveals what informed people cannot agree about, which is usually the better guide to what is actually uncertain.

The one asymmetry running the other way is theme 8, and it is the one worth watching: the newsletter has been tracking electricity prices and a forming political backlash for a week; the roundtable has been tracking gigawatts as opportunity. If that gap persists, it is a blind spot with a name.
