10 years ago2016Wells Fargo's fake accounts · North Korea's fifth test · an asteroid launch · a league-wide protest
BUSINESS2016Sep 8, 2016

Wells Fargo admits its staff opened two million fake accounts to hit sales quotas

On September 8, 2016, the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau fined Wells Fargo $100 million — the largest penalty in the agency's five-year history — after finding employees had opened more than two million deposit and credit-card accounts customers never authorized, moving their money to meet sales targets. The bank paid an additional $85 million to the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency and the City of Los Angeles the same day.

Wells Fargo fired 5,300 employees over the practice, but its CEO, John Stumpf, initially kept his job; he resigned under congressional pressure a month later.

Context & background. Wells Fargo had run an internal cross-selling program since at least 2011, ranking branches on how many products — checking, savings, credit cards — each customer held. When employees couldn't hit the quotas honestly, some opened accounts in customers' names without consent, sometimes forging signatures, and shifted money between accounts to fund them, generating fees on products no customer had asked for.

The details. The CFPB order covered accounts opened without authorization between 2011 and 2016. Wells Fargo paid $100 million to the CFPB — its largest fine to date at the time — plus $35 million to the OCC and $50 million to the City and County of Los Angeles, which had sued separately over the same conduct. The settlement language did not require Wells Fargo to admit wrongdoing, standard for such orders, though the bank did not contest the underlying findings.

Why it still matters. Stumpf told Congress the practice was the fault of low-level employees; lawmakers weren't persuaded, and he resigned that October, with tens of millions in prior compensation later clawed back by the board. The case reshaped how U.S. regulators scrutinize sales-incentive structures at retail banks and remains the reference point whenever a bank is accused of pressuring staff into misconduct.

POLITICS2016Sep 9, 2016

North Korea sets off its fifth and, to that point, largest nuclear test

North Korea conducted its fifth underground nuclear test on September 9, 2016 — the 68th anniversary of the state's founding — at its Punggye-ri site. South Korea, the United States and China condemned it within hours; Seoul called it "fanatic recklessness."

It was the country's second test that year, coming eight months after a smaller one in January, and pushed the UN Security Council toward tougher sanctions than those passed after the earlier test.

Context & background. North Korea had tested nuclear devices in 2006, 2009, 2013 and January 2016, each time drawing new UN sanctions that did little to slow the program. Kim Jong Un timed the fifth test to the anniversary of North Korea's 1948 founding, a propaganda calendar the state uses deliberately for weapons milestones.

The details. Seismic monitors registered the blast within minutes, before Pyongyang's own announcement, which described it as validating a standardized warhead design that could be fitted to ballistic missiles. President Obama called for "serious consequences"; the Security Council moved toward a further sanctions resolution, adopted that November, restricting coal exports and other trade beyond what followed the January test.

Why it still matters. The test preceded North Korea's first claimed hydrogen-bomb test and its intercontinental-range missile launches the following year, the run-up to the 2018 Kim–Trump summit diplomacy. The anniversary-timed pattern of tests continued for years afterward.

SCIENCE2016Sep 8, 2016

NASA launches its first mission to bring back a piece of an asteroid

OSIRIS-REx lifted off from Cape Canaveral on September 8, 2016 atop an Atlas V rocket, beginning a round trip to the asteroid Bennu and back — the first U.S. mission designed to collect a sample from an asteroid and return it to Earth.

The spacecraft reached Bennu in December 2018, collected material in October 2020, and delivered roughly 122 grams of it to Earth in September 2023, more than four times the mission's original target.

Context & background. Bennu is a roughly 500-metre carbon-rich asteroid, chosen partly because its orbit brings it close to Earth every six years and partly because it carries a small but real chance of a late-22nd-century Earth impact — giving the mission a planetary-defense angle alongside the science.

The details. The spacecraft used a robotic arm called TAGSAM to briefly touch Bennu's surface and fire a burst of nitrogen gas to stir up loose material for collection, rather than landing. Mission managers had to replan the touch-and-go site late, after finding Bennu's surface far rockier than pre-launch images had suggested. The sample capsule parachuted into the Utah desert on September 24, 2023; the spacecraft itself, renamed OSIRIS-APEx, is now en route to a second asteroid, Apophis.

Why it still matters. The Bennu sample is the largest ever returned from an asteroid and is still being distributed to laboratories worldwide; early analysis reported amino acids and other building blocks of life in the material. It set the template NASA and other agencies have since followed for sample-return missions to primitive bodies.

SPORT2016Sep 11, 2016

Kaepernick's anthem protest spreads across the NFL on opening Sunday

On September 11, 2016 — the league's first Sunday of the regular season and the 15th anniversary of the September 11 attacks — anthem protests inspired by Colin Kaepernick's preseason sit-downs reached beyond the San Francisco 49ers for the first time. All 53 Seattle Seahawks players and head coach Pete Carroll linked arms during the anthem; other players around the league knelt or raised a fist.

Kaepernick, then a backup quarterback, had first sat during the anthem that August to protest police violence and racial injustice; by opening weekend the gesture had become a league-wide talking point rather than one player's stance.

Context & background. Kaepernick sat, and later knelt, during the anthem starting in the 2016 preseason, saying he would not "show pride in a flag for a country that oppresses black people and people of color." The NFL had no rule at the time requiring players to be on the field for the anthem at all.

The details. By the September 11 games, teammate Eric Reid had begun kneeling alongside Kaepernick, Rams players raised fists, and the Seahawks — who opened at home that Sunday — chose team-wide arm-linking as a middle path between protest and normalcy. Miami's ownership said it would not require players to stand but preferred they did.

Why it still matters. The gesture outlasted Kaepernick's own NFL career — he opted out of his 49ers contract after the season and was not signed by another team — and recurred every subsequent season, eventually prompting a 2018 NFL policy requiring players on the field to stand, which the league later did not enforce. It remains the reference point for athlete protest during the anthem in American sport.

25 years ago2001An assassination two days early · September 11 · an airline's collapse · a quiz-show fraud
CONFLICT2001Sep 9, 2001

Al-Qaeda assassins kill Afghan resistance leader Massoud two days before 9/11

On September 9, 2001, two al-Qaeda operatives posing as journalists detonated a bomb hidden in a camera during a staged interview with Ahmad Shah Massoud, the Northern Alliance's defense minister and the most effective military opponent the Taliban and al-Qaeda faced inside Afghanistan. Massoud died of his wounds; the bombers were killed in the blast.

The killing had been ordered by Osama bin Laden months earlier, after Massoud warned the European Parliament that al-Qaeda was planning a major attack on American soil.

Context & background. Massoud, known as the "Lion of Panjshir," had fought Soviet forces, rival Afghan factions and then the Taliban continuously since the late 1970s, holding the Panjshir Valley as the last significant territory outside Taliban control by 2001. In April 2001 he addressed the European Parliament warning that if the world did not help stop the Taliban and al-Qaeda, "they will strike in a way that will shock the world."

The details. The assassins, Abd as-Sattar Dahmane and Rachid Bouraoui el-Ouaer, both Tunisian nationals, had spent weeks arranging the interview through a Belgium-based Islamist network, posing as journalists. The bomb, built into a camera's battery pack, detonated as the interview began. A declassified U.S. Defense Intelligence Agency report later noted that Massoud's own intelligence network had picked up fragments of al-Qaeda's plans for an attack on the United States before his death.

Why it still matters. General Mohammed Fahim succeeded Massoud as military commander two days before the September 11 attacks made the Northern Alliance the United States' primary ground ally in the invasion of Afghanistan that October. Whether the timing was coordinated with 9/11 to weaken that ally in advance remains, per available assessments, unproven rather than disproven.

DISASTER2001Sep 11, 2001

Hijacked airliners kill nearly 3,000 people in New York, Washington and Pennsylvania

On the morning of September 11, 2001, 19 al-Qaeda hijackers took control of four domestic U.S. flights. American Airlines Flight 11 struck the World Trade Center's North Tower at 8:46 a.m.; United Flight 175 struck the South Tower at 9:03 a.m.; American Flight 77 struck the Pentagon at 9:37 a.m.; and United Flight 93, apparently headed for Washington, crashed in a field near Shanksville, Pennsylvania at 10:03 a.m. after passengers stormed the cockpit.

Both towers collapsed within two hours of impact. 2,977 people died, excluding the 19 hijackers; the FAA grounded all civilian aircraft over the United States, the first such shutdown in the agency's history.

Context & background. The 19 hijackers were organized into four teams by al-Qaeda, some trained at U.S. flight schools over the preceding two years, and boarded flights carrying only box-cutters and knives, exploiting the era's minimal cockpit security. Flights 11 and 175 had departed Boston within minutes of each other that morning.

The details. The South Tower, hit second, collapsed first, at 9:59 a.m., because the impact severed more of its central support columns; the North Tower fell at 10:28 a.m. Recovery work at the World Trade Center site continued for months; the toll includes 343 New York City firefighters and 71 law-enforcement officers who died in the towers' collapse. Congress passed the Authorization for Use of Military Force three days later, and the U.S. began bombing Taliban and al-Qaeda positions in Afghanistan on October 7.

Why it still matters. The attacks reshaped U.S. aviation security (the Transportation Security Administration was created within two months), foreign policy (two decades of war in Afghanistan followed, ending in 2021) and domestic law (the USA PATRIOT Act, passed that October). Osama bin Laden was killed by U.S. forces in Pakistan in 2011.

BUSINESS2001Sep 12, 2001

Australia's second airline collapses, costing 16,000 jobs

Air New Zealand placed the Ansett Australia group into voluntary administration on September 12, 2001, ending the 65-year-old airline's operations and putting more than 16,000 people out of work — the largest single mass job loss in Australian history.

Ansett's problems predated the September 11 attacks — the carrier was losing roughly A$1.3 million a day to an aging fleet and competition from Qantas and budget rivals — but the attacks' shock to global aviation removed any remaining chance of a rescue.

Context & background. Ansett was Australia's second-largest domestic carrier, founded in 1936 by Reginald Ansett and bought outright by Air New Zealand in 2000. It had been losing money for years on an aging fleet, high labor costs and Qantas's dominance of the domestic market before Air New Zealand, itself under financial strain, decided against further capital injections.

The details. Administrators from PricewaterhouseCoopers found "no realistic chance for Ansett to be revived as a viable concern" within days of taking over. Employees eventually recovered about 96% of their entitlements through an Air New Zealand compensation package, asset sales and a federal government loan repaid via a passenger ticket levy that lasted into the following decade.

Why it still matters. The collapse briefly left Qantas as Australia's only major full-service domestic carrier, prompting government moves to encourage new entrants — Virgin Blue expanded rapidly into the gap — and left a lasting duopoly structure in Australian domestic aviation that persisted for two decades.

CULTURE2001Sep 10, 2001

A British Army major cheats his way to a £1 million quiz-show jackpot using planted coughs

Over two days of recording at Elstree Studios, contestant Charles Ingram reached the top prize on the UK version of Who Wants to Be a Millionaire? on September 10, 2001, with a fellow audience member, Tecwen Whittock, coughing to signal correct answers as Ingram read them aloud.

Production staff grew suspicious during taping and reviewed the footage before broadcast; the episode was never aired as a normal show, only later shown as evidence in a fraud trial.

Context & background. Ingram, a major in the British Army, appeared on the show with his wife Diana, who had herself previously won £32,000 on the programme and was known to producers. Coughing from the studio audience was audible on the recording each time Ingram hesitated over an answer before confidently choosing the right one.

The details. Production staff, alerted by the pattern, delayed paying out the prize and handed the tape to lawyers. Ingram, his wife and Whittock were tried at Southwark Crown Court and convicted in April 2003 of "procuring the execution of a valuable security by deception." Ingram received an 18-month suspended sentence, a £15,000 fine and had to pay £10,000 in prosecution costs; the Army separately forced his resignation later in 2003 after 16 years' service.

Why it still matters. Because the episode never aired as intended, the case became known through news coverage and later dramatisation — including the 2020 ITV drama Quiz — rather than through the broadcast itself, and remains Britain's reference case for game-show fraud. Ingram and his wife maintained their innocence for years afterward.

POLITICS1976Sep 9, 1976

Mao Zedong dies, ending Communist China's founding era

Mao Zedong died at 00:10 on September 9, 1976, in Beijing at age 82, after months of declining health; the Communist Party waited until 4 p.m. that day to announce it, in a national radio broadcast appealing for unity.

His death set off an immediate power struggle inside the leadership that ended a month later with the arrest of the "Gang of Four," clearing the way for Deng Xiaoping's rise and China's economic reforms from 1978.

Context & background. Mao had led the Chinese Communist Party since the 1930s, founded the People's Republic in 1949, and by the mid-1970s was largely incapacitated by Parkinson's disease and heart problems, rarely appearing in public in his final year.

The details. Life support was withdrawn on the night of September 8 after doctors determined recovery was impossible. Vice-chairman Wang Dongxing took charge of immediate arrangements; a 377-member funeral committee was formed, though it doubled as a battleground for factional politics, with some officials pointedly excluded. Flags flew at half-staff and all public entertainment was suspended nationwide for a week.

Why it still matters. Within a month, Hua Guofeng and the military moved to arrest the Gang of Four, ending the Cultural Revolution in all but name and opening the path to Deng Xiaoping's return and the market reforms that began in 1978 — the policy shift that produced the China of today.

DISASTER1976Sep 10, 1976

176 die as two airliners collide over Zagreb in the world's deadliest mid-air crash to date

A British Airways Trident and an Inex-Adria DC-9 collided at 33,000 feet over Zagreb on September 10, 1976, killing all 176 people aboard both aircraft — at the time the deadliest mid-air collision in aviation history.

Investigators found an understaffed, overworked Zagreb air-traffic control sector had let the DC-9 climb into the Trident's altitude; the sole controller on duty tried a last-second warning in the wrong language for the crew he was addressing, which made the situation worse rather than better.

Context & background. Yugoslav airspace over Zagreb sat under one of Europe's busiest but least modernised air-traffic corridors, handling transit traffic between northern Europe and the Mediterranean with radar and staffing that had not kept pace with 1970s traffic growth.

The details. The upper-sector controller was working the position alone because of a staffing shortfall; when the DC-9 climbed to the Trident's assigned flight level 330, he attempted a last-moment correction over the radio in Serbo-Croatian to the British Airways crew, who could not understand the instruction in time. The two aircraft collided almost head-on; wreckage fell across a wide area outside the city.

Why it still matters. The disaster — still Croatia's deadliest aviation accident — pushed European states toward standardising English as the working language of international air-traffic control and accelerated investment in radar coverage and controller staffing across the region, part of the broader push that eventually made collision-avoidance systems (TCAS) mandatory decades later.

CONFLICT1976Sep 10–11, 1976

Croatian nationalists hijack a TWA jet and a bomb they planted kills a New York bomb-squad officer

Five Croatian nationalists led by Zvonko Bušić hijacked TWA Flight 355 out of New York on September 10, 1976, demanding newspapers publish a pro-independence appeal and claiming to have planted a bomb in a Grand Central Terminal locker.

The bomb was real: while an NYPD bomb squad tried to move and disable it on September 11, it detonated, killing officer Brian Murray and wounding a second officer.

Context & background. Bušić and four accomplices — including his American wife, Julienne — belonged to a Croatian nationalist movement seeking independence from Yugoslavia and had planned the hijacking to draw attention to their cause, giving detailed instructions for finding and disarming the Grand Central device rather than hiding it.

The details. The hijackers directed the TWA jet on a transatlantic diversion via Canada, Iceland and London before landing in Paris, and surrendered to French police once their statement had been published as demanded. In New York, officers Brian Murray and Terrence McTigue attempted to move the locker bomb to a firing range for safe disposal; it detonated during the attempt, killing Murray.

Why it still matters. Bušić was convicted of air piracy resulting in death and served 32 years before parole and deportation to Croatia in 2008; he died by suicide in 2013. The case is an early example of hijacking used as a media strategy for a nationalist cause, a tactic that recurred through the following two decades of airliner hijackings before cockpit security tightened after 2001.

Editor's view

Two of the three eras share a real thread: the Zagreb collision and the Grand Central bombing sit fifty years back as failures of a young, understaffed air-traffic system and improvised nationalist terrorism; twenty-five years later, in the same calendar week, hijacked airliners became a weapon on a different scale entirely. The other six items this week don't share that thread — a bank's decade-old fraud, a dictator's death, an asteroid launch, a quiz-show con — and forcing one across all eleven would be dishonest. Read each era for what actually happened in it, not for a moral it doesn't carry.
