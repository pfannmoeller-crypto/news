Dwarkesh Patel's AI theses, 2023–2026 — read as one argument

Over three years of essays, the podcast host has built a single position: that continual learning is the bottleneck standing between today's models and anything worth calling AGI, that the economy's failure to absorb AI is a capability problem rather than a diffusion problem, and that this makes him bearish for a few years and wilder-than-consensus bullish after that. He has attached dates to it. This is that argument in the order he developed it, with the checks and the named opposition where they belong.

His argument in brief

The theses at full strength, before any checking — with his own framing device up front: "Things take longer to happen than you think they will, and then they happen faster than you thought they could." (Rudiger Dornbusch, whom he quotes to open the June 2025 essay.)

Continual learning is the bottleneck, and it is not a detail.

Today's models don't get better the way a person does. You cannot give one high-level feedback and watch it improve; you are stuck with what comes out of the box, and prompt engineering is not a substitute. The reason human workers are valuable is not raw intelligence — it is the ability to build context, notice their own failures, and pick up small efficiencies through practice. Nothing in the current paradigm does that.

The failure to absorb AI is a capability gap, not a diffusion lag.

The standard economist's answer — new technologies always take decades to diffuse — is an evasion. A genuine human-level model would be radically easier to onboard than a person: it could read your entire Slack and Drive in minutes, inherit the skills of every other copy, and sidestep the lemons problem that makes hiring risky. If the capability existed, firms would buy trillions of dollars of it. That they don't is information about the models, not about organisational inertia.

Either on-the-job learning arrives soon, or AGI doesn't.

The labs are spending enormous sums pre-baking skills into models through mid-training and purpose-built RL environments — teaching them to drive a browser, to build a financial model in Excel. But humans need no such rehearsal for every tool they will ever touch. So one of two things is true: either models will shortly learn on the job by themselves, making all that pre-baking pointless, or they won't — in which case AGI is not close. The labs' own behaviour reveals which they expect.

RL scaling is borrowing credibility that pretraining earned.

Pretraining had something close to a physical law: clean loss improvements across orders of magnitude of compute. Reinforcement learning on verifiable rewards has no publicly known well-fit trend, and where outsiders have reconstructed one from scattered datapoints, the results are discouraging.

Goal-post shifting is the rational response, not a dodge.

Shown Gemini 3 in 2020, he would have been certain it could automate half of knowledge work. It can't. The honest update is that intelligence and labour contain more than we thought — not that the models disappoint. His compressed version: models keep getting more impressive at the rate short-timelines people predict, and more useful at the rate long-timelines people predict.

And then it gets extremely strange.

None of this is pessimism about the destination. When continual learning is solved, AI won't merely match human workers — it will be deployed across the whole economy, learning on every job at once, with every copy able to merge what it learned back into the others. One system effectively learns every job in the world. He expects this within a decade or two, and his own reaction to saying so out loud is unprintable enthusiasm rather than dread — "That's fucking crazy!"

The argument, walked through

In the order he built it, 2023 to 2026. Checks and other views sit under the claim they belong to. Verdict scale: 5/5 primary source · 4/5 corroborated press · 3/5 single credible source · 2/5 aggregator only.

Where he started: an open question, not a position Dec 2023

Will scaling work? was written as a dialogue between a believer and a sceptic, and he declined to fully take a side — landing on roughly 70% that scaling plus algorithmic and hardware progress delivers AGI by 2040. The worry he pressed hardest was data: not enough high-quality text in existence, by orders of magnitude, with self-play and synthetic data as the hoped-for escape. Read from 2026, the striking thing is not the probability estimate but the choice of bottleneck. Data scarcity became the industry's organising problem — RLVR, synthetic pipelines, the entire RL-environment supply chain — three years before it became consensus.

CheckOpen until 2040 C 4/5 — The 70%-by-2040 estimate cannot be graded for another fourteen years. Recorded here because it is the earliest dated position and sets the baseline for everything that follows.

The essay that made the argument: continual learning Jun 2025

"The LLM baseline at many tasks might be higher than an average human's. But there's no way to give a model high level feedback. You're stuck with the abilities you get out of the box."

The case is built from his own failures rather than from theory, which is what gives it force. He had spent over a hundred hours trying to build LLM tools for his post-production workflow — rewriting auto-generated transcripts for readability, pulling tweetable clips, co-writing essays passage by passage. These are short-horizon, language-in-language-out tasks, exactly where LLMs should be strongest. His verdict was 5 out of 10, and he was careful to add that this is still impressive.

The teaching analogy does the heavy lifting. To teach a child saxophone, you have her blow into it, listen, and adjust. Now imagine instead: the student gets one attempt, and the moment she errs you send her away and write detailed notes about what went wrong. The next student reads your notes and tries to play Charlie Parker cold. When she fails, you refine the notes again. No amount of prompt-honing makes that work — and it is the only channel users have for teaching a model anything.

He anticipates the obvious reply. Yes, a session gets smarter as it runs: rewrite a paragraph, tell the model its draft was bad, and its next suggestions improve markedly. But that understanding evaporates when the session ends. Rolling context windows that compact into summaries help in software engineering, which is text-native — he notes that even Claude Code will reverse a hard-won optimisation after a compaction, because the reason for it didn't survive into the summary.

He undercuts himself repeatedly, and the piece would misrepresent him without it. The same essay calls today's LLMs "magical". After cataloguing their failures he stops himself — "enough cold water" — and points at the reasoning traces of o3 and Gemini 2.5 as genuinely reasoning, refusing to be one of those people handed a golden-egg laying goose who complains about the quacking. On the technical objections he flags his own standing plainly: "I'm not an AI researcher, so far be it for me to contradict them." He calls it "hilariously arrogant" to have implied R1 and o1 were easy. And he insists his probability distributions are wide enough that preparing for a misaligned 2028 superintelligence "still makes a lot of sense — I think this is a totally plausible outcome." Filed as a sceptic, he is not one.

Other viewsThis is where the serious disagreement lives, and it is not a two-sided fight — it is a four-way split on what kind of problem this even is. Nathan Lambert (Interconnects, "Contra Dwarkesh on Continual Learning") calls weight-level learning "the ultimate algorithmic nerd-snipe": keep scaling context, memory and retrieval and you get something functionally indistinguishable from continual learning for free — a systems problem, not an algorithms problem. Ryan Greenblatt (Redwood Research) attacks the premise instead: humans also mostly learn by noticing what went well and updating efficiently, so RL is not categorically different — the gaps are robust self-verification and sample efficiency, both quantitative and both improving. Richard Sutton comes from the opposite flank: LLMs are a dead end regardless, and no amount of continual learning fixes an architecture that learns by imitating text rather than from grounded experience. Supporting him: Andrej Karpathy, whose "decade of agents" framing names the same gaps and lands on a 10–15 year timeline, and Beren Millidge, who proposes the hive-mind workaround Dwarkesh later adopts. Zvi Mowshowitz broadly agrees, noting that coding is unusually well-suited to current models while accounting and consulting show little gain despite nominal capability.

The observation holds. The diagnosis — that this is a distinct, hard, years-long problem rather than a scaffolding gap — is genuinely contested by people who work on the systems.

The two dated bets Jun 2025

He committed to numbers, which is rarer than it should be and is what makes the corpus checkable at all. At 50/50 odds: an AI does end-to-end taxes for his small business as well as a competent general manager would — chasing receipts across websites, emailing people for missing invoices, filling the form, filing it — by 2028. And an AI learns on the job as organically as a human, such that a hired AI video editor would after six months understand his preferences and audience the way a person would, by 2032.

He also pre-empted the obvious objection to his own position: having made a great fuss about continual learning being a profound handicap, his timeline still lands on what would at minimum be a broadly deployed intelligence explosion within seven years. He conceded the point rather than dodging it — that is, on his own account, a wild world arriving fast.

CheckBoth open C 2/5 — Neither is resolvable in 2026, and the interim evidence is soft. On taxes: no agent performs multi-week end-to-end tax workflows at general-manager quality. Benchmark scores are hard to read across suites — high marks on OSWorld-Verified sit next to roughly 20% on the harder OSWorld 2.0 — and benchmark performance is not the thing he bet on anyway. On continual learning: no lab has shipped production weight-level on-the-job learning as of mid-2026. Both trends are consistent with his direction, neither settles it. Sources here are aggregator-grade; treat the interim reading as weak.

The forecast he bet against — now nearly resolvable Jun 2025

He named a specific opposing prediction: Anthropic's Sholto Douglas and Trenton Bricken, on his own podcast, expecting reliable computer-use agents by end of 2026 — tell it to do your taxes and it works through your email, orders and Slack and files the return. He said he'd bet against, and gave reasons rather than instinct: longer horizons force longer rollouts on more compute-expensive multimodal input; there is no pretraining corpus for computer use comparable to text; and even conceptually simple algorithmic advances take years to iron out — it was roughly two years, he says, from GPT-4 to o1 for the far simpler idea of training on verifiable maths and code.

CheckNot yet settled — leaning his way C 2/5 — Four months of 2026 remain. Three architectures now compete (Anthropic's computer use, OpenAI's background computer use since April 2026, Google's Gemini computer use), but "reliable" in the sense the forecast meant is not in evidence, and the gap between friendly and hard benchmarks is large. Do not score this as won. Mostly true, rounded up C 5/5 — GPT-4 shipped 14 March 2023 and o1-preview 12 September 2024: about eighteen months, not two years. The rounding runs in the direction of his argument that these things take longer than expected.

The lognormal shape: this decade or bust Jun 2025

The structural claim underneath the timelines. Progress has been driven by scaling training compute at over 4× a year, and that cannot continue past this decade — not for chips, not for power, not as a share of GDP. After 2030, progress must come mostly from algorithms, where the low-hanging fruit will already be picked. So the annual probability of AGI craters. AGI timelines, in his phrase, are very lognormal.

CheckTrue C 5/5 — Epoch AI reports frontier training compute growing 4–5× per year, with a ~4× trend since 2018; no evidence the rate broke in 2025–26. True C 5/5 — The physical ceiling is well documented; Epoch's power-usage trend has training electricity demand roughly doubling annually, which runs into hard limits around 2030.
Other viewsThe premises hold; the cliff shape is contested. Ege Erdil and Tamay Besiroglu (ex-Epoch, now Mechanize) reject it outright — they expect AGI around 2045 and explosive but continuous economic growth with no discrete takeoff moment, and specifically deny that the end of compute scaling implies a sharp probability cliff. From the other side, Daniel Kokotajlo (AI 2027) argued the opposite: once you hit a superhuman coder, automated R&D compresses what would linearly take until 2032 into a fraction of that. Notably, Kokotajlo has since moved his own median from 2028 to roughly 2030 — still inside Dwarkesh's decade, which narrows the gap between them from years to months.

The compute facts are solid. Whether they imply a cliff or a plateau is the actual disagreement, and it is unresolved.

What are we scaling? Dec 2025

"Either these models will soon learn on the job in a self directed way - making all this pre-baking pointless - or they won't - which means AGI is not imminent."

The sharpest form of the argument, and the one that most resembles a trap. He notes he is confused by people who hold short timelines and are bullish on scaling RL atop LLMs — if we were close to a human-like learner, training on verifiable outcomes would be beside the point. Robotics makes it vivid: in some fundamental sense robotics is an algorithms problem, since humans learn to teleoperate existing hardware with almost no training. A human-like learner would largely solve it. That we instead must visit a thousand homes to learn dish-picking and laundry-folding is the evidence.

He then closes the escape routes. To the reply that clumsy RL is merely scaffolding on the way to an automated AI researcher who will then solve robust learning: that has the flavour of losing money on every sale and making it up in volume — an automated researcher cracks a problem humans have failed at for most of a century, while lacking the learning ability of a child. And the labs' own conduct contradicts it: you do not need to pre-bake a consultant's PowerPoint skills in order to automate Ilya Sutskever.

CheckTrue C 3/5 — The Beren Millidge post he quotes ("Most Algorithmic Progress is Data Progress", 2 August 2025) exists and argues what he says it does: much of what is credited as algorithmic progress is data progress, with billions spent on expert-written trajectories — which Millidge reads as evidence the models still lack the core of learning an AGI would need.

Why human labour is valuable: the macrophage story Dec 2025

The best-turned passage in the corpus. At a dinner, a biologist with long timelines is asked what AI would struggle with; she describes examining slides to judge whether a dot is a macrophage or merely resembles one. The AI researcher present replies that image classification is a textbook deep learning problem, easily trained.

Dwarkesh reads the exchange as exposing the crux. Human workers are valuable precisely because nobody has to build a schleppy training loop for each fragment of their job. It is not net-productive to build a custom pipeline for how one particular lab prepares its slides, then another for the next micro-task, and onward forever. Every day involves a hundred small things requiring judgement and context learned on the job — differing between people and between days for the same person. You cannot automate one job by baking in a predefined skill set, let alone all of them.

This is the argument at its strongest: concrete, falsifiable in principle, and not dependent on any timeline being right.

Diffusion lag is "cope" Dec 2025

His most exposed position, and his own word for it: the claim that AI isn't widely deployed because technology takes time to diffuse is, he writes, "cope", glossing over models simply lacking the capabilities. He quotes Steven Byrnes approvingly — ask how skilled, entrepreneurial immigrants integrate into an economy immediately, then note that AGI could do those things too. His own extension: an actual human-level model would diffuse faster than hiring, since it could absorb your Slack and Drive in minutes, inherit other copies' skills, and avoid the lemons problem that makes a bad hire costly. If capabilities were there, firms would spend trillions annually, because knowledge workers collectively earn tens of trillions in wages.

CheckWas right in 2025, decayed since C 5/5 — He put lab revenue "4 orders of magnitude" below the trillions AGI would command. That was defensible in mid-2025, with OpenAI near $10bn. It is no longer: combined frontier-lab run-rate is roughly $70–100bn by mid-2026, with Anthropic reported to have passed OpenAI. Against a trillions-scale target the gap is now closer to 1.5–2 orders of magnitude. The metric he chose has moved against him faster than his thesis predicted — though he can fairly reply that revenue growing 10× while knowledge work remains unautomated is itself evidence for the capability gap.
Other viewsHere he stands nearly alone, and the most interesting opposition is not from economists but from a lab CEO. Erik Brynjolfsson's Productivity J-Curve (with Rock and Syverson) is the rigorous counter: general-purpose technologies show falling then rising measured productivity because the complementary intangible investments — workflow redesign, reskilling, data pipelines — take years and are largely invisible in the statistics. Daron Acemoglu goes further than Dwarkesh in a different direction, estimating roughly 0.55% total productivity gain over a decade on both capability and diffusion grounds. And Dario Amodei — the lab chief with the shortest timelines, who ought to be his ally — contradicted him directly on the podcast: diffusion is real, not cope. The milder version he skips: diffusion lag is well documented for genuine breakthrough technologies too, from electrification onward, so its presence says nothing either way about whether capabilities are missing.

The strongest form of his claim — that a true AGI would diffuse fast — is hard to dispute. The inference from slow diffusion today to missing capability is where the economists have the better of it.

Goal-post shifting, defended Dec 2025

"Models keep getting more impressive at the rate the short timelines people predict, but more useful at the rate the long timelines people predict."

He grants that AI bulls are often right to accuse bears of moving goal posts, then argues some movement is justified. Shown Gemini 3 in 2020 he would have been certain it could automate half of knowledge work. We keep solving what looked like the sufficient bottlenecks — general understanding, few-shot learning, reasoning — and still lack AGI in the sense of automating 95% of knowledge work. The rational response is to conclude that the old definition was too narrow. He then predicts his own future goal-post move: by 2030, real continual-learning progress and hundreds of billions in revenue, and he still won't call it AGI.

CheckOpen until 2030 C 2/5 — Not resolvable. For plausibility only: at a combined run-rate near $70–100bn in mid-2026 and growing, the revenue half of the prediction looks conservative rather than bold. The continual-learning half is the load-bearing part and remains untested.
Other viewsThe formulation is elegant and hard to falsify in practice, since most outcomes can be read as either impressive or useful after the fact. No named critic could be found making that charge — it circulates in comment threads rather than as a stated position, and it would be unfair to attribute it to anyone in particular. Worth noting against himself: Dwarkesh praises François Chollet precisely for refusing moving goal posts and drawing a fixed falsifiable line with ARC-AGI. METR's task-horizon work arms both sides — the doubling time for task length fell from about 7 months to 3–4 through 2024–25, then METR's own early-2026 update expected reversion to the slower trend and warned of test-suite saturation. Meanwhile 16–20 hour task horizons have not produced Fortune 500 transformation.

RL is laundering pretraining's prestige Dec 2025

Pretraining delivered a clean, general improvement in loss across orders of magnitude of compute — as close to a physical law as the field has produced, albeit on a power law, which he notes is as weak as exponential growth is strong. RLVR has no publicly known well-fit trend, and people are borrowing the credibility of the former to justify bullishness about the latter.

CheckTrue C 5/5 — Toby Ord's "How Well Does RL Scale?" does conclude roughly what is attributed to him: extrapolating from o1→o3, something like a 1,000,000× scale-up of total RL compute would be needed for a GPT-level jump. Ord calls it breathtakingly inefficient scaling, and himself flags that the trend cannot be independently verified across labs for lack of published training curves.
Other viewsNathan Lambert disputes the inference rather than the observation: labs do have usable, predictable internal RL scaling curves — they are proprietary and unpublished, which is not the same as non-existent. As of 2026 there are academic attempts at formal RL and hybrid pretraining/RL scaling laws, but nothing with the canonical standing of Kaplan or Chinchilla. On the narrow question — is there a public RL scaling law — Dwarkesh is still right.

Where the bullishness lives: broadly deployed intelligence explosion Jun 2025 · Dec 2025

The half of his position that gets lost when he is filed as a sceptic. Discussions of takeoff focus on software-only or software-plus-hardware singularities; he thinks the main driver atop AGI will be continual learning itself. Agents deployed across the economy do jobs, learn while doing them, and — unlike humans — merge learnings across every copy. One AI effectively learns every job in the world. He puts it plainly: a model capable of online learning might functionally become a superintelligence quite rapidly with no further algorithmic progress at all.

But he declines the dramatic version, and the reasoning is careful. Solving continual learning won't be a single announcement — it will resemble in-context learning, where GPT-3 was a genuine breakthrough that nobody would call solved even now. Labs will ship something they call continual learning that genuinely counts as progress, while human-level capability takes another 5–10 years. So no runaway advantage for whoever gets there first: playing with the feature reveals how it was built, and rivals replicate within months. He expects diminishing returns from deployment too — the first thousand consultant agents learn a great deal, the next thousand less — and suspects it may turn out to be another power law. His stated prior is simply that competition stays fierce, since every previous supposed flywheel has failed to produce a durable lead.

This is the most carefully hedged section in the corpus, and the least quoted.

Where it stands now: labs are discarding the valuable data Jun 2026

The most recent turn, from The next big breakthrough will be AIs learning on the job and its companion The data black hole at the center of AI. The labs' bet is that training on millions of verifiable tasks across thousands of RL environments will produce general problem-solving — persistence through ambiguous multi-week work — the way raw scaling once solved natural language processing. His counter-position is that the genuinely valuable signal is real deployment interaction data, which is not being systematically fed back into training. Sample efficiency, not environment count, is the binding constraint.

Other viewsNo prominent named rebuttal to this specific essay could be located — the question is handled obliquely in Lambert's ongoing RL-environments coverage rather than met head-on. That absence is itself worth recording: the argument is two months old and has not yet been properly tested.

Stepping back

The error direction does not lean the way it usually does. Across the checkable claims, the pattern is mixed rather than self-serving. The one rhetorical rounding — GPT-4 to o1 as "two years" when it was eighteen months — does favour his "things take longer" thesis. But the most consequential decay runs the other way: the revenue gap he cited as four orders of magnitude has closed to roughly 1.5–2, faster than his framing anticipated. He picked a metric that has since embarrassed him, and has not quietly dropped it. That is a mark in favour of the corpus, not against it.

The messenger

Born 2000, started the podcast at university in 2020 as The Lunar Society — after the 18th-century Birmingham club — renaming it when listeners mistook it for a crypto show. Named to the TIME100 Creators list in 2026. His book The Scaling Era: An Oral History of AI, 2019–2025 (Stripe Press, October 2025, edited with Gavin Leech) collects interview material from Amodei, Hassabis, Sutskever, Yudkowsky and Zuckerberg.

The method is the unusual part. He reports one to two weeks of preparation per guest, sometimes implementing technical concepts before discussing them, and has hired personal tutors in economics, hardware and physics. Guests are chosen for what he can learn rather than for profile — his most-listened episode was with the historian Sarah Paine, not a chief executive. His December 2025 strategy document states the ambition without embarrassment: that people will one day look back on this period as we look back on the Enlightenment, and he wants the debates to have happened on the podcast, and to have happened well. The same document explains the pivot toward essays: interviews work best when he brings a thesis for the guest to push against, he believes lab insiders are only months ahead rather than holding deep secrets, and some questions need weeks of research rather than a live exchange.

On his own record he is honest but self-graded. The December 2025 essay walks back his earlier enthusiasm for RL scaling. The October 2025 Sutton follow-up deepens and steelmans a guest's position rather than admitting error. He has claimed his continual-learning essay identified a bottleneck later named publicly by Altman and Hassabis — plausible, but a causal claim graded by its author. From the August 2024 progress update comes the most disarming detail: he was two months from quitting for a startup, twice, before a viral post drew praise from Jeff Bezos.

The venue, and how he is criticised

These essays are self-published on his own Substack — no editor, no adversarial process, and a subscriber base that broadly shares his priors. That is the correct discount to apply. Against it: he publishes named critics' rebuttals in his own comment section, links the people who disagree with him, and interviews them at length.

Criticism of him is overwhelmingly intellectual rather than ethical, which is itself a finding. Lambert disputes the continual-learning frame and accuses him of making the same error as sceptics who deny models "really" reason — treating human intelligence as the necessary standard. Zvi Mowshowitz reviews nearly every major episode and returns to a recurring unease, though the specific complaint proved hard to pin down. Reviews of The Scaling Era praise it while noting it centres corporate and academic elites and deliberately leaves contradictions between experts unresolved. Nobody credible has called him a stenographer for the labs.

What survives

  • The core observation. Models cannot be taught by their users. Nobody serious disputes the phenomenon — the fight is over whether it is architectural, quantitative or a scaffolding gap.
  • The either/or on pre-baking. That the labs' investment in mid-training and RL environments reveals a working assumption that models will keep generalising poorly is a genuinely sharp observation about revealed preference.
  • The macrophage argument. Human labour is valuable because no one has to build a training loop for each fragment of the job. This holds independently of every timeline in the corpus.
  • No public RL scaling law. Still true in 2026, and Ord's analysis backs the specific number.
  • The compute facts. 4×/year, and a ceiling around 2030. Both solid.

What to discount

  • "Diffusion lag is cope." The weakest link, and the one where he stands against Brynjolfsson, Acemoglu and Dario Amodei. His strong form — real AGI would diffuse fast — is fine; the inference from today's slow diffusion to missing capability is not established.
  • The four-orders-of-magnitude gap. Cite the 2026 figure, not his.
  • The lognormal cliff. The compute premises are solid; that they imply a sharp probability cliff rather than a plateau is contested by Erdil and Besiroglu, who know the compute data as well as anyone.
  • "Impressive fast, useful slow." Memorable, and hard to falsify in practice, since most outcomes fit one category or the other after the fact. He is better on this when he praises Chollet for drawing a fixed line instead.
  • Interim readings of his open bets. Benchmark scores are not the thing he wagered on. Wait for 2028.
