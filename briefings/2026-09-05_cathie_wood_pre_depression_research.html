Cathie Wood: the pre-1930s economy may be coming back

On September 4, 2026 — "employment Friday," the Friday before Labor Day — ARK Invest CEO Cathie Wood recorded her monthly 52-minute solo monologue "In The Know." Her frame: technology revolutions change growth regimes, and five are running at once, so the last 125 years of ~3% global growth, the low-inflation post-Depression yield curve, and even the 2008 oil-price ceiling may all be about to break. This piece checks the twenty factual claims she builds that argument on.

1

Her argument in brief

Stated at its strongest — including her own caveats that this is "a work in process" and that she is "posing as many questions as having answers."

For roughly 125 years, global real GDP growth has held near 3%. Before that, across four centuries, it averaged well under 1%. The industrial revolution — three platforms (railroads, telephone/electricity, internal combustion) arriving together — produced a five-fold step up in the growth rate. Today five platforms are arriving together (AI, robotics, energy storage, blockchain, multiomics/life-science technology), so a comparable step-up is plausible: growth could at least double, and on ARK's more aggressive scenarios, run several times higher.

If that regime shift is real, it should show up first in prices, not just growth. New general-purpose technologies are historically deflationary, and before the Federal Reserve matured as an inflation-fighting institution, that deflationary undertow left long-term interest rates below short-term rates most of the time — an inverted yield curve was the normal condition, not a recession warning. A Fed chair who watches gold and hard-money history, rather than only the headline consumer-price index, would read today's data the same way: inflation gauges that strip out noise are already close to target, and technology-driven disinflation (in compute, in energy, in gene sequencing) is about to intensify. Layer in an oil-supply glut as OPEC members compete rather than cooperate, and the case is that inflation could surprise not just lower, but negative.

None of this requires today's federal debt to be alarming: measured against the private economy's equity value rather than only against GDP — a comparison she herself calls "not apples to apples" but a useful point of view — the debt burden is falling, not rising, because AI-era wealth creation is outrunning it. And the capital pouring into AI compute is not the railroad mania of the 1800s, where speculative capital chased a hoped-for future — AI infrastructure is generating revenue and high returns on invested capital today, in real time, which her own account of Elon Musk's compute build argues is happening faster and cheaper than the largest AI labs are paying for equivalent capacity.

The argument, walked through

The five load-bearing claims, in the order she builds them. Verdict scale: 5/5 primary source · 4/5 two independent sources · 3/5 single credible source · 2/5 aggregator only.

1 · 125 years of ~3% growth is ending 2026

"We believe that growth rate is going to at least double" — Cathie Wood, In The Know

She anchors the whole episode in a Brett Winton (ARK chief futurist) chart running back to 100,000 BC — by her own admission "very rough estimates" — showing ~0.6% average growth for four centuries before 1500-1900, a two-fold lift from the printing press and early trade, then a five-fold lift to today's ~3% from the industrial revolution's three platforms. Her forecast: at least a doubling within five years, calling that "quite a conservative estimate," and she notes Elon Musk is "starting to use 10 to 15%."

CheckMostly true C 3/5 — the historical shape is directionally right: Maddison Project/Our World in Data estimates put pre-1820 global growth well under 1% and 20th-century growth near 3%, though the clean 400-year/125-year split is not a standard academic figure. True C 5/5 — the IMF's own 3.1% figure (below) is accurate. Outlier forecast — the doubling-to-15% claim itself is where she stands alone: Acemoglu's own modelling puts AI's cumulative GDP effect near 1.1%, Pissarides sees at most 0.5 percentage points of added growth per decade, and Goldman Sachs sits in a $7 trillion middle case. No mainstream forecaster is near 6%, let alone 15%.
Other viewsDaron Acemoglu (MIT, NBER) and Christopher Pissarides (LSE) both model AI's macro growth effect as modest over a decade; Goldman Sachs's aggregate estimate sits well below ARK's; the IMF, whose own 3.1% figure she cites approvingly, has not revised its outlook upward despite the AI buildout she says should already be visible in the data.

The history she cites checks out; the extrapolation from it does not have company.

2 · Before the Depression, an inverted curve was normal pre-1930s

"More often than not the yield curve was inverted, meaning long rates were below short rates"

This is the episode's title claim. She argues that before the Fed matured as an institution (created 1913, but the pattern she describes runs through the 1930s), the US yield curve was inverted "more than 50%, I think it was more than 60% of the time," with an average inversion of "roughly 100 basis points" — because early industrial-era deflation pulled long rates down even as growth pulled short rates up. She reads today's flattening curve as the start of the same pattern, not a recession signal, "because real growth is accelerating... but inflation is likely to come down."

CheckMostly true C 3/5 — the historical core holds up: NBER macrohistory research on 19th-century term structure finds short-term rates exceeded long-term rates on average before 1930, and the curve ran inverted for roughly 700 consecutive days heading into 1929. Her specific figures — the ">50-60% of the time" and "~100bp average" — could not be independently confirmed from a single named source; treat them as a directionally plausible but unverified point estimate.
Other viewsNo named economist has publicly endorsed or challenged the specific pre-Depression-analogy framing; it appears to be ARK's own construction. Worth noting on its own: the same historical record she cites contains both the 1920-21 depression and the 1929 crash — inversions preceded both. "Benign" is her interpretation of the pattern, not a feature of the historical record itself.

The fact pattern is real; whether an inverted curve was ever actually benign, rather than merely common, is a separate and unresolved question.

3 · The oil glut: Abu Dhabi's exit and "$30 oil" May 2026 –

"its production is up 78% since then to a record a little over 4 million barrels per day"

She points to Abu Dhabi's exit from OPEC as evidence Gulf producers now see peak oil demand — a claim she deliberately leaves loose ("the demand for oil is peaking, has peaked, is in the process of peaking, however you want to say that") — and are racing to monetize reserves before the price falls, with Venezuela "threatening" to follow. She adds that the US is pumping "13.6 million barrels a day" and "exporting over 6 million of those," versus "almost nothing" in 2015. Her scenario, stated as a hedge rather than a forecast: "we would not be surprised to see the oil price drop back to $30."

CheckTrue C 4/5 — the UAE's OPEC exit was effective May 1, 2026, and its June output hit a record 4.1 million barrels/day, up roughly 7-9% versus its prior average. The "78%" in the transcript is almost certainly an ASR mis-hearing of "7 to 8%," not a real figure. True C 5/5 — US production of 13.6M bpd is accurate. Wrong figure C 5/5 — US crude exports set a record of 5.6M bpd (April 2026), not ">6 million"; the 13.6M figure is total production including refined products, likely the source of the mix-up. Unsupported — no forecaster found bridges to $30: JPMorgan's year-end call is $78, the EIA projects ~$69 by 2027; the IEA sees demand plateauing around 2029-30 while OPEC's Secretary-General publicly rejects the idea of peak demand outright.
Other viewsIEA (demand plateau ~2029-30) is the closest support for her directional call; OPEC's al-Ghais explicitly disputes it, forecasting demand near 123M bpd by 2050. Venezuela's threatened OPEC exit could not be corroborated in press coverage as of this writing.

The supply-glut mechanics are real and understated rather than overstated on exports; the $30 price target is her own scenario, and she frames it as one — "we would not be surprised" — not a forecast anyone else shares.

4 · Warsh, gold, and the inflation Fed chairs choose to watch Jan – Aug 2026

"the gold price peaked on the day President Trump nominated him"

Kevin Warsh, as Fed chairman, gave a Jackson Hole address she calls "quite illuminating," focused on headline PCE inflation at 3.7% — high by the last 30 years' standards, with a 2% target. She argues this overstates his real concern, because his congressional testimony instead cited the Dallas Fed's trimmed-mean PCE at 2.3%, "very close to the 2%," and a private inflation gauge near 2.4% headline / 1.3% core — evidence, in her reading, that "he probably has his eye on true inflation" rather than the noisy headline print. She ties his hard-money instincts to gold's price action, which she says "peaked on the day President Trump nominated him," and expects gold to "stabilize... maybe go down" as the dollar strengthens — hedged immediately with "jury's out on that... we're going to be in a wait-and-see mode."

CheckTrue C 5/5 — Warsh's August 28 Jackson Hole speech did headline 3.7% y/y PCE (4.1% on a six-month annualized basis); notably, the speech itself was hawkish and floated further rate hikes — the opposite of a disinflation read, delivered just six days before this episode. Mostly true C 3/5 — Dallas Fed trimmed-mean PCE was 2.3% for the twelve months to July 2026, matching her figure. Mostly true C 3/5 — gold's all-time high (~$5,594) fell on January 29, the flash-crash news of Warsh's nomination broke January 30 (gold down 15-16% over 48 hours, its worst drop since the early 1980s), and the formal nomination came March 4 — three distinct dates the episode compresses into one. The aftermath is contested: one outlet reports gold later rebounding to fresh highs, but the price levels cited across sources conflict, so this is shown as an open conflict rather than resolved.
Other viewsTorsten Slok (Apollo) treats stagflation, not disinflation, as his 2026 base case, reserving a deflation scenario for an AI-success case starting 2027 or later — a genuine split with Wood's timing, not just her magnitude. Warsh's own hawkish Jackson Hole tone, delivered six days before this episode aired, sits in tension with her "he's really watching a lower number" read of him.

Every individual data point checks out; stitching three separate gold-price dates into "the day he was nominated," and reading a hawkish speech as secretly dovish, are both her interpretive choices layered on top of accurate figures.

5 · AI capex is not a railroad bubble 2026

"I think uh Anthropic agreed to pay 50 billion dollars per gigawatt"

She distinguishes today's AI buildout from the 1800s, when "so much capital chasing the rail opportunity" left roughly 200 railroads bankrupt on a hope that revenue would eventually follow. AI is different, she argues, because "the returns on invested capital in this space are huge today" — pointing to Elon Musk's data centers turning "from huge losses to massive profits," and citing a cost comparison: Anthropic paying "50 billion dollars per gigawatt" for compute versus Musk's team building at "somewhere in the mid to high 20[billions]," which she reads as an immediate return advantage for Musk. She backs the "AI creates jobs" side of that argument with September 4's employment report and a Ramp survey showing AI-heavy adopters growing headcount faster than peers.

CheckWrong figure / conflation C 4/5 — this is the run's largest correction. The real xAI compute deal reported in the press is $1.25 billion/month through May 2029 (roughly $40 billion-plus total) for about 325,000 GPUs and 300+ megawatts of Colossus capacity — not a "$50 billion per gigawatt" price. The $50 billion figure belongs to a separate Anthropic commitment to US compute infrastructure (in the context of a Google/Broadcom TPU expansion), not to a per-gigawatt price and not to xAI at all. No source was found for xAI's own build cost in "mid-to-high $20 billions per gigawatt." True C 5/5 — the September 4 headline jobs numbers check out exactly as stated: +162,000 nonfarm payrolls versus a roughly 53,000 consensus, unemployment steady at 4.1%, average hourly earnings +3.1%, participation edging up. Unverifiable — the "+450,000" household-survey figure could not be corroborated in release coverage. Wrong direction C 4/5 — the 16-24 youth unemployment rate she cites as rising from 8.5% to 9.1% is in fact 9.1% as of July 2026, down from 10.8% a year earlier; she has the level roughly right and the direction backwards.
Other viewsMichael Burry has publicly attacked AI-capex accounting (depreciation schedules, circular vendor financing) as inflating reported returns; MIT's NANDA project found 95% of enterprise AI pilots show no P&L return; Satya Nadella (Microsoft) counters with a Jevons-paradox argument that falling compute costs expand demand rather than signal a bubble. None of the three critiques addresses the other two, and none of them engages her specific per-gigawatt comparison, which independent reporting does not support as stated. Ramp's own researchers caution their AI-adopter headcount finding is correlation, not causation.

Her jobs evidence is largely solid and one data point is backwards; her signature AI-capex comparison — the one meant to prove the deals are cheap and rational — rests on a specific dollar figure that does not describe the company or the pricing structure she attributes it to.

Where it's contested

The named opposition to this argument clusters in four places, and no single critic engages the whole thesis.

Growth. Daron Acemoglu (MIT/NBER) and Christopher Pissarides (LSE) both publish AI growth-effect estimates far below hers — cumulative GDP impact near 1% versus her 2x-to-5x regime shift — with Goldman Sachs occupying a wide middle ground. ARK sits alone at the high end of any published spectrum.

Inflation. Torsten Slok of Apollo's base case for 2026 is stagflation, not disinflation; he reserves an AI-driven deflation scenario for 2027 or later, contingent on AI actually succeeding at scale. Warsh's own Jackson Hole tone — floating rate hikes six days before this episode — reads hawkish, not like a chairman quietly targeting a lower inflation number.

Oil. Nobody in the reporting reviewed supports a $30 price: JPMorgan ($78 year-end), the EIA (~$69 by 2027) and the IEA (demand plateauing ~2029-30) are all well above it, and OPEC Secretary-General Haitham al-Ghais rejects the idea of peak demand outright, forecasting continued growth to roughly 123 million bpd by 2050.

AI capex. Three critiques operate on different layers and none rebuts the others: Michael Burry targets the accounting (depreciation, circular vendor deals); MIT's NANDA project targets realized enterprise ROI (95% of pilots show none); Satya Nadella defends the spending on Jevons-paradox grounds (cheaper compute expands total demand). Wood's specific per-gigawatt cost comparison — the concrete evidence she offers that the deals are rational — is not supported by independent reporting on the companies named.

Jobs. Ramp Economics Lab's own study of 21,500 firms, which she cites to argue AI creates jobs, is explicitly flagged by its authors as showing correlation between AI adoption and headcount growth, not causation.

Bitcoin and gold. She flags this call's fragility herself in real time — "Famous last words. I don't want to be the famous last words here" — before making it. Her February 2026 version of this same call — sell gold, buy bitcoin — aged badly in the months that followed: gold went on to a fresh record and bitcoin logged three consecutive red quarters (TheStreet). And the specific mechanism she leans on here — "the correlation between Bitcoin and gold... is very low" — is currently contradicted by the data: the 90-day rolling correlation between the two assets sits around 0.86.

Stepping back

The error direction in this run. Reading the verdicts above rather than assuming a pattern: most of the wrong or unsupported figures exaggerate in the direction that favors her thesis — "$50 billion per gigawatt" inflates the AI-returns story, ">6 million bpd" of exports inflates the supply-glut story, "99.99% per year" inference-cost deflation (see scorecard) overstates even ARK's own published figure, and the youth-unemployment direction is flipped from worsening to improving. The one exception cuts the other way: understating GDP at "$30 trillion" makes the debt-to-GDP ratio she is arguing is not-that-bad look somewhat worse than it is. One run is not a base rate, but the pattern is worth naming.

The messenger

ARK Invest earns roughly 0.75% in fees on assets under management concentrated in long-duration, high-growth-multiple strategies — funds that benefit directly from a narrative in which growth accelerates and inflation, and therefore discount rates, fall. This episode aired on ARK's own YouTube channel, unmoderated and without a counter-voice. It is also part of a pattern: her February 2026 gold-to-bitcoin call on the same channel did not hold up over the following two quarters.

Track record, both ways

Her Tesla $4,000 (pre-split) 2018 target was hit in January 2021. Her 2014 Nvidia buy was directionally right, but ARK fully exited the position in January 2023, before the AI-driven surge, forgoing over $1 billion in gains by industry estimates. Her 2020 "$12 oil" call was wrong (a supply shock she has acknowledged). Her 2021-22 disinflation call was wrong near-term and only partially vindicated by late 2023. She does sometimes revise down: ARK cut its bitcoin price target from $1.5 million to $1.2 million in November 2025. ARKK's five-year annualized return is about -7.5% against the S&P 500's +11.2%; its ten-year return is roughly in line with the index, and 2025 was a strong year for the fund by Morningstar's ranking even as mid-2026 brought the outflows above. Morningstar has separately downgraded ARK's fund-rating process (Neutral to Negative) citing concentration risk and team depth.

What survives

  • The historical yield-curve fact. Pre-Depression America really did run an inverted curve most of the time, for reasons plausibly tied to early industrial-era deflation.
  • The oil-supply-glut mechanics. The UAE's OPEC exit and its production surge are real and understated on the export side, not exaggerated.
  • The September jobs beat. Headline payrolls, unemployment, and wage growth all check out as stated.
  • Her directional record on tech-driven deflation. Genome-sequencing and general AI-cost declines are real, large, and ongoing, even where her specific numbers overshoot.

What to discount

  • The specific magnitudes. A 2x-to-5x growth acceleration, $30 oil, "$50 billion per gigawatt," and outright negative inflation are all scenarios she states with hedges ("we would not be surprised," "jury's out") that do not appear in any other forecaster's numbers reviewed here.
  • Venue and incentive. This is ARK's own channel, unmoderated, delivered by a fee-earning asset manager whose flagship funds are structurally long the story she is telling.
  • The gold-and-Warsh timeline. Real events, compressed into a cleaner story than the record supports, with a contested aftermath.
