historyBackground: where things stood on 1 October
  • Tesla: The National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (the US federal road-safety regulator) had ordered Tesla on 10 September to answer 21 questions under oath about how it decided the Cybercab, a driverless taxi without steering wheel or pedals, complies with US safety rules. Earlier reports said the 30 September deadline passed without a reply; in fact it had been extended (see below). The Roadster sports-car reveal had been postponed.

  • Legal: X Corp and xAI had dropped their claim that Apple unfairly shut out competitors, and were still suing OpenAI (maker of ChatGPT), with a trial reported for 11 January 2027. Musk's own suit against OpenAI's chief executive Sam Altman sat on appeal. xAI had asked an appeals court to pause a Minnesota law on AI-generated sexual images; no ruling had come.
  • Other ventures: The Boring Company had raised $3 billion from investors, led by the United Arab Emirates, in a deal valuing it at $23 billion. Neuralink and X Money (the payments service on X) had no confirmed developments.

TESLAResults for July–SeptemberFirst reported 21 Oct 2026

Tesla's profit fell by 43% on flat sales as spending on AI and factories more than doubled

Tesla reported July–September revenue of $28.0 billion, about the same as a year earlier, while profit as officially reported fell to $787 million. The company spent far more on new products: robots, the Cybercab, trucks and AI computing. It gave no numeric forecast for deliveries or earnings.

The business that is growing is not cars: subscriptions to its paid driver-assistance software (Full Self-Driving) rose by two thirds, and the services line grew by a third, while vehicle revenue and battery-storage revenue both fell. Tesla says its Cybercab (driverless taxi) service began carrying passengers in Austin on 3 September.

Context & background. Tesla sold roughly the same value of goods as last year but earned far less on it. The gross margin (the share of sales left after the direct cost of making the product) actually rose to 19.6% from 18.0% a year earlier, so the squeeze came further down: overheads such as research and staff rose 39% to $4.8 billion. That is the cost of the shift Tesla has been describing since last year, from selling cars to selling driving software, robots and energy equipment.

The details. Car revenue was $20.6 billion, down 3%. Energy generation and storage revenue was $2.9 billion, down 16%, although battery-storage installations rose 10%. Tesla used to earn money by selling pollution-rule credits to other carmakers; that income fell from $417 million to $113 million. Services and other revenue rose 33% to $4.6 billion. Cash from operations was $6.3 billion; after $4.7 billion spent on factories and equipment, $1.5 billion remained. Tesla also states that its driver-assistance software, which still needs a human watching, is in over 75% of new cars delivered in North America; that its driverless taxis have driven nearly two million miles in total with nobody at the wheel; that its humanoid robot, Optimus, has started to be built on its main production lines in Fremont, California; and that its electric truck, the Semi, is in volume production in Nevada. These figures come from news sites quoting Tesla.

Why it still matters. The numbers suggest the price investors put on Tesla now depends mostly on software and robots rather than the car business, which is shrinking slightly while costs climb. A quarter with driverless rides in service gives a figure to follow, but the revenue from it is not reported separately.

Watch items. Cybercab regulatory answers (next card); whether Tesla's robot and electric truck (Semi) start bringing in sales in October–December.

TESLARegulator deadlineDue 30 Oct 2026

Tesla's sworn answers on its driverless taxi are due tomorrow, after a 30-day extension

The deadline for Tesla to answer the regulator's questions about its driverless taxi, the Cybercab, was extended to 30 October; earlier reports that it passed on 30 September without a reply were wrong. Tesla asked on 18 September for eight weeks, until 20 November, and the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (the US federal road-safety regulator) gave it 30 days instead.

The regulator has not found that the Cybercab breaks any rule; the question is how Tesla decided, on its own judgement, that a car with no foot brake complies with the federal braking rules.

Context & background. The order is a formal demand to answer questions under oath, signed on 10 September by the regulator's chief counsel, Peter Simshauser, after the regulator opened a review on 3 September, the day Tesla began paid Cybercab service in Austin. US carmakers declare for themselves that their cars meet safety rules; the government does not approve each model in advance. The order says a vehicle without a foot-operated brake "could not certify" to the federal braking rule (standard 135) without special permission (an exemption).

The details. According to Teslanorth, the regulator granted the extension on 22 September and recorded it in a memo dated 29 September, which does not say why 30 days rather than eight weeks. An officer of Tesla must sign a sworn statement that the answers are complete; lying in a sworn statement can be a crime. Nothing in the order suspends service, and Tesla added a Cybercab to the Austin fleet on 21 September.

Why it still matters. Some reports treated the deadline as having lapsed; the extension memo shows it had not. What matters now is whether the answers are filed and whether Tesla discloses them. A sworn description of how the company decided the car complies with the rules would be the first detailed public account.

Watch items. Whether Tesla or the regulator says anything on or after 30 October; whether the regulator opens a formal investigation or Tesla seeks special permission (an exemption).

TESLAProduct reveal15 Oct 2026

Tesla's new Roadster hopped and flipped on air jets in a staged demonstration, with production promised for 2028

On 15 October Musk showed the long-delayed Roadster, a two-seat sports car, at a SpaceX-owned test site in Texas. With no driver aboard, it climbed a ramp, flipped in the air and landed, and cleared a row of parked Teslas using bursts of compressed air from nozzles. Musk claimed it reaches 60 miles per hour (about 97 km/h) in under one second.

It did not fly in any sustained sense, no price was given, and Musk said production would start in 2028.

Context & background. The Roadster was first shown in 2017 with deliveries promised for 2020. The reveal had been postponed from an earlier date; the Federal Aviation Administration (the US aviation regulator) had closed airspace near Waco, Texas, for the event, so people expected it to fly.

The details. The demonstration was staged: Musk said no one rode in the car "to make sure we don't kill anyone", warned that "success is not guaranteed" and kept the team at a distance. A Smithsonian curator quoted in the report said a hybrid of car and aircraft is likely to do both badly and that what was shown amounts to "hopping" rather than sustained flight. Musk also said an audience was needed "to prove that it's not AI", meaning not computer-generated. Tesla's quarterly report lists the unveiling but gives no further engineering data.

Why it still matters. The demonstration took place at a SpaceX-owned test site, in the same week the two companies' boards began considering a merger. Musk's past timelines have often slipped by years, so the 2028 date is a hope, not a promise.

Watch items. Any price or booking terms; any regulator comment on a road vehicle with air jets.

SpaceX's AI division now brings in more than half its revenue and takes most of the money it invests

SpaceX, whose shares trade on the stock market, reported July–September revenue of $14.6 billion, up 161% on a year earlier, and a profit of $4.4 billion. Its AI division, which includes Grok, its chatbot, brought in $7.56 billion of that and, according to the company, took $16.4 billion of the $19.6 billion SpaceX invested in computers and data centres.

It gave no numeric forecast. The release does not mention a merger with Tesla.

Context & background. SpaceX acquired xAI in February 2026, and its shares trade on the Nasdaq stock exchange in New York. The report shows how much of the group is AI, and how much money that work uses. SpaceX calls its AI division "Super Intelligence" in the release.

The details. Starlink, the satellite-internet service, grew from 7.2 to 13.6 million consumer subscribers, paying on average $69 a month. SPACEXMerger talks19–20 Oct 2026

Tesla and SpaceX boards each set up a group of independent directors to consider combining the companies

The boards of SpaceX and Tesla have each set up a special committee of board members with no stake in the deal to study a possible merger, Musk said on SpaceX's results call on 20 October. He said the committees were formed at shareholders' request and that he cannot take part because "I'm a conflicted party", meaning he has a personal interest on both sides.

No terms have been agreed. Musk said there is "a lot of merit" in a combination but that "the shareholders have to be supportive".

Context & background. Musk cited artificial-intelligence software, robotics, plans to put computer centres in space (Starmind) and a joint chip factory (Terafab) as reasons for a combination. A company in which the chief executive sits on both sides of a deal normally hands the negotiation to board members with no stake in the outcome; that is what the committees are for.

The details. TechCrunch reports that Tesla's seven independent directors (named in a 19 October document filed for its shareholders) include its chair, Robyn Denholm, and that SpaceX has six; it does not say who sits on the committees. One director, Ira Ehrenpreis, sits on both boards. Antonio Gracias, a SpaceX director not counted as independent, runs Valor Equity Partners, which owns 3.4% of SpaceX and financed SpaceX's purchase of AI computers, which SpaceX then rents. Musk controls more than half the votes at SpaceX; at Tesla, a majority of all shareholders must approve, not just those who vote, which Musk himself called a much bigger hurdle.

Why it still matters. A merger would join a profitable, AI-heavy space-and-satellite group to a car, energy and robot company whose profit fell by almost half this quarter. Open questions include how many Tesla shares each SpaceX share would be worth and whether the directors are truly independent. Forming committees is a procedural step, not a decision.

Watch items. Names of committee members; any proposed terms or a timetable; shareholders suing over conflicts of interest.

On 19 October Musk posted on X: "Next year, I will give one of my SpaceX shares to every kid in America. This will be the first of many." That is roughly 72 million children; at SpaceX's share price of $146 at the end of trading on Friday 16 October the shares would be worth about $10.5 billion, according to the Deseret News.

He has not said how it would work, when it starts or what age counts as a child; children cannot hold shares directly.

Context & background. The details. Why it still matters. It is unclear how a gift this size would be taxed or reported. Until a mechanism exists, it is a promise.

SPACEXLaunch planTarget: Nov 2026

Starship Flight 15 is targeted for November and would try to catch both stages with the launch tower

Musk said on the 20 October results call that the next flight of Starship, SpaceX's giant rocket, would be in November and aim to catch both parts of the rocket, the booster and the upper ship, in the launch tower's giant mechanical arms. No day has been announced, and the Federal Aviation Administration (the US aviation regulator) has not yet issued the licence.

Gwynne Shotwell said SpaceX expects the licence to cover tower recovery of both stages and says the regulator has been helpful.

Context & background. Catching the booster with the tower's arms (the "chopsticks") has already been done; the ship is harder because it returns from orbital speed and must reach the arms after a final engine burn. SpaceX says the Flight 14 satellites can send about ten times as much data to Earth as a launch of its older rocket, Falcon 9, carrying earlier Starlink satellites.

The details. SpaceX's own results confirm Flight 14 as the first orbital Starship flight and its first that carried paying cargo. The November date and the catch plan come from one news site's account of the call, and so far only that site reports them. An animation of the sequence was shown on the day, not a demonstration.

Why it still matters. Reusing the top part of the rocket is what could make it cheap enough for the satellite and space-computing plans to make economic sense. A delay would be normal: earlier Starship dates moved four times before Flight 14.

Watch items. The aviation licence; a launch date; whether the ship catch is attempted on this flight or deferred.

edit_noteEditor's view

Musk's companies are moving closer together

1. The AI division is the group. SpaceX's results show its AI division is the largest line of revenue and takes most of the investment. Judge the Musk companies by the AI division, not by rockets or cars.

2. Merger talks are at an early formal stage. Committees exist, but no price or terms have been agreed. The barrier is Tesla's vote, which Musk himself called a much bigger hurdle.

3. Tesla's profit story and its product story are diverging. Profit fell 43% while the Roadster, the Optimus robot and the Cybercab dominated the news. The test of the Cybercab is what the sworn documents show, due tomorrow.

4. The Cybercab deadline was extended. Earlier reports that it had passed were wrong; 30 October is the date to watch.

5. Promises cost nothing; a plan for delivering them does. Both the share promise and the Roadster's 2028 date have no mechanism or price yet.

Still to come: the Cybercab answers, the midterm result and any Starship Flight 15 licence.
