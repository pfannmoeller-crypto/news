Geopolitics · 18–24 September 2026

Iran's pause tops two weeks as Washington and Tehran talk for the first time since June

The 19 September edition closed with an unannounced, single-source nine-day pause in Iran's war (which began when the United States struck Iran on 28 February 2026), a Russian parliamentary vote still being counted, a United Nations fact-finding report freshly published, and a NATO (North Atlantic Treaty Organisation) drone shoot-down whose origin was still preliminary. This window moves all four forward. Iran's pause on strikes against neighboring states reached fourteen days without a ballistic missile launch and eighteen without a drone attack by the tracker this briefing has used since 5 September — and, more concretely, US and Iranian officials sat down together on the sidelines of the United Nations General Assembly for the first publicly acknowledged meeting between the two governments since June, even as President Trump told the Assembly Iran faces a choice between a deal and "annihilating" the Islamic Republic and Iran's military command answered with a threat of its own. Russia's Central Election Commission — a state body, not an independent one — certified a record supermajority for the ruling party in a vote European Union officials called a "veneer of democracy." Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky and Trump floated a narrower, energy-infrastructure-only ceasefire after meeting in New York — a proposal Russia's own posture, according to the Institute for the Study of War, a Washington think tank that tracks the war daily, had already rejected in comparable form days earlier. And Lithuania's shot-down drone now has a name and a likely origin, while Poland logged a second brief airspace violation near Kaliningrad this week. Seven cards, gaps named where they remain.

Ukraine / Russia — a narrower ceasefire offer meets a certified supermajority

2 items
Diplomacy22 Sep

Zelensky offers Russia an energy ceasefire after meeting Trump, but Moscow had already rejected a similar plan

Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky met US President Donald Trump for roughly 40 minutes on the sidelines of the United Nations General Assembly in New York on 22 September and said afterward that Ukraine was "ready for any kind of format of energy ceasefire," provided Russia stops striking Ukraine's energy system in return, according to the Kyiv Independent and Al Jazeera. Zelensky named Trump's effort to bring President Vladimir Putin to a possible in-person meeting — floated for the Group of 20 (G20) summit in Miami — as "the most important thing" still standing between the war and a settlement, and said he hoped fighting could end "before winter."

The offer landed awkwardly. The Institute for the Study of War's assessment for the same period reports that Kremlin officials had already rejected a comparable, narrower proposal for a ceasefire limited to energy-infrastructure strikes, while restating that Russia is not willing to engage in genuine peace negotiations. Trump, for his part, focused on the economic angle, telling reporters that Ukrainian strikes on Russian oil refineries were pushing up global diesel prices ahead of November's US midterm elections; he declined to describe what a wider deal would involve, saying only "I think we're gonna make a deal, and I don't want to say what the deal is."

Context & background. The 19 September edition tracked the shuttle diplomacy — separate trips to each side rather than joint talks — of Steve Witkoff, Trump's special envoy for the Middle East, and Jared Kushner, Trump's son-in-law and a senior White House adviser, toward a targeted October trilateral round between the United States, Russia and Ukraine. This meeting is the first public Trump–Zelensky exchange since then, and it introduces a narrower proposal — a ceasefire on energy-infrastructure strikes specifically, not a general halt to fighting — rather than confirming the trilateral round itself.

The details. Zelensky was explicit that his offer is conditional and bilateral, not a unilateral Ukrainian halt: "If they don't want us to attack their diesel and refineries, and etc, we are ready — if they will not attack our energy system." The Kremlin's position is not a flat no: Al Jazeera reports Russia had previously welcomed an earlier US-proposed energy moratorium but wants it to also cover Russian seaborne energy exports and to come with sanctions relief attached, while the Institute for the Study of War separately describes Kremlin officials rejecting a comparable ceasefire proposal in the same window and reiterating there is no meaningful appetite for peace talks. Those two readings are not strictly contradictory — Moscow may be rejecting this specific, unadorned version while holding out for a broader package — but this briefing found no single account reconciling them. Separately, after meeting UK Prime Minister Andy Burnham the same day, Zelensky warned Russia was "preparing a new massive attack against Ukraine" and rejected any discussion of easing sanctions while the threat continues.

Why it still matters. An offer floated in a press conference and a rejection logged by a battlefield tracker are not describing the same negotiation in real time, and that gap is itself the story: both governments are managing public expectations ahead of the US midterm elections and a possible G20 summit without having actually aligned on terms. What would change this: a direct, on-record Russian response to the 22 September proposal, or a confirmed date for the Trump–Putin–Zelensky meeting.

Russian politics18–21 Sep

United Russia certifies a record wartime supermajority as the EU calls the vote a "veneer of democracy"

Russia's Central Election Commission declared final results on 21 September for the three-day State Duma vote the 19 September edition had left uncounted: the ruling United Russia party took just under 58% of the vote and 355 of the chamber's 450 seats, a record for the party, according to Radio Free Europe/Radio Liberty and a separate account from CNN (Cable News Network). Official turnout was 59.32%, up from 51.7% in the last such election in 2021. Four Kremlin-aligned parties — the Communist Party, the Liberal Democratic Party, A Just Russia and New People — took the remaining seats.

Yabloko, the only registered party opposed to the war, had already been barred from the ballot before voting began. European Union foreign policy chief Kaja Kallas said the result showed "the veneer of democracy is all that remains in Russia" and separately raised concern about voting having taken place in Russian-occupied Ukrainian territory. In Chechnya, officials reported 99.85% support for the list led by regional leader Ramzan Kadyrov, a margin Radio Free Europe/Radio Liberty's report described critics as calling implausible; Russia's own independent election monitor, Golos, had already shut down in 2025 and no international observers were invited to watch the count.

Iran / Hormuz — a two-week pause, a first meeting, and a stalled accountability track

3 items
De-escalation17–23 Sep

Iran's pause tops two weeks as US and Iranian officials meet for the first time since June

Iran's pause on strikes against neighboring states — nine days old and single-sourced when the 19 September edition closed — had reached fourteen consecutive days without a ballistic missile launch and eighteen without a drone attack by the 23 September cutoff, according to the day-by-day campaign tracker maintained by GlobalSecurity.org, which this briefing has cross-checked since 5 September. The pause survived a Russian and Chinese veto — a blocking vote only the five permanent Security Council members can cast — of a US-backed United Nations Security Council resolution on 17 September and a General Assembly speech by President Trump on 22 September in which he told Iran it faces a choice between a deal that lets it "rebuild" and "annihilating" the Islamic Republic, adding "we will get it done one way or another." Iran's General Staff of the Armed Forces answered within hours, calling the remarks a sign of "strategic weakness" and vowing "more severe, crushing, and unpredictable blows" in response to any further attack — a conditional threat, the tracker notes, not a resumption of strikes.

The rhetoric came alongside real contact: US special envoy Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner met Iranian Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi for roughly three hours on the sidelines of the Assembly on 22 September — the first publicly acknowledged meeting between the two governments since June, per the tracker. President Trump called it "a very good meeting, a very productive meeting" with another round expected soon. Iran's asking price hardened rather than softened: an Iranian military spokesman told Agence France-Presse that Tehran now wants the full $24 billion in Iranian assets frozen under Western sanctions released in a single instalment up front, plus an end to Saudi Arabia's blockade of areas of Yemen held by the Houthis, an Iran-aligned armed movement, before substantive talks resume — a new condition not on the table in earlier rounds.

Why it still matters. A pause that has now run twice as long as when this briefing first flagged it, paired with the first direct US–Iran contact in three months, is the strongest evidence yet that both governments see more value in managed de-escalation than in a third strike wave — but a public "annihilate"-or-deal ultimatum and a public "crushing blows" threat, issued within hours of each other, show neither side has actually softened its terms. What would change this reading: a resumption of strikes, a confirmed second round of talks, or either government moving off the asset-release and Yemen conditions now on record.

Hormuz shipping20–23 Sep

Hormuz traffic hits the campaign's lowest recorded count as Brent crude falls back under $100

Shipping through the Strait of Hormuz — the narrow shipping channel most of the world's seaborne oil passes through — fell to the lowest level GlobalSecurity.org's tracker has recorded since the war began: the International Monetary Fund's PortWatch project logged a single commercial transit on 20 September, against a pre-crisis baseline of roughly 85 vessels a day, while Reuters and the trade outlet BusinessLine separately reported traffic running below the strait's own ten-day average. An independent monitor counted 396 vessels holding position away from berth inside the strait and Gulf watch box — the zone trackers monitor for ships waiting near the strait — as of 23 September — ships waiting rather than transiting. Set against that, Bloomberg reported Middle East oil flows averaging 17.1 million barrels a day over the same ten days, a figure the tracker notes is unrevised and not obviously consistent with a strait running at a single transit a day.

Brent crude oil (the international oil price benchmark), which the 19 September edition put at roughly $106 a barrel, fell below $100 on 22 September to a two-week low of $98.80 — a fifth consecutive session (trading day) of declines, the longest losing streak since the strait's traffic first collapsed — after Saudi Arabia restarted its East-West pipeline, shut since 13 September following drone attacks, and prepared to resume exports from the port of Yanbu. A security source told the tracker full resumption could take weeks. US retail diesel stood at a record $6.50 a gallon and regular gasoline at $4.37 a gallon, against $3.19 a year earlier.

Accountability21 Sep

The UN (United Nations) fact-finding mission repeats its findings to the full Human Rights Council; neither accused government engages

Sara Hossain, chair of the Human Rights Council's fact-finding mission on Iran, presented the mission's 17 September findings to the full Council on 21 September, restating that there are "reasonable grounds to believe" the United States committed war crimes in the strikes that opened the Iran war on 28 February and that Iranian security forces separately committed crimes against humanity in their crackdown on the 2025–2026 protests — mass anti-government demonstrations that spread across Iran. The mission's account of the protest crackdown, by the Iranian government's own count, put the death toll at 3,038 people, including at least 221 children, with Hossain describing survivors' wounds from metal shotgun pellets that caused "permanent blindness in many cases." The Council extended the mission's mandate through 2028.

Neither accused government has changed position since last week. The mission again noted close to three dozen information requests sent to Iran that went unanswered, and the White House's dismissal of the findings — delivered the day the written report was published — stands as the administration's only public response. Hossain also flagged a draft Iranian law that would impose sentences of up to 30 years' imprisonment for communicating with foreign entities, which she said would further chill documentation of abuses on top of a five-month internet shutdown the mission says already prevented independent verification of casualty figures during the protests.

Sanctions & Trade — the law is signed; enforcement is the open question

1 item
Enforcement outlook17–22 Sep

Democrats, China and India react to the new sanctions law; analysts doubt Washington will use its full new tariff power

In the days after President Trump signed the Lindsey O. Graham Sanctioning Russia and Iran Act into law on 18 September, opposition to the bill sharpened rather than faded. House Minority Leader Hakeem Jeffries said Trump "cannot be entrusted with new powers to impose tariffs that will further raise costs for American families," and Representative Don Beyer of Virginia called the law "well intentioned, but terribly flawed," warning it lets Trump target "nearly any country as a sanctions-evader" with tariffs of up to 100% and "no guardrails or oversight, and no expiration." The Yale Budget Lab — a Yale University research center that tracks the cost of tariffs and taxes — estimates households already pay about $1,100 a year more under tariffs currently in effect, a figure Democratic critics cited as evidence the new authority could compound an existing burden.

The countries most exposed to the new power responded cautiously. China's foreign ministry spokesman Guo Jiakun opposed what he called "long-arm jurisdiction" — laws one country applies beyond its own borders — "and unilateral sanctions that have no basis in international law," while India said it was "monitoring further developments" and emphasized its commitment to "diversified sourcing" of energy. Trade analysts at Steptoe, an international law firm, judge India the most likely near-term target for secondary tariffs — tariffs imposed on a third country for doing business with the sanctioned one, rather than on the sanctioned country itself — but note that when Washington previously used similar authority, in August 2025, India received only a 25% stacked tariff — one layered on top of tariffs already in place — rather than the full rate on offer — before that penalty was removed in February 2026 trade talks — a precedent the analysts read as evidence tariffs function as negotiating leverage rather than a tool the administration intends to apply at full strength.

Context & background. The 19 September edition covered the bill's passage and signature and flagged that its "practical bite now depends entirely on whether the Treasury Department actually uses tools" it has been slow to use on existing Russia sanctions. This card does not revisit the House or Senate votes; it covers only what has happened in the six days since signature — reactions from the countries and lawmakers the law is meant to pressure, and analysts' first attempt to forecast enforcement.

The details. The Democratic objections quoted here focus on the tariff authority's open-endedness — no expiration, no congressional check on how it is used — rather than on the sanctions provisions targeting Russia's tanker fleet and gas-export ventures, which drew less criticism. Steptoe's analysis is explicit that it expects enforcement "based on bilateral relationships" rather than a uniform standard, and separately judges that "secondary tariffs of up to 100%, while costly if enforced, is not as effective a chokepoint as blocking sanctions" — sanctions that flatly forbid a transaction, rather than taxing it — because demand for energy is inelastic (it does not drop much even as costs rise), so buyers can reroute or absorb costs rather than stop purchasing. India's own public statement stopped short of any commitment to reduce Russian oil purchases.

Why it still matters. A sanctions law with a headline number — the figure used to describe it publicly, 100% tariffs — that its own likely target previously received at a quarter of that rate, and then had removed entirely after negotiation, is exactly the enforcement pattern last week's edition anticipated: broad authority, narrow and negotiable use. What would change this reading: a first actual application of the new tariff power at anywhere near its maximum rate against a named country, rather than a repeat of the India precedent.

Hybrid warfare / Europe — Lithuania's drone gets a name as Poland logs a second incident

Airspace incidents15–23 Sep

Lithuania's shot-down drone is identified as Russian; Poland reports a second airspace incursion from Kaliningrad this week

The drone Italian NATO (North Atlantic Treaty Organisation) fighter jets shot down over Lithuania on 15 September — the 19 September edition's lead hybrid-warfare story (hybrid warfare meaning deniable, below-open-conflict tactics such as drone incursions and cyberattacks, rather than open combat), with its origin and payload still preliminary at the time — has since been identified by Lithuanian President Gitanas Nausėda as a Russian "Gerbera-2" decoy drone that appears to have been aimed at Ukraine before straying into Lithuanian airspace, according to reporting reviewed by this briefing. A separate weekly roundup of European drone incidents describes debris from the same drone as "partly wooden and charred," consistent with Shahed-136-derivative — Iran's exploding attack drone — or Geran-style decoy construction — Russia's own version of the same drone — and quotes a NATO (North Atlantic Treaty Organisation) general characterizing the incident and three earlier 2026 Baltic airspace incursions — Estonia in May, Latvia in June and again in August — as "calculated hybrid probing rather than precursors to conventional attack" (deniable, low-level testing of defenses, not a prelude to open war).

On 23 September, a Russian Mi-8 military helicopter crossed roughly 300 metres into Polish airspace near Braniewo, close to Russia's Kaliningrad exclave, and stayed for about 42 seconds before leaving; Poland scrambled fighter jets in response. Poland's Operational Command — the military headquarters that runs Poland's day-to-day armed-forces operations — said "the nature of the incident indicates that Russia is once again testing the readiness of our air defence," and Deputy Defense Minister Władysław Kosiniak-Kamysz said interceptions of Russian aircraft along the North Atlantic Treaty Organisation's (NATO) eastern flank — the alliance's member states bordering Russia and Belarus — have risen by more than 250% recently. The same day, the Institute for the Study of War reported a separate Russian Ministry of Defense claim — assessed as false by the tracker — that Russian forces had encircled the Ukrainian town of Dobropillya, part of what the assessment calls an intensifying Russian cognitive-warfare campaign — a push to spread confusion and false narratives rather than to win ground militarily.
