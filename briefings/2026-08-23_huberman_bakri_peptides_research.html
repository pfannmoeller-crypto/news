A Physician Makes the Case for Peptides — His Argument, Walked Through

Dr. Abud Bakri, an internal-medicine physician, spends roughly two hours with Andrew Huberman on BPC-157, the Soviet-era bioregulators and the gray market that now supplies both. He is enthusiastic and he is careful, often in the same sentence. This piece presents his case as he means it, then walks it through in his order — with the checks and the named counter-positions attached where they belong.

1 · His argument in brief

Stated as strongly as they mean it. These are Bakri's theses unless marked otherwise — Huberman contributes one of the load-bearing arguments himself and it is attributed to him. No verdicts in this section; those come later.

  • "Peptide" is not a useful category — receptors are. The word groups together molecules that have nothing in common. What actually predicts whether a compound does something reliable is whether it hits an identified receptor. GLP-1 agonists do, and produce large, consistent clinical effects. BPC-157 and thymosin beta-4 do not have a clearly identified target, and behave accordingly: fascinating in animals, unpredictable in people. Judge by target, not by the label.
  • BPC-157 is the most interesting unexplored compound in medicine. It comes from gastric juice, a tissue whose whole job is protecting itself from constant assault. In animals it accelerates healing of tendon, ligament, nerve and gut, protects against burn-induced ulcers, and keeps working even when corticosteroids are given alongside — which is remarkable, because corticosteroids normally stop wound healing. It does this without a known receptor and with no established lethal dose despite enormous overdoses in animals.
  • The absence of human data is an economic failure, not a scientific verdict. BPC-157 cannot be meaningfully patented — change one amino acid and it is a different compound — so nobody has a financial reason to run the trial. Millions of people are already using it. The knowledge gap persists not because the compound was tested and failed, but because the structure of drug development has no way to pay for the test.
  • Both possible answers are bad, which is why the question is urgent. Either BPC-157 works and millions of people are being denied something useful, or it does not work or is harmful and millions of people are injecting it right now. There is no comfortable version of the status quo.
  • Regulation created the gray market it now condemns. Restricting peptides at the compounding pharmacy did not stop demand; it pushed people to "research purposes only" websites where nobody checks purity, dose or even identity. The regulatory action moved the risk rather than reducing it.
  • The delivery system is the danger, not necessarily the molecule — this one is Huberman's, argued in his own voice. The most likely harm is not BPC-157 itself but contaminated product, wrong compounds, unknown solvents, and a telehealth layer where a physician rubber-stamps an e-commerce checkout. The people getting hurt are getting hurt by the supply chain. Bakri gestures at the same idea when he attributes bad reports to sourcing and contamination, but the crisp thesis is the host's.
  • Clinicians are marking up the drugs they prescribe, and patients do not know. With compounded medication a physician can buy at one price and charge another, capturing the difference invisibly. Patients have every right to ask what their doctor paid.
  • Dismissing Croatian and Russian research is parochialism, not method. The question that matters is whether the science was done carefully — randomised, blinded, controlled — not which country's regulator blessed it. Soviet-era research had different goals (better soldiers, better astronauts) than the Western subscription-drug model, and different is not automatically worse.
  • The honest clinical model is a frank conversation, not a prescription. A physician should be able to say: this is promising, it is not approved, there is minimal human data, are you willing to try it with defined endpoints and monitoring? That is better medicine than either a rubber stamp or a refusal that sends the patient to a website.
  • Fundamentals first. Anyone not sleeping, training, eating and managing stress properly has no business injecting anything. Peptides are the last ten percent, not the first.

The argument, walked through

Receptors as the organising idea

"It's one of the languages of the human body." — and peptides split, he says, by "whether or not they have receptors or they have no receptor." — Bakri

This is the frame Bakri builds everything else on, and it is genuinely a good one. Rather than arguing compound by compound, he offers the listener a sorting rule. On one side sit molecules with a known docking point — the GLP-1 agonists — where you can predict the effect, dose it, and expect it to reproduce. On the other sit BPC-157 and thymosin beta-4, which he places in a category of compounds that clearly do something in animals while nobody can say through what. Huberman is visibly taken with the distinction and says he has not heard peptides described this way before.

Bakri does not treat "no receptor" as mysticism. He offers candidate mechanisms: the compound may modify existing proteins, or link them in configurations that favour gene transcription. For the Russian peptides he is more specific — they act as epigenetic modifiers, binding into the groove of DNA at particular sites and opening or closing chromatin, functionally similar to how a steroid hormone switches on a gene programme.

Check · "no identified receptor" for BPC-157 and thymosin beta-4 Misleading C 4/5  Thymosin beta-4 has two established molecular targets, not zero: it is a well-characterised G-actin sequestering protein, and Ku80 was published as a receptor for it in the Journal of Biological Chemistry in 2007. For BPC-157 the claim is more defensible but still overstated — no dedicated cloned receptor exists, but a VEGFR2–Akt–eNOS signalling pathway has been proposed in the literature since around 2016 as its angiogenic mechanism. "No confirmed dedicated receptor" would be accurate; "no receptor identified" overstates the gap, and it overstates it in the direction that makes the compounds sound more unexplored than they are.

The frame is more useful than the fact that populates it — and the error runs toward mystery rather than away from it.

Where the compound came from

The origin story occupies a surprising amount of the episode, and Bakri tells it well. Pavlov, he notes, did more than ring bells at dogs: he collected canine gastric juice through surgical fistulas and sold it as a medicine, and that was part of how he funded himself. Separately, Hans Selye described what happens to a stressed animal — enlarged adrenals, a destroyed gastric lining, a shrunken thymus. A Croatian group put the two together and reasoned that if stress destroys the gut lining, the gut must contain something that protects it.

They fractionated gastric juice, found a large parent protein they called body protection compound, and isolated the fifteen-amino-acid fragment that appeared to carry the activity. Bakri is careful about a point that most online material gets wrong: the body does not manufacture BPC-157. It manufactures the parent protein, of which BPC-157 is a fragment.

"So they did all kinds of horrible things to these mice." — Bakri

Severed tendons, cut ACLs, burn wounds. Faster healing across the board, and — the finding that he clearly considers the most striking — burn-injured animals that would normally develop severe gastric ulcers developed none. It was the Achilles tendon paper, he says, that escaped into the bodybuilding world and produced the market that exists today, which he notes is an ironic inversion: the compound was originally investigated as a gastric treatment, and its fame rests on a musculoskeletal use that was never the point.

Check · the origin narrative Mostly true C 3-4/5  Pavlov's laboratory did commercially sell gastric juice as a digestive tonic and it was a real funding source. Selye's 1936 Nature description of the General Adaptation Syndrome triad — adrenal hypertrophy, thymicolymphatic involution, gastroduodenal ulceration — is textbook and accurately rendered. The Zagreb group under Predrag Sikirić published the first BPC-157 entry indexed on PubMed in 1992, close enough to the "91" in the transcript. Bakri describes the parent protein as "a $40,000 dalton giant peptide", which is a garbled rendering of approximately 40 kDa rather than a separate factual error.

The history is sound, and it is doing real work: it explains why a gut compound would plausibly have systemic protective effects at all.

The part he thinks everyone is missing

Before this section it is worth recording a disclaimer Huberman inserts mid-sentence, because it sets the tone for everything that follows. Describing what strikes him about the compound, he interrupts himself: "and by the way that's not an endorsement for BPC." He then explains why he finds the animal data remarkable at all — his own laboratory worked for years on optic nerve repair, and nerve regeneration in the central nervous system is famously close to impossible. A compound that appears to accelerate nerve, tendon and ligament healing without a known receptor is, in his word, weird.

The strand that follows is the one Bakri himself rates highest, and it has almost nothing to do with the injuries the compound is famous for.

"They also did weird things on the neurological side." — Bakri

Mice were made drunk and given BPC-157, and by his account got less drunk and navigated mazes better. More striking, mice put through alcohol withdrawal — a state that can be lethal, and which in a hospital requires benzodiazepines to survive — did not show the withdrawal symptoms. Huberman immediately breaks in with the second disclaimer of the segment: "We did not just recommend you take BPC with alcohol. Want to be very clear."

Bakri's own ranking of the evidence follows, and it cuts directly against the market that has formed around the compound: the musculoskeletal use gets all the hype, but he considers the neurological, neuropsychiatric and gastric effects far more interesting, because they suggest the compound is modulating the gut–brain axis rather than repairing tissue locally.

He supports this with reports running in the opposite direction from the usual testimonials. Patients come to him complaining — not praising — that their stimulant medication stopped working since starting oral BPC-157. He points to discussion boards full of people describing low mood and flat affect on it. These are not endorsements; they are signals that something is happening to dopaminergic signalling, in a direction users did not want. He offers a mechanism: if BPC-157 is what the Croatian group claims — part of the gut's defence system against noxious agents — then acting through the gut to damp signalling to the brain is exactly what it would be expected to do. He anchors this to real work, naming Duke's Diego Bohórquez on neuropod cells, which signal through the vagus nerve to promote or suppress dopamine release, and says he would help fund a study there.

Other views · why this strand deserves more weight than it gets, and less Methodologically this is the most interesting material in the episode, for a reason neither speaker names: the negative reports are more informative than the positive ones. A person injecting an expensive compound for tendon repair has every incentive to perceive improvement, which is why the healing anecdotes are weak. A person annoyed that their stimulant stopped working has no such incentive — the report cuts against what they wanted. Unexpected, unwelcome, mechanistically coherent effects are the class of anecdote least explained by expectation.

Against that: every one of the animal findings cited here — the intoxication effect, the withdrawal protection — comes from the same single Croatian group whose concentration Bakri flags scrupulously two sections later, and the caveat is not repeated here. The gut–brain mechanism is a hypothesis he is offering, not a finding, and he says as much. None of the neuropsychiatric claims in this segment were independently checkable within this analysis, and they are not counted in the scorecard.

His most interesting argument is the one that would sell the fewest vials.

The single-group problem, raised by him first

The most important thing to record about this episode is that Bakri does not have to be cornered into the central weakness of his own case. He volunteers it, repeatedly, unprompted.

"That's the big concern of BPC. All the data comes from one group. So people can be skeptical." — Bakri

He returns to it when Huberman asks directly about cancer risk — whether the angiogenic effect might vascularise a tumour someone did not know they had. Bakri answers that there is no cancer signal in the animal literature, and then immediately qualifies his own answer: "Now that all that literature comes from one group. So we have to be very careful." He notes a couple of Chinese groups have replicated parts of the work, and that interest is now growing elsewhere.

Check · concentration of the BPC-157 literature True C 4/5  Confirmed and, if anything, understated. Sikirić's group has authored more than 150 papers on the compound — essentially the totality of the animal literature — and the great majority of the roughly 200 published BPC-157 studies list Sikirić or his frequent co-author Sven Seiwerth. Reporting by STAT in February 2026 found that outside that group only one team, in Taiwan, has independently run several BPC-157 studies. Independent replication is thin rather than absent.
Other views · is the data gap a market failure or a verdict? Bakri's framing — promising compound, starved of trial funding — has real named support. Paul Knoepfler (stem-cell researcher, UC Davis) makes the same economic argument: an unpatentable molecule attracts no trial sponsor even when large numbers of people already use it. Flynn McGuire (chief medical resident, University of Utah Health) is the most credible voice from inside conventional medicine and lands close to Bakri: some studies outside Croatia show broadly similar findings, and there is enough preclinical evidence to justify more rigorous investigation.

The counter-position is that the concentration is itself the finding. McGuire also supplies the sharpest version of it — that much of the research comes from a small number of teams, often in less demanding journals and without sufficient independent replication, and that curing things in mice has a poor track record of transferring. A Polish review team concluded that without rigorous studies it will remain difficult to argue BPC-157 meets the criteria of a legitimate therapeutic agent. Most pointedly, Vladimir Trkulja, acting head of pharmacology at the University of Zagreb — Sikirić's own institution — told reporters he is not aware of any formal human study in which the peptide was used. That last quotation needs a bridge, or it will read as catching Bakri in a fabrication, which it does not. The check at c4 confirms two Croatian human trials genuinely took place, exactly as Bakri describes them. Both statements can be true at once because the trials exist only as abstracts: from inside the formal literature, where a study counts once it is published in full, there is nothing to see. That is the substance of the problem rather than a contradiction of it — and it is Bakri, not his critics, who volunteers that the full data was never released. USADA's institutional position is that because BPC-157 has not been extensively studied in humans, it is unknown whether there is a safe dose. Notably, no credible independent voice was found arguing that the animal data is strong and sufficient — even the optimists want the human trials first.

He concedes the weakness so consistently that the fair criticism is not that he hides it, but that conceding it does not change what he does with the compound afterwards.

What human evidence actually exists

"The data is not available. The abstracts are only available." — Bakri, on the Croatian ulcerative-colitis trials

Asked what has been done in people, Bakri gives a precise and unflattering answer. Two small trials, phase one and phase two, run by the same Croatian group in the early 2000s, using BPC-157 as a rectal enema in ulcerative colitis at doses around 80 mg — orders of magnitude above the 100–200 micrograms people typically inject. Placebo-controlled. Phase one showed no adverse effects; phase two, at roughly forty patients, produced at least a positive signal. And then the qualification that matters: the full data was never published.

He adds a finding that cuts against the enthusiasm around oral BPC-157: when they measured it, they could not find the compound in the bloodstream. Either it is degraded immediately or it stays local to the gut lining. Huberman seizes on the implication — that a compound credited with effects throughout the body may never leave the gut at all. Bakri's answer is honest and deflating: "We don't know where it goes, how it goes."

On the story that arguably built the market — an Eastern European athlete with a completely severed Achilles who allegedly injected BPC-157 and medalled at the Olympics — Bakri refuses to play along. He calls it hearsay, doubts the athlete used only BPC-157, and observes that the story was subsequently used by vendors as a marketing instrument.

Check · the human trials, and the hamstring trial he says is running Mostly true C 4/5  The Croatian trials are real: a phase 1 safety and pharmacokinetic study of PL 14736 (BPC-157) by enema in healthy volunteers, and a multicentre, randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled phase 2 enema study in mild-to-moderate ulcerative colitis, both Pliva-sponsored. Both appear only as conference and journal abstracts with no locatable full text — exactly as he describes. The specific 80 mg dose and n=40 could not be confirmed independently at abstract level. His claim of a US phase 2 hamstring trial checks out precisely: NCT07437547, sponsor Hudson Biotech, roughly 120 participants, actively enrolling. That is the single most concrete forward-looking claim in the episode and it is correct.

On the question where he had the most room to inflate — what has been proven in humans — he is straighter than the market that sells the compound.

Safety, and the number that does not exist

"We don't even know the LD50 of BPC, which makes it hard for it to become an FDA approved [drug]." — Bakri

This is a nicely double-edged passage. Bakri reports that animals have been given something like a thousand times the human-equivalent dose without meaningful adverse effects — which sounds reassuring, and which he then turns into a regulatory obstacle. Because no dose killed half the animals, there is no LD50; and because there is no LD50, a clinician has no toxicological anchor to prescribe from. The absence of harm is itself a barrier to approval.

Huberman presses the tumour question carefully and explicitly disavows leading the witness. Bakri answers that carcinogenic compounds usually leave a signature in animal data — he offers Cardarine as the cautionary example, a metabolically promising drug abandoned after rodent cancer signals — and that BPC-157 shows none. He then adds a genuinely interesting complication: BPC-157 is not a uniform angiogenesis promoter, and in at least one melanoma model it lowered VEGF. It might be anti-cancer. Nobody has tested it.

The real-world signals he does report are smaller and more mundane: worsening haematomas, and facial angiomas becoming more prominent — both consistent with a compound that promotes blood vessel formation. Huberman contributes a matching observation from a physician friend.

Check · Cardarine as the carcinogenicity comparison True C 3/5  GW501516 development was halted around 2007 after rodent studies showed rapid multi-organ tumour formation, and it appears on the WADA Prohibited List. The analogy is apt: it is a real case where animal data caught a cancer risk before humans were exposed at scale, which is precisely why its absence for BPC-157 is worth something — and precisely why the fact that the absence rests on one group's work matters.

The strongest safety argument available for BPC-157 is that a large signal probably would have shown up by now; the weakest part of it is who did the looking.

The regulatory machine, and the workaround

Here the episode becomes genuinely useful, because Bakri is describing a system he operates inside. Compounding pharmacies work from FDA lists: category one substances may be compounded, category two may not. BPC-157 was moved to category two — do not compound — and a market that had been quietly served by prescription for years was cut off.

What happened next is the part worth studying. Compounders relabelled the compound as PDA, pentadecapeptide arginate, and carried on.

"It's the same exact thing." — Bakri. Huberman, audibly caught out: "Really?" — "Yes. One of them will be an acetate, one of them will be an arginate, but the PDA is BPC-157." — Bakri

The listed substance is the acetate salt; the arginate salt is a different entry, therefore not the listed substance, therefore prescribable. Bakri reports that physicians are writing for PDA now. He layers a second complication on top: even where the FDA permits, state medical boards may not, and he says he personally received a letter from one state instructing him that non-FDA- approved peptides may not be prescribed regardless. Telehealth does not escape this, because the governing law is where the patient sits, not where the prescriber does.

His forecast for what the permission would produce is notably unsentimental for someone advocating access:

"There's going to be a doctor somewhere in a room that's going to stamp the prescription, but it's just... e-commerce. It's supplements with a stamp of a doctor, which is not good medical care at all." — Bakri
Check · the category-two timeline Mostly true C 4/5  One date is wrong and two are right. BPC-157 was placed in the 503A category two bulks listing in 2023, not "late 2024" as stated. He is correct that it was removed from category two on 15 April 2026, and correct that it has not been added to category one — the Pharmacy Compounding Advisory Committee voted 8–6 in July 2026 to recommend category one status tied to an ulcerative colitis indication, but that has not been finalised. The compound sits in genuine limbo: not barred, not affirmatively cleared.
Check · PDA is BPC-157 under another name Mostly true C 3/5  PDA uses the identical fifteen-amino-acid sequence and differs only in counter-ion — arginate rather than the acetate salt named in the FDA listing. Industry and compounding-law commentary describes the arginate rebrand as a route to market the same active peptide outside the category two restriction. No FDA ruling specifically on salt-form equivalence was located, so the regulatory characterisation is secondary interpretation rather than settled law.
Other views · regulation as obstacle, or as the correct response The episode consistently frames restriction as the problem. The best-documented opposing case is an ITIF analysis by Katie Kortbein and Meghan Ostertag (July 2026) arguing the FDA should not permit untested peptides into compounded medication: several peptides under review have no human safety studies at all; compounding pharmacies cannot reliably detect heavy metals or incorrect protein sequences; and much of the supply is sourced through Chinese gray markets. The FDA's own stated reasoning is narrower and more technical than "we are being cautious": compounded BPC-157 may pose immunogenicity risk for certain administration routes and presents complexities in peptide-related impurities and API characterisation, leaving the agency without enough information to know whether it would cause harm.

On compounded GLP-1s specifically, Stanford research cited in that analysis found a fifteen-fold increase in poison-control calls related to injected GLP-1 drugs, disproportionately linked to dosing errors more common with compounded product — a concrete counterweight to the episode's "the compounders were doing everyone a favour" framing.

Worth noting for symmetry: the capture argument runs in both directions. The same ITIF piece observes that several members of the FDA advisory panel had peptide-industry financial ties, while a Washington Examiner op-ed made the same accusation about the panel that voted to ease restrictions. Whichever way one reads it, the committee was not a neutral body.

His description of the mechanism is better sourced than his dates, and his own forecast of what liberalisation produces is bleaker than his advocacy for it implies.

Where the material actually comes from

The supply-chain segment is the most commercially informative part of the episode and also where the checkable claims fare worst. Bakri's structural point is sound: there is a single upstream source of active ingredient, and it branches into three tiers of decreasing rigour — regulated pharmaceutical manufacture, compounding pharmacies of wildly varying quality, and "research purposes only" websites where quality is unknown and varies batch to batch. Huberman's observation that essentially nobody buying research-grade peptides is running cell culture at home is correct and worth stating plainly.

"There are no such thing as American-made peptides." — Bakri

He also produces the market number that has since travelled widely:

"Maybe I'd say 5 to 10 billion dollars on gray market peptides being spent in the United States in 2025, and that's going to grow this year." — Bakri

And the observation that explains the shape of the demand better than any number: the GLP-1 boom did not merely create appetite for peptides, it normalised self-injection. Once tens of millions of people had learned that a subcutaneous pen hurts less than a mosquito bite, the psychological barrier that had confined injectables to bodybuilders was gone. He notes the market is heavily female and describes affiliate selling spreading through social networks the way multi-level marketing did a generation ago.

Check · "no such thing as American-made peptides" False C 4/5  As an absolute claim this does not survive. Bachem operates peptide API manufacturing in Vista, California alongside Bachem Americas in Torrance; CordenPharma runs peptide manufacturing in Boulder, Colorado; PolyPeptide and Lonza also maintain US peptide facilities. Genuine domestic pharmaceutical-grade peptide API production exists. The directional point survives — China does dominate the low-cost bulk synthesis that feeds the gray market and much of the compounding supply — but "everything's from China" is not accurate, and the error runs in the direction that makes gray-market sourcing sound less avoidable than it is.
Check · $5–10 billion US gray market in 2025 Unverifiable C 1/5  No sourced estimate of US gray-market or research-peptide spending exists in any market research I could locate — the established firms size the legitimate peptide therapeutics market, which is a different thing entirely. Bakri prefaces the figure with "maybe I'd say", so he is not presenting it as sourced. It should be treated as one insider's order-of-magnitude impression and nothing more. Given how readily a number like this gets repeated as fact, that distinction is worth holding onto.
Check · compounders and the shortage window Mostly true C 2/5  The mechanism is real: compounders may produce a copy of an approved drug during a declared FDA shortage. FDA declared the tirzepatide shortage resolved in December 2024 and semaglutide in February 2025, after which compounders were required to wind down, prompting litigation. The episode's implication that compounders were invited in and then punished has a factual basis, though the wind-down followed the shortage ending rather than a change of heart.

His structural map of the supply chain is the most valuable thing in this section; the two headline figures attached to it are the least supported claims in the episode.

The Russian peptides

The second half turns to Vladimir Khavinson's bioregulators, and here Bakri is at his most encyclopedic. He explains an origin story that is either wonderful or convenient depending on temperament: Soviet submariners and cosmonauts returning from deployment visibly aged, with disrupted circadian rhythm and damaged immunity; Khavinson tasked with finding something to reverse it; extracts of pineal and thymus tissue; then the isolation of the specific peptides carrying the effect.

He is precise about a naming confusion that trips up most secondary sources — pinealon does not come from the pineal gland at all. It is a tripeptide isolated from cortexin, a cortical brain extract. Epitalon is the tetrapeptide from the pineal. Huberman, mildly exasperated, proposes renaming pinealon to EDR after its amino acid sequence, and they use that for the rest of the conversation.

On epitalon, Bakri gives the telomerase story — enhanced telomerase activity and telomere length — and Huberman immediately raises the obvious objection: many cancers upregulate telomerase precisely in order to become immortal, so a telomerase activator is a double-edged instrument. Bakri's answer is the most epistemically honest sentence in the episode:

"So far, we haven't seen any signal of problems. But we also haven't looked very hard or very long." — Bakri

He applies the same caveat to the whole Russian corpus without being asked, noting flatly that it is not the gold-standard American research that Western readers are trained to want. Earlier in the conversation he had made a related and unusually candid point cutting against the romance of Soviet science: there are Soviet-era compounds that sound extraordinary in the original literature and turn out, when tested, to be less potent than claimed.

Check · peptide identities and the pineal naming quirk True C 3/5  Epitalon is the tetrapeptide Ala-Glu-Asp-Gly derived from pineal extract; pinealon is the tripeptide Glu-Asp-Arg derived from cortexin, a brain cortex extract, and genuinely does not come from the pineal gland despite its name. Both trace to Khavinson's bioregulator series. He has this right where a great deal of online material has it wrong.
Check · epitalon, telomerase, and the cancer question Mostly true, with a material omission C 3/5  The telomerase claim is published: Khavinson and Bondarev reported epitalon-induced telomerase activity and telomere elongation in human somatic cells in Bulletin of Experimental Biology and Medicine in 2003. The tumour-safety claim also holds up on its own terms — Anisimov and Khavinson's SHR-mouse lifespan work found no effect on total spontaneous tumour incidence and reported a six-fold reduction in leukaemia, and animals were followed to natural death. Two things are missing from his answer. First, virtually all of this comes from one research lineage, a caveat he applies scrupulously to BPC-157 and does not repeat here. Second, and more consequential: a 2025 in-vitro study (Al-Dulaimi et al.) found epitalon activated Alternative Lengthening of Telomeres in breast cancer cells — a finding that complicates the safety picture in exactly the direction Huberman was probing, and which does not reach the listener.
Other views · the parochialism question, taken seriously Huberman's challenge — why should a Croatian or Russian trial count for less? — is a fair methodological question and deserves a straight answer. The honest answer is that the objection is not geographic but evidentiary. Searching for named Western scientists who have evaluated Khavinson's corpus on its merits turns up remarkably little in either direction: only two Western laboratory studies of any Khavinson bioregulator could be identified (Al-Dulaimi et al. 2025 and Gatta et al. 2025), both cell-culture work addressing neither dose, route, pharmacokinetics nor clinical effect. No independent Western randomised controlled trial of any Khavinson bioregulator appears to have been published.

State this plainly: I could not find a single named credible Western scientist arguing that the Soviet-era work was methodologically rigorous by modern standards — but neither did I find a substantive published Western refutation of it. The corpus has largely been ignored rather than assessed. That is a weaker position than Huberman's parochialism framing implies, and also a weaker position than dismissal implies. On general peptide safety, biogerontologist Matt Kaeberlein has publicly pushed back on the "peptides are safe and natural" framing, citing a 2026 incident in which two people became critically ill from peptides distributed at a longevity conference — though that is a statement about the market, not about Khavinson specifically.
Other views · is telomerase activation a plausible longevity lever at all? Maria Blasco (director, Spanish National Cancer Research Centre) is the field's leading advocate: her lab's AAV-delivered telomerase gene therapy in mice delayed ageing and extended lifespan without increasing cancer, with the vector chosen specifically to avoid insertional risk. Against that sits the reason the objection exists at all — roughly 85–90% of human tumours reactivate telomerase as an enabling step, which is why uncontrolled systemic activation is the default toxicological concern.

The asymmetry is the finding here: Blasco's case is a carefully vectored gene therapy tested in animals with cancer-surveillance endpoints built into the design. That is not the same object as an unstudied peptide self-administered systemically, and no named telomere biologist was found defending the latter by the former's standard.

He is the most rigorous voice in the room about Croatian data and the least rigorous about Russian data, and the difference is not explained in the episode.

Anecdote, and what both men do with it

The episode's persuasive weight does not rest on the animal literature. It rests on two stories. Bakri tore his triceps, describes an arm purple from shoulder to elbow, injected BPC-157 locally at a dose he says was far above what people normally use, and reports his physiotherapist's astonishment at the healing rate — roughly three to four weeks against a typical three months. Huberman reports a neck strain that resolved two days after a small local injection.

What is notable is that both men immediately undercut their own stories.

"Would I have healed that fast anyways? I don't know." — Bakri
"I don't know what would have happened had I just waited." — Huberman

Huberman goes further and volunteers the balancing evidence unprompted: people report no effect, people report feeling unwell, results may reflect the solvent rather than the compound, or contamination, or individual variation. He explicitly says the reports cannot be ascribed to BPC-157 given placebo effects, and adds that he is not saying it to protect himself but so people understand how the field arrived where it is. Bakri separately reports that he has seen injuries where BPC-157 did not help much.

Huberman also adds a third possibility that complicates his guest's clean either/or framing, and it deserves to survive: the likeliest outcome may be neither vindication nor refutation but noise — adverse events start being reported, nobody can establish whether they were caused by the compound, by misuse, or by whatever it was dissolved in, and the whole category drifts back into obscurity with the question still open. Causal ambiguity kills it regardless of the truth.

The constructive proposal underneath the anecdotes is the interesting part: Bakri wants the scattered n-of-1 reports aggregated into one place so signals can be read off them, and hopes that a category one listing would at least generate observational data from supervised prescribing.

Other views · can aggregated anecdote generate evidence? There is a genuine specialist split here rather than a caricature. Rory Collins and colleagues (Oxford), in "The Magic of Randomization versus the Myth of Real-World Evidence" (NEJM, 2020), give the canonical sceptical position: uncontrolled data, even aggregated at scale, systematically misleads for anything short of very large effects, because it cannot separate treatment effect from selection, secular trend and regression to the mean. Hans-Georg Eichler (then senior medical officer at the EMA) and co-authors replied directly in Clinical Pharmacology & Therapeutics (2021) that real-world evidence has legitimate uses in safety surveillance and external control arms even though it cannot replace randomisation on efficacy. Bakri's aggregation proposal sits closer to Eichler than critics would grant.

But the specific anecdotes fail on narrower grounds than "placebo". Lower-grade partial tendon tears are managed conservatively over a two-to-six-week window precisely because that is the expected unaided recovery arc — so "healed in three to four weeks while taking BPC-157" is not distinguishable from "healed in three to four weeks". Acute minor neck strains resolve within days as their modal natural history. And Huberman's separate report elsewhere in the episode that pinealon roughly doubled his REM sleep rests on consumer wearable staging, which validation studies against polysomnography consistently identify as the least reliable metric such devices produce, with REM percentage varying night to night for entirely mundane reasons. Properly randomised, blinded, crossover n-of-1 designs can generate valid individual evidence; none of these three observations has any of those properties.

They caveat the anecdotes accurately and then keep telling them, which is what anecdotes are for.

The GLP-1 anchor, and what approval actually buys

Asked which peptide has the most robust evidence, Bakri does not hesitate: the GLP-1 agonists, approved, effective, close to a miracle drug. What follows is the strongest passage in the episode, because he uses the approved compounds to show what "approved" does and does not mean. He lists the adverse effects without softening: pancreatitis, common gastrointestinal effects, dehydration, rodent medullary thyroid carcinoma, diabetic retinopathy worsening, and weight regain after discontinuation.

Then he delivers the observation that best captures the whole ecosystem:

"So the irony here is we're using unproven peptides to help manage the side effects of FDA-approved peptides." — Bakri

Huberman's closing frame is that the regulatory process does not deliver safety but delivers a mechanism — approval means a system exists that will keep learning about the drug as millions use it, whereas for BPC-157 there is no such system at all. Bakri's counterpoint is that the same process is slow enough to cut both ways. His final advice is conservative: fundamentals first, a knowledgeable physician, a reputable source, and start small.

Rodent medullary thyroid carcinoma is an actual FDA boxed warning, with contraindication for personal or family history of MTC or MEN2. The retinopathy signal comes from SUSTAIN-6. The STEP-1 extension found roughly two-thirds of lost weight regained a year after stopping semaglutide. This is an accurate and notably unflinching list from someone advocating for the category.

The most useful thing either man says about unapproved peptides is said by pointing at the approved ones.

Stepping back

Which way the errors lean

Read off this run's verdicts rather than assumed: the pattern is unusually specific. Bakri is accurate — often impressively so — on claims that can be checked against a primary document, and loose on claims that cannot. The peptide sequences are right. The naming quirk that defeats most secondary sources is right. The Selye triad is right. The GLP-1 adverse-effect list is right and unflattering to his own side. The pivotal trial durations are right. Most strikingly, the one concrete forward-looking claim in the entire episode — that a US phase 2 hamstring trial is running — is exactly right, down to a real registration number.

The failures cluster in a different category: unsourceable market and supply-chain assertions. The gray-market dollar figure has no basis anywhere. "No such thing as American-made peptides" is simply wrong. The category two date is off by a year. The "no receptor" framing overstates the mystery. And on epitalon, the omission of a 2025 finding that it activated alternative telomere lengthening in cancer cells matters more than any of the numerical slips, because Huberman was asking precisely that question.

Directionally, most of the failures do lean toward the permissive reading — a bigger market, an unavoidable Chinese supply chain, more unexplored mystery, a cleaner safety picture for epitalon. But the lean is real without being universal, and it would misrepresent the episode to file it as systematic advocacy: on the two questions where a motivated speaker had the most to gain by shading — what has actually been proven in humans, and what the approved drugs do to people — he was straighter than the industry that surrounds him. A handful of verdicts in one episode is not a base rate, and this piece does not establish one.

The messenger

Dr. Abud Bakri. His own material describes him as a board-certified internal medicine physician and a graduate of the University of Colorado School of Medicine, leading clinical governance and protocol architecture at an organisation called The Systems, with stated focus on peptide therapeutics and bioregulators. Two things could not be established and should not be glossed: his precise ownership or equity position in any peptide-related business, and the identity of the organisation behind the "we" that consults with orthopaedic groups and maintains what he describes as a large compilation of n-of-1 reports. A PubMed search returned no peer-reviewed peptide papers authored by him — his expertise, as presented, is clinical and encyclopedic rather than published. He discloses on air that he personally uses BPC-157, pinealon and epitalon, which is more than many guests do.

Andrew Huberman. The venue is his own podcast — an owned platform, not adversarial journalism — and the episode carries four advertisement reads. His own disclosure page states that he serves as a scientific adviser to AG1, Eight Sleep, Function Health, Momentous, Reveri and WHOOP and receives financial compensation from them, and that he co-founded Scicomm Media, which holds investments in some Huberman Lab sponsors. Three of the four sponsors in this episode fall under that disclosure — Eight Sleep, AG1 and Function Health, with Lingo the exception. His track record on health claims has attracted substantive published criticism — a 2024 Slate critique by biomedical scientist Andrea Love cited overstatement of ashwagandha evidence, a rat study presented as human evidence, and false balance on fluoride — alongside genuine mainstream recognition for bringing science to a mass audience. No documented instance of a formal public correction by him was found, which is an absence-of-evidence finding rather than proof.

His own disclosures in this episode should be stated precisely, because the loose version does him a disservice. On BPC-157 he says he has tried it and does not currently take it, and that when he did, it came through a compounding pharmacy on prescription rather than from a gray-market site. On pinealon he describes occasional use — at most about three times a month. He also volunteers something more awkward: during the era of "research purposes only" websites he obtained product that way and was, in his words, too frightened to take it. That is a candid admission and it is not required of him.

One shift is worth recording precisely because it cuts against him. Previewing an earlier peptides episode, Huberman wrote publicly that BPC-157 has many animal studies but essentially no clinical trials, that the circulating anecdata are enticing, and that there are real risks. That is a more cautious public posture than describing personal experience with these compounds on air. He has not abandoned the caveats — he repeats them throughout — but the modelling has changed.

Predrag Sikirić, who is discussed at length and is not present to answer — which obliges more care here than anywhere else in this piece. Reporting by STAT in February 2026 states that he owns PharmaCotherapia, which sponsored a clinical trial in Tijuana, and serves as chief executive of Diagen, which holds a patent on a stabilised version of BPC-157 currently offered for sale — and that these interests were not disclosed on any of the team's published papers the outlet reviewed. This rests on a single outlet. I have not independently confirmed the company ownership, the officer role, or the disclosure records, and the finding is consequential enough that it should be verified before anyone relies on it. If it holds, it describes an undisclosed commercial interest at the origin of the entire evidence base — which would be material, and which the episode does not mention. Stated at its proper strength: a serious reported allegation, not an established fact.

What could not be substantiated

These are unreviewed, not rejected. Bakri's board certification and the legal structure of The Systems; the identity of the orthopaedic group; any standalone conflict-of-interest disclosure by him outside the podcast; the specific 80 mg dose and forty-patient figure for the Croatian phase 2 trial, which exists only at abstract level; the claim that a BPC-157 patent passed to Teva through acquisition; the assertion that a vial costs five dollars to produce; the claim that more than half the peptide market is female; and the exact regulatory circumstances of individual gray-market vendor closures, where the only available sources are low-grade aggregator sites repeating each other.

Bakri raises the single-group problem himself, calls the Olympic story hearsay, undercuts his own healing anecdote in the same breath as telling it, describes the telehealth future he is arguing toward as "supplements with a stamp of a doctor", and closes by telling people to fix their sleep before injecting anything. What survives

  • The receptor heuristic, as a heuristic. Even with the factual overstatement corrected, sorting these compounds by whether a target is established is a better guide than the word "peptide", which groups molecules with nothing in common.
  • The evidence map for BPC-157. Extensive animal work, concentrated in one group, two unpublished human trials, one registered US phase 2 trial now enrolling. That is an accurate picture and it is more sober than most of what is written about the compound.
  • The regulatory mechanics. The category system, the salt-form workaround, the state board layer above the federal one, and the telehealth rule following the patient — all correctly described and rarely explained this clearly anywhere.
  • The delivery-system argument. Huberman's position that the likeliest harm is the supply chain rather than the molecule is well reasoned, and the compounded-GLP-1 poison-control data supports rather than undermines it — while also complicating his conclusion.
  • The GLP-1 comparison as a teaching device. Using the approved drugs to show what approval buys — an ongoing learning system rather than a guarantee of safety — is the most transferable idea in the episode.
  • The disclosed anecdotes, as anecdotes. Both men label them correctly. Kept at that status they are legitimate data about why the market exists.

What to discount

Cutting in both directions, deliberately.

  • Against the episode's case: the $5–10bn market figure, which has no source; the claim that no American peptide manufacturing exists; the "no receptor" framing; and the epitalon safety answer, which omits the finding most relevant to the question being asked.
  • Against the episode's case: the healing anecdotes, which fail on natural-history and regression-to-the-mean grounds before placebo is even considered, and the REM sleep observation, which rests on the least reliable measurement consumer wearables produce.
  • Against the critics: the "it's just a supplement pitch" reading does not survive contact with the transcript — the guest raises the single-group problem himself and the host presses the cancer question hard. Dismissing the episode wholesale means discarding accurate regulatory mechanics and an accurate adverse-effect list.
  • Against the critics: the restriction case is not disinterested either. ITIF receives industry funding from parties with a competitive stake against compounded alternatives, and the advisory panel has been accused of industry capture from both directions.
  • Against everyone: the Khavinson corpus has been neither replicated nor refuted in the West. Treating that silence as vindication is wrong; treating it as refutation is equally wrong.
