1

His argument in brief

This is the piece's strongest reconstruction of the guest's position, not a transcript of it. Six theses, no verdicts — those come in section 2.

A reference range describes a population, not a patient

A man at the bottom of a lab's printed range is, on Steiner's argument, told he is "fine" by a statistic built from everyone else. He wants the clinical picture — fatigue, mood, libido, bone and muscle loss — read against the patient, not only the bracket on the printout. He roots this in deficiency, not enhancement: a man who wonders for years why he cannot build muscle, testing at a quarter of the range's bottom, is his central case.

Replacement is not enhancement

The public fear of testosterone therapy, on his account, comes from conflating physiological replacement with the supraphysiological doses he saw in competitive bodybuilding. He concedes this against his own commercial interest: testosterone at the top of the range or above, in his words, "ist jetzt nicht was Longevity-promoting ist" — it does not make anyone live longer. The argument is deficiency, not a number to chase.

These are molecules the body already makes

BPC-157 occurs in gastric juice; both thymosins come from the thymus; GLP-1 agonists are the same drug class already approved for diabetes and obesity. Framing them as exotic "hormone hacking" obscures, in his telling, that the pharmacology is downstream of ordinary human biochemistry — an argument licensed GLP-1 drugs have already won in the clinic.

Not knowing the long term is not the same as refusing to act

Steiner's own answer to "how long can you take this" is "wir wissen es nicht" — we don't know. He treats that admission as a starting point, not an end: disclosure, observation for adverse effects, and a case-by-case decision with a doctor willing to engage, rather than blanket refusal because no formal trial exists yet.

Hundreds of people observed closely are information

He describes access to what he himself calls "makaber" to say out loud — hundreds of coaching clients using these substances, watched over time. He does not claim this is a trial; he claims medicine dismisses this kind of observation only because it does not fit the format it expects, throwing away a real signal along with the noise.

For women, the caution runs the other way

On hormone therapy for women, Steiner inverts the frame: decades of professional caution rest on a landmark study since re-read and largely walked back for younger, recently menopausal women, and the cost of that lingering caution — years of unaddressed symptoms — falls on patients, not the doctors withholding treatment.

Group 1 — Testosterone: the co-host's case and the guest's practice

The strongest thing in this half of the episode is not the guest's — it is the co-host's own story, and the first edition gave it to Steiner. Corrected here: a man who wondered for years why he could not build muscle, tested at roughly a quarter of his lab's lower limit, tried clomifene first and stopped over severe headaches, then started testosterone replacement.

The co-host, on what changed after he started treatment: mein Leben hat sich seitdem komplett verändert — my life has completely changed since then.

He also describes tadalafil at doses far above the label, a first relationship after years on the dating market, and a second child. Steiner's own contribution here is narrower than v1 suggested: the clinical framing that follows — low testosterone can amplify depression, and the honest question in therapy is which came first.

CheckWrong figure C 4/5 — The co-host: he gives his measured level as 0.6–0.8 ng/ml against a lab range he states as "8 bis 12". A German lab's reference range for men 20–49 is 2.49–8.36 ng/ml (Endokrinologikum); the harmonised US range is 2.64–9.16 (Endocrine Society, 2017). "8–12" is not a plausible range on either scale — closer to a unit mix-up or the top end alone. The substance survives: 0.6–0.8 ng/ml is severe hypogonadism regardless.
CheckMostly true C 3/5 — The co-host: clomifene helped a little, but caused severe headaches. Clomifene is licensed for ovulation induction; male use for secondary hypogonadism is off-label but supported by meta-analysis — it raises endogenous testosterone with less spermatogenic suppression than exogenous therapy, and headache is a recognised adverse effect (Andrology meta-analysis).
CheckMostly true — safety-relevant C 3/5 — The co-host: up to 80 mg of tadalafil, with no effect beyond nasal congestion. The FDA label's maximum approved dose is 20 mg as needed or 5 mg daily (FDA label) — 80 mg is four times it. Efficacy plateaus near 20 mg while adverse effects keep rising; nasal congestion is on the label, but exceeding it also raises hypotension risk, especially in combination with nitrates.

Steiner's own doses come next, and they run above what guidelines describe as replacement.

CheckMisleading C 3/5 — The guest: he describes replacement doses "gefächert von 90 mg bis hin zu 150 mg" a week, middle ~125 mg ester weight (roughly 80–90 mg active), against natural production of ~50 mg a week. Endogenous production is closer to 3–10 mg a day, so ~50/week is reasonable — but guideline replacement typically runs ~75–100 mg a week (Endotext; Endocrine Society). His range, with a 125 mg middle, sits at or above that band — toward supraphysiological. Two hedges of his own belong beside this, both dropped by the first edition. On abuse: "es gibt ja auch ein Missbrauchspotenzial" — there is abuse potential too — "Wenn du mal anfängst Testos zu spritzen, dann juckt sie in den Fingern zu sagen, ach komm, was passiert eigentlich mal, wenn ich mehr nehme?" — once you start injecting, your fingers itch to ask what happens with more — men, above all, do not stay at the dose, and he counts himself: "die Entscheidung habe ich auch viele Monate meines Lebens getroffen zu sagen, ja, ich will halt dumm sein" — for many months of my life I made that decision, to be stupid. On preconditions: "wenn dein Lebensstil nicht gut ist, er sich durch solche Maßnahmen eventuell noch negativer auswirken kann als vorher" — a poor lifestyle can be made worse by these measures — and, on peptides, "Du verbrennst einfach nur Geld" — you're just burning money.
CheckTrue — a hedge v1 did not carry C 4/5 — The guest: testosterone at the top of the range or above "ist jetzt nicht was Longevity-promoting ist" — his own words for the concession that high-normal or supraphysiological testosterone does not make anyone live longer, and that his case is about deficiency. This tracks the German Society for Endocrinology's position that placebo-controlled benefit in ageing men concentrates mostly on libido (DGE), and guidelines that gate therapy on documented deficiency rather than a target number.
CheckWrong — the most consequential error in the half C 4/5 — The guest: "Testosteron macht nicht per se unfruchtbar" — testosterone does not per se cause infertility — "es ist kein Verhütungsmittel" — it is not a contraceptive — and impaired sperm quality is "eher Lifestyle-bedingt", so a spermiogram beforehand is the right move. But exogenous testosterone suppresses LH and FSH and spermatogenesis with them; WHO's own male-contraceptive trials used it for exactly this reason, reliably producing azoospermia or severe oligozoospermia (World Journal of Men's Health), recovery taking a median 3–12 months, sometimes over 24 (Fertility and Sterility). "Not per se infertility" is defensible; the flat denial that it lowers fertility is not. The spermiogram advice is sound.
CheckMisleading C 3/5 — The guest: hCG and hMG maintain testicular function alongside testosterone; "wir kriegen das Ding besser als vorher, in allen Fällen bisher" — we get it working better than before, every case so far — using drugs "unsere Ärzte nicht komplett verschreiben können" — doctors will not fully prescribe. Low-dose hCG has observational, not randomised, support against azoospermia during testosterone therapy (Fertility and Sterility, 2010); both hCG and hMG are EU-licensed for other indications, and this use is off-label. Nothing published supports better-than-baseline outcomes in every case — but his aside that doctors won't prescribe all of it is accurate.
CheckMisleading by omission C 4/5 — The guest: the standard warnings on heart and blood pressure apply "nur sehr bedingt" — only in a limited way — and must be checked case by case; "gibt es so krasse No-gos, wie man das immer so hört… würde ich gar nicht mehr so sagen" — the absolute no-gos people always cite, he no longer says that. His individualised handling of uncomplicated hypertension or stable heart disease is broadly defensible. What he never names: active or suspected prostate or breast cancer, untreated severe sleep apnoea and elevated PSA are absolute contraindications, and PSA monitoring — which he omits — is mandatory (EAU Guidelines on Male Hypogonadism).
CheckMostly true — undersells what remains C 5/5 — The guest: cardiac disease is a case-by-case matter, not a blanket bar. TRAVERSE found non-inferiority for major adverse cardiac events, and the FDA removed the class-wide boxed cardiovascular warning on 28 February 2025 (FDA) — while adding a blood-pressure warning, the same trial finding more atrial fibrillation, VTE and acute kidney injury. "Case by case" is right; the risk he omits is real.
CheckMostly true (haematocrit) · Misleading (telmisartan) C 3/5 — The guest: testosterone raises red-cell production — "einer der Gründe, warum wir mehr Leistung haben. EPO, Blutdoping" — the same mechanism as EPO and blood doping — and thick blood plus testosterone is "theoretisch auch gefährlich", sometimes needing co-medication like telmisartan, "das hätte ohnehin vielleicht Vorteile" — which might help anyway. Erythrocytosis is documented and monitored. Telmisartan is licensed for hypertension; its PPAR-γ activity is mechanistic, not outcomes evidence (American Journal of Hypertension), and there is no basis for taking it absent hypertension.
CheckTrue (mechanism) · Open (TRT incidence) C 4/5 — The guest: DHT, not testosterone itself, drives baldness; 5-alpha-reductase activity is genetic; on TRT alone, hair loss is "verschwindend gering" — vanishingly small. The mechanism is well established (Cleveland Clinic). No TRT-specific incidence figure was located to check "vanishingly small" against.
CheckMostly true (hair, off-label) · Misleading (prostate) C 3/5 — The co-host: inside a careful passage on polypharmacy, he describes low-dose dutasteride to lower DHT, framed as a trade-off — floating that the combination might be "besser als vorher, weil eine DHT-Senkung auch das Prostatakrebsrisiko generell sinken kann". Dutasteride is approved only for benign prostatic hyperplasia; hair use is off-label everywhere, with documented risks of erectile dysfunction, reduced libido and gynaecomastia. On prostate cancer: PCPT and REDUCE found fewer overall diagnoses but more high-grade tumours, and the FDA rejected a prevention indication in 2011 (NCI; NEJM, REDUCE). The first edition said this was not in the transcript. It is — from the co-host, who hedges it himself.
CheckMostly true C 4/5 — The guest: a crying child lowers a father's testosterone — "nachweisbar… evolutionsbiologischer Mechanismus". Gettler et al. found a substantial decline in men who became fathers, largest among the most involved caregivers, read as a mating-versus-parenting trade-off (PNAS, 2011). What was measured is fatherhood generally, not a child's crying specifically.
CheckMisleading C 4/5 — The guest: average testosterone is falling "drastisch" across industrialised nations. Travison's cohort analysis found a real, age-independent secular decline across birth cohorts, substantially attributable to rising BMI (JCEM, 2007). Real, but "drastic" overstates a modest trend across decades; later cohorts are mixed.
CheckOpen — and it is his stated evidence standard C 2/5 — The guest: bodybuilders he coached "haben wunderweiß was mit ihrem Körper angestellt und sind im Nachhinein immer noch gesund und alt geworden" — did all sorts of things to their bodies and are still healthy and old afterwards. Not checkable as an anecdote. A population-level counterpoint exists: investigative reporting has documented elevated premature mortality among competitive bodybuilders (Washington Post, 2022).

Weber, Universitätsmedizin Mainz, media spokesman of the Deutsche Gesellschaft für Endokrinologie, states the position that cuts at the premise: the fall in hormones is the consequence of ageing, not its cause (DGE). The co-host's story is the episode's most persuasive evidence and its most exposed error in the first edition; the guest's own practice runs at or above guideline-described replacement, with one flat denial — on fertility — that a clinician would not let stand.

Group 2 — Women: hormones, testosterone, thyroid

This is the group the first edition did not have at all, and it holds the most safety-relevant new material in the second half. All thirteen claims here are the guest's own; a host raises it after many messages from women following an earlier episode at the Fibo trade fair.

CheckTrue (ratio) · Misleading (benefit) · Wrong (novelty) C 4/5 — The guest: post-menopausal testosterone for women is "unfassbar gut" — incredibly good; women naturally have more testosterone than oestrogen by molar amount; and a pharmacy compounds a gel to their "own prescription and formula," nobody else in Germany, he says. The ratio is correct. On benefit, the 2019 Global Consensus names one evidence-based indication — hypoactive sexual desire disorder after formal assessment, moderate effect — and advises against compounded testosterone for lack of efficacy and safety data (Global Consensus 2019; Davis & Wahlin-Jacobsen, 2015). On novelty, German compounding pharmacies already offer such gels (Einhorn-Apotheke).

His reframing of hormone-replacement caution for women in general is a live scientific debate compressed into a confident claim resting on two wrong supporting facts — worth saying precisely, because the reframe itself has genuine mainstream support.

CheckMisleading C 4/5 — The guest: doctors' reluctance to prescribe HRT rests on a "Fehlinterpretation der Women's Health Initiative" — a misreading of the WHI study, whose participants were "vermehrt Raucher" and used "synthetische Hormonformen, die heutzutage gar nicht mehr gegeben werden" — disproportionately smokers, on synthetic hormone forms no longer given today. The core reframe — WHI's headline results don't generalise to younger, recently menopausal women — is now mainstream, per the Menopause Society's 2022 statement (NAMS 2022). His two supporting facts are wrong: WHI participants were mostly former, not current, smokers, and CEE plus MPA are still manufactured and prescribed today, just less preferred.
CheckWrong C 4/5 — The guest: "jede Frau sollte sich spätestens mit 35 mal anschauen" — every woman should look into HRT by 35 at the latest. No guideline recommends universal consideration by that age. Menopausal hormone therapy is indicated for symptomatic menopause transition or diagnosed premature ovarian insufficiency; the Menopause Society explicitly rejects pre-emptive treatment of asymptomatic women years ahead of menopause (NAMS 2022).
CheckOpen C 2/5 — The guest: pregnenolone for wellbeing and energy, "eher bei Frauen als bei Männern" — more in women than men. Human evidence is confined to small trials in psychiatric populations; no robust randomised trial exists in healthy adults, and no sex-specific data were located (Marx et al.).
CheckWrong C 3/5 — The guest: low-dose DHEA for women on the pill with "mentale oder anderweitige Probleme" — mental or other problems — calling it a "Gegenspieler von Kortisol, zumindest ein Stück weit" — a cortisol antagonist, to some degree — and saying it must be tracked with bloodwork. The Endocrine Society's guideline recommends against generalised DHEA use in women: indications inadequate, efficacy and safety data lacking (JCEM 2014). "Cortisol antagonist" is a loose popularisation; his bloodwork caveat is sound regardless.
CheckWrong C 3/5 — The guest: reverse T3 "blockiert den Rezeptor… das T3 kann nicht gebildet werden" — blocks the receptor, T3 can't form — and T4 "wirkt einfach nicht". Reverse T3 is an inactive metabolite whose role is metabolic down-regulation during illness or starvation, not meaningful receptor blockade; "rT3 dominance" is an internet construct without accepted clinical use (CCJM, 2018).
CheckMisleading C 4/5 — The guest: "Konversionsstörung von T4 zu T3 öfter, als wir denken können" — a T4-to-T3 conversion disorder more common than thought. The 2012 ETA and 2014 ATA guidelines hold levothyroxine monotherapy as standard; combination therapy is experimental for persistent symptoms, and the DIO2-polymorphism evidence is mixed (ETA 2012). A contested minority hypothesis, presented as a frequent diagnosis.

c48 carries both the doctor reference the first edition said did not exist, and two checkable errors in the same sentence.

CheckWrong (both facts) — the doctor reference is real C 4/5 — The guest: "Also niemand verschreibt dir T3" — nobody prescribes you T3 — in Germany; at most a combination product such as "Novotyral 7510" [sic] — Novothyral, 75/10 as he gives it — and: "man kann z.B. mit seinem Arzt besprechen auf ein Komimedikament gesetzt zu werden" [sic] — one can discuss switching to a combination medicine with one's doctor. Liothyronine alone is licensed and prescribable in Germany, as Thybon Henning, 20 and 100 µg (Gelbe Liste). Novothyral is actually 75 µg T4 to 15 µg T3, not 75/10 (aponet). Both facts are wrong. What is not wrong, and what the first edition said did not exist: this is the one place in three hours he refers a listener to a physician.
CheckMisleading (suppression) · Wrong (trial figure) C 3/5 — The guest: "du machst dir mit T3 deine Schilddrüse kaputt, das existiert nicht" — the idea that T3 destroys your thyroid doesn't exist, citing "einen Medikamentenforscher" and studies "mit bis zu 150 Mikrogramm T3" where the only finding was more muscle breakdown. Exogenous T3 does suppress TSH and endogenous thyroid function through negative feedback, generally reversibly — "destroys" is the doctors' exaggeration he argues against; the flat denial is his own overcorrection. No 150 µg trial was located. The best-documented supraphysiological trial — 50–75 µg/day, 63 days, healthy men — found increased bone turnover, net skeletal calcium loss and suppressed parathyroid hormone, not only muscle loss (Smith 2003, Thyroid).
CheckWrong — safety-relevant C 3/5 — The guest: "Ich persönlich bin im Biohacking ein Fan davon geworden, in Ausnahmefällen T3 direkt einzusetzen… für eine leichte Stoffwechselerhöhung… geringere Nebenwirkungsquote" — a personal fan, in exceptional cases, of T3 directly for a mild metabolic boost, lower side-effect rate. No guideline supports T3 for metabolic enhancement in euthyroid people; its sharp peak carries more cardiac risk — tachycardia, atrial fibrillation — than T4 at equivalent effect, plus the bone effects under c49 (Smith 2003; ETA 2012).
CheckMostly true C 3/5 — The guest: "völlig absurd, wie viele junge Frauen" — completely absurd how many young women — are put on L-thyroxine early. German sources and the DEGAM S2k guideline describe levothyroxine as markedly over-prescribed, with caution urged on subclinical hypothyroidism (DEGAM S2k, 2024). No age-sex prevalence figure was located to check "wie viele" against.
CheckMisleading C 3/5 — The guest: iodine and selenium "fördert die Produktion dieser Enzyme" — promote deiodinase production. True for selenium — deiodinases are selenoenzymes; iodine is the substrate for hormone synthesis itself, not a driver of deiodinase production — folding both together overstates iodine's role (PubMed).
CheckMisleading C 2/5 — The guest: "die Todeskombination im Bodybuilding" — the death combination in bodybuilding — T3, clenbuterol and insulin, through potassium shifts that make "Titin steif" — titin stiff — and then the heart stops. The stimulant-plus-insulin-plus-diuretic pattern, with electrolyte shifts and arrhythmia, is a recognised cause of bodybuilder deaths (Spiller 2013). The titin-stiffening mechanism specifically was not found in the cardiology or toxicology literature — a real danger, given an invented mechanism.
Other viewsOn menopausal therapy, the Menopause Society's 2022 position statement both vindicates the guest's reframe — WHI's headline results do not generalise to younger, recently menopausal women — and falsifies his two supporting facts, in one document. On testosterone for women, the 2019 Global Consensus Position Statement (multi-society) names one evidence-based indication and explicitly does not recommend compounded preparations. On DHEA, the Endocrine Society's 2014 guideline recommends against generalised use in women; Prof. Sven Diederich's line from the DGE applies: long-term data are entirely absent. On thyroid, the European Thyroid Association 2012 and American Thyroid Association 2014 guidelines keep levothyroxine monotherapy as standard and call combination therapy experimental; the Cleveland Clinic Journal of Medicine (2018) treats "reverse T3 dominance" as an internet construct. Where the record supports the guest: the DEGAM S2k guideline 2024 and German professional press agree that levothyroxine is markedly over-prescribed, which is his c51. No named German endocrinologist was located responding specifically to the T3-for-metabolism claim in c50 — the correction there rests on the guideline documents and the Smith 2003 trial, not on a critic.

This group holds the piece's clearest safety flag: T3 used off-label for a "metabolic boost" in people without diagnosed thyroid disease, alongside a real but narrow doctor referral the first edition missed entirely.

Group 3 — Peptides

Credit the guest twice here, and mean it: the caveats attached to the bladder-pain study below are his own, offered unprompted, and his own dismissal of the human peptide literature understates a trial currently enrolling that might vindicate him.

CheckMostly true — his caveats are accurate C 3/5 — The guest: the bladder-pain-syndrome study — 12 women, all prior medication failed, a single 10 mg BPC-157 injection, 10 of 12 "zu 100% verschwunden", 2 of 12 "zu 80%"; no measurement, only a questionnaire; to a host's "Kontrollgruppe gab's wahrscheinlich auch nicht, ne?" — probably no control group either? — his "Gab's nicht" — there wasn't one; "wissenschaftlich nicht brauchbar" — not scientifically usable, but "ein interessantes Ergebnis" anyway. His own caveats match the study precisely.

CheckMostly true — undersells one trial C 3/5 — The guest: on the state of the human evidence, "Die Menschenstudien existieren. Sie sind nur lächerlich schlecht gemacht." — the human studies do exist, they're just laughably badly made. The human record is as thin as he says, on the whole. CheckMostly true (origin, sequence) · rodent-only (bone, neuro, bowel) C 3/5 — The guest: BPC-157 comes from gastric juice, is 15 amino acids long, "kann Knochenbrüche heilen… an Versuchen gesehen" — can heal bone fractures, seen in experiments — with potential neuroprotective effects and nasal-drop experimentation. CheckMostly true (mechanism, preclinical) · Unverifiable (hair) C 3/5 — The guest: TB-500, synthetic thymosin β4 from the thymus, drives angiogenesis through two pathways, inhibits apoptosis, and interacts with hair follicles — "nach einer Haartransplantation… keine so blöde Idee". RegeneRx's live thymosin β4 programme is an eye-drop formulation, Phase 3.

The legal position

Background law, not verdicts — carried unchanged from the first edition.

The episode never supplies the legal position; a German reader needs it. In Austria the medicines agency BASG published an official notice on 27 May 2026: many of the "peptides" offered online are classified as medicinal products without marketing authorisation, and buyers risk legal consequences (BASG). In Germany a "research use only" label carries no legal weight: under the Arzneimittelgesetz, function determines medicinal-product status, and a vendor's stated purpose is irrelevant. BPC-157 has no German or EU authorisation and personal import runs against §73 AMG. One legal-practice source states that TB-500 is listed under the Anti-Doping-Gesetz as a growth factor, making possession above a trivial quantity a criminal rather than administrative matter — single-sourced, flagged.

Pharmacy compounding is lawful as a mechanism — which is how a testosterone gel for a woman (c16) can be made against a named prescription — but for the peptides it is not available in practice: a German pharmacy may compound only from starting materials with certified pharmaceutical-grade documentation, and for most of these peptides no such material exists. Thymosin α1 is not authorised anywhere in the EU; the only lawful route would be a §73(3) AMG single-patient import through a licensed pharmacy on an individual prescription — doctor-initiated and case by case, not online ordering. No BfArM or PEI statement naming BPC-157, TB-500 or GHK-Cu was found; treat that as a gap, not as silence. What BfArM did publish, in 2025, is the risk information on testosterone prescribing cited in group 1.

Alpha-GPC (c34) sits under a different regime: EU novel-food law, Regulation 2015/2283, which applies in every member state — the guest's "not allowed in Germany" is EU-wide, and its availability from webshops in other member states reflects enforcement, not permission.

What survives

  • The guest's core deficiency case — a symptomatic man at the bottom of a range is a clinical picture, not only a printout — and his own concession that high-normal testosterone isn't longevity-promoting (c5), which holds up against the guideline literature.
  • The co-host's testosterone story, correctly reattributed: severe measured deficiency, an extreme dose tried, real life change — the episode's strongest evidence, once credited to him.
  • The guest's own caveats on the bladder-pain pilot (c17), his "we don't know" on long-term peptide safety (c28), and his admission that injected testosterone carries an abuse potential he did not resist himself (c4) — hedges that cost him commercially.
  • The reframe of women's hormone-therapy caution as partly resting on a misread trial (c42) — wrong on two supporting facts, right on the direction guidance has moved.
  • The critics' and regulators' caution where not overstated: the EAU's contraindication list (c8), the Menopause Society's rejection of pre-emptive HRT (c43), and the FDA's continuing blood-pressure warning after its boxed cardiovascular warning was removed (c9).

What to discount

  • The guest's flat denial that testosterone lowers fertility (c6) — the half's single most consequential error, against a well-established mechanism and WHO trial data.
  • The guest's mechanism claim for GHK-Cu (c22) and thymosin α1's regulatory status (c26) — confident language dressing up preclinical work or non-existent approval as settled fact.
  • The co-host's figures where his own data disagrees with the guest's framing — the underlying claims ("life completely changed", the dutasteride trade-off) still ask the reader to weigh a personal account, not a trial.
  • A host's borrowed authority — Attia's modafinil dose (c38), the drug-"toxicity" ranking (c41) — both misstate a checkable, public source.
  • Regulatory caution as a fixed point on either side: the FDA removed its boxed cardiovascular warning on testosterone in 2025 while keeping the blood-pressure one (c9), and BfArM's risk notice is from the same year — "the authorities say" has a shelf life of about a year in this field.
  • The compiler's own first edition — the coverage boundary and no-disclaimer findings withdrawn above. Both were false; both are why a second edition exists.
