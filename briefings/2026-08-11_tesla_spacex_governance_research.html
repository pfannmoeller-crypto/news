A Tesla–SpaceX merger: who would actually protect the minority?

Musk has pointed publicly to "overlap" between two companies he controls. No terms exist, and none may. But the question of what would happen if they did has a concrete answer — and it is not the one most commentary assumes, because both companies quietly left Delaware in 2024 and the legal machinery everyone still cites no longer applies to either of them.

The fact that changes the analysis

Correction to an earlier answer in this archive. On 10 August I wrote that a Tesla–SpaceX merger would fall under Delaware's entire fairness standard, requiring an independent special committee and a majority-of-the-minority vote under the MFW framework. That is wrong. Both companies are Texas corporations and have been since 2024. Delaware doctrine is a comparison, not the governing law. The error was not neutral: it described minority protections as materially stronger than they are.

SpaceX converted from Delaware to Texas on 14 February 2024 — two weeks after Chancellor McCormick rescinded Musk's pay package. Tesla followed on 21 June 2024, after a shareholder vote on 13 June that passed with 63% approval. Delaware courts have since held Tesla's reincorporation valid.

So a merger would be a Texas-to-Texas transaction. Under the internal affairs doctrine, each company's own state law governs its directors' duties — and here both sides sit under the same statute. There is no Delaware/Texas tension to arbitrate; there is simply no Delaware.

CheckConfirmed C 5/5 — SpaceX's conversion is documented in its Certificate of Conversion and restated formation certificate and confirmed in the S-1 filed for the June 2026 IPO. Tesla's is documented in the June 2024 proxy and the Certificate of Conversion filed with the Texas Secretary of State.

Every commentary that analyses this deal through MFW, entire fairness or Delaware SB 21 is analysing a jurisdiction neither company is in.

2

What Texas law actually provides

The baseline is TBOC § 21.418, which validates an interested-party transaction if it is disclosed and approved by disinterested directors, or approved by shareholders, or simply fair to the company. Structurally similar to Delaware — but without the decades of Chancery case law on how burdens shift and what independence means in practice.

Then came Texas SB 29, signed 14 May 2025. Three changes matter here:

ProvisionEffect
Business judgment rule codifiedApplies to duty of loyalty claims as well as duty of care — including conflicted transactions. In Delaware the BJR traditionally does not apply where a conflict exists; that is the whole point of entire fairness. Texas has reversed the default.
Committee safe harbour with judicial pre-clearanceA company may form a committee of disinterested directors and ask a Texas Business Court to declare them independent in advance. Law-firm analyses read that determination as dispositive — foreclosing the later, retrospective independence challenge that decided Tornetta.
Derivative-suit ownership thresholdCompanies may require a plaintiff to hold up to 3% of outstanding shares to bring a derivative claim (via HB 3699 amending TBOC § 21.552).
The provision with the largest practical effect. Texas authorised the 3% threshold on 14 May 2025. Tesla's board adopted the statutory maximum on 15 May 2025. A next-day amendment following enabling legislation is ordinary corporate practice and says nothing by itself about how it came about — what matters is the effect. At Tesla's market capitalisation that is roughly $30bn+ of stock required simply to have standing to sue. In practice that leaves Musk himself and a handful of index giants. A federal court has already enforced such a threshold to dismiss a small shareholder's claim.

Disputes would go to the Texas Business Courts, operating since September 2024 with jurisdiction over public-company governance matters at any amount in controversy — specialist judges, no jury, and no accumulated body of controller-transaction precedent to constrain them.

CheckConfirmed C 4/5 — SB 29 signed 14 May 2025; Tesla bylaw amendment 15 May 2025 at the statutory maximum. The one-day interval is documented. Not verified: whether SpaceX adopted an identical provision — plausible given the pattern, but I could not confirm it from a filing.
Other viewsDelaware moved in the same direction, which complicates any simple "Texas is the loose one" story. Delaware SB 21 (March 2025) amended DGCL § 144 to create a statutory safe harbour requiring an independent committee or a disinterested shareholder vote — disjunctive, where MFW required both — and defined a controller partly by a 33⅓% voting threshold. The Delaware Supreme Court upheld its constitutionality in early 2026. Charles Elson (Delaware, emeritus) called it "a total capitulation to Mr. Musk and controllers like him"; Lawrence Cunningham (Delaware) defended it as necessary to keep the state competitive. Both states relaxed. Texas simply went further, and only Texas offers pre-clearance of independence and a shareholding barrier to suing.
3

Three precedents, pointing different ways

SolarCity (2016) — the closest analogue, and Musk won

Tesla acquired SolarCity for $2.6bn in stock while Musk was chairman and roughly 22% owner of both. Shareholders sued. The Chancery Court assumed controller status in the plaintiffs' favour and applied entire fairness — the toughest standard available. In April 2022 Vice Chancellor Slights ruled for Musk, and the Delaware Supreme Court affirmed unanimously in June 2023.

The reasoning matters more than the result. Slights catalogued roughly eleven instances of problematic conduct — Musk dealt directly with management and advisers without board authorisation, and in the court's view should have withdrawn entirely rather than recusing selectively. Tesla formed no special committee at all, which the court called unexplained. What saved the deal was that Tesla voluntarily conditioned it on a majority-of-the-minority vote that Delaware law did not require, and an informed institutional shareholder base approved it overwhelmingly.

The lesson a lawyer would draw: the shareholder vote, not the committee, is what carried it. That is the playbook to expect.

Tornetta (2018–2025) — the findings that were never overturned

In January 2024 Chancellor McCormick rescinded Musk's 2018 pay package, finding him a controller of Tesla at 21.9% ownership on the strength of influence rather than arithmetic. Her findings on director independence were specific and damaging: Kimbal Musk is his brother; compensation-committee chair Ira Ehrenpreis a long-standing friend and co-investor; Antonio Gracias testified to holidays together, Christmases with the family, serving as best man at Kimbal's wedding, and a $1m loan he could not recall being repaid with interest. She also held that a 2024 ratification vote — with 77% approval and the opinion mailed to shareholders — could not cure a structurally conflicted process.

CheckReversed on remedy only C 3/5 — The Delaware Supreme Court's per curiam decision of 19 December 2025 vacated the rescission, reasoning that voiding six years of compensation was excessive and that the plaintiff bore the burden on the form of remedy. Reporting indicates the court expressly avoided disturbing the findings on control, director independence and the ineffectiveness of ratification. Musk received $1 in nominal damages; the fee award fell from $345m to roughly $54m. I could not read the full opinion, so the scope of what survives is taken from secondary analysis.

The xAI investment (2025–26) — the precedent nobody cites, and the most alarming

In November 2025 Tesla shareholders were asked to approve an investment in xAI. Counting abstentions as votes against per Tesla's own bylaws, it did not pass — 1.06bn for against 916m against plus abstentions. In January 2026 the board approved roughly $2bn into xAI anyway, over the reported objection of chair Robyn Denholm, on the reasoning that the instrument — a Series E preferred investment — differed from what shareholders had been asked to approve. How substantive that difference was could not be established from the filings, so it is reported as Tesla’s stated position rather than assessed.

Why this matters more than SolarCity. The SolarCity defence rests on the proposition that a shareholder vote is a meaningful check. The xAI episode is a direct, recent instance of a shareholder vote failing and the transaction proceeding regardless. Anyone relying on "shareholders will get a vote" should weigh what happened the last time they exercised one.

Antonio Gracias, whose testimony is quoted above, no longer sits on Tesla’s board — he is on SpaceX’s. His evidence matters here not because he would serve on a committee, but because it shows what a court accepts as proof that a director is not independent. That leaves two plausible candidates out of nine — and both are contested. New York State Comptroller Thomas DiNapoli publicly urged votes against Ehrenpreis, Gebbia and Wilson-Thompson in October 2025. AkademikerPension, explaining its exclusion of the stock, called Tesla the megacap "with the least independence on the board, which literally consists of friends and family."

At SpaceX the position is starker. The company claimed controlled-company status under Nasdaq rules at its IPO, which exempts it from having a majority-independent board, an independent compensation committee, and an independent nominating committee. It is not exempt from the SEC's independent audit-committee requirement — which is why Roelof Botha joined on 17 June 2026, days after listing. On the available record he is the one clearly independent director across either company.

CheckConfirmed C 4/5 — SpaceX: Musk holds ~42% economically and 82.4% of votes (final S-1/A at the $135 price), via Class B shares carrying 10 votes each, of which he holds 93.6%. Tesla: Musk's stake is ~20.3% per April 2026 filings — not the 28.4% widely repeated, which is his 2010 IPO figure. The 2025 pay package could lift him toward 25–29% as tranches vest; until they do, an irrevocable proxy votes them proportionally, so they add no voting power.

The counter-intuitive conclusion. Tesla shareholders — despite a board that a Delaware court found substantially non-independent — hold the only real leverage in this transaction, because Musk cannot outvote them. SpaceX public shareholders, who bought into the largest IPO in history ten weeks ago, have economic exposure and no vote, no class action and no jury. Individual arbitration remains available, and whether appraisal rights apply under this structure is unverified — but the collective remedies were waived in the charter before they bought.

Which inverts the intuitive reading of a merger. If SpaceX's 94× sales multiple is generous relative to Tesla's 11×, the party being disadvantaged on price would be the Tesla side — and that is the side with a vote. The SpaceX minority, holding the richer paper, has no say at all but arguably less to lose on relative value.

Other viewsCalPERS, the New York State Common Retirement Fund and the NYC Comptroller jointly raised the SpaceX governance structure before the IPO, objecting specifically to the arbitration and class-action waiver. They bought or declined with the terms fully disclosed — the S-1 runs 38 pages of risk factors and tells investors plainly they do not have the same protections as at a conventionally governed Nasdaq company. The fair counter-argument: none of this was hidden, the discount for it is in principle already in the price, and Musk has prevailed in every major related-party challenge so far — outright on appeal in SolarCity, and in Tornetta on the remedy rather than on the underlying liability findings, which were left standing. Governance risk that is disclosed and priced is a different thing from governance risk that is concealed.
6

Stepping back

Error direction. Only one substantive error occurred in producing this analysis, and it was mine: applying Delaware law to Texas corporations, which overstated minority protection. Every correction that followed from fixing it ran the same way — protections are weaker than the standard commentary assumes, not stronger. Worth flagging — though a single corrected premise does not establish a general pattern.

The related-party web is already large

This would not be the first transaction between these entities. Tesla's 2025 10-K disclosed roughly $573m of transactions across Musk-controlled companies; SpaceX paid Tesla about $405m for Megapacks in the first half of 2026 alone. A merger would not create the entanglement — it would formalise one that already exists and is disclosed piecemeal.

What would signal this is real

  • Formation of a special committee at Tesla — a disclosable event that necessarily precedes terms. Watch who is on it: if it is Hartung and Wilson-Thompson alone, the independence challenge writes itself. If Tesla adds new directors first, that is a sign of serious process design.
  • An application to a Texas Business Court for advance certification of committee independence under SB 29 — a step with no Delaware analogue, and the clearest tell that counsel is building a litigation-proof record.
  • Whether a majority-of-the-minority condition is announced ab initio rather than added later. SolarCity turned on that vote; its absence at the outset would be telling.
  • Whether SpaceX minority holders are offered anything at all — appraisal rights, a separate class vote, a collar. They cannot demand it, so any protection they receive would be voluntary.

What could not be substantiated

  • The full text of the December 2025 Delaware Supreme Court opinion — the scope of what survives on control and independence rests on secondary analysis.
  • Whether SpaceX adopted Tesla's 3% derivative-suit bylaw. Plausible, unconfirmed.
  • Whether appraisal rights are available under Texas law in this structure, and whether they can be waived by charter.
  • Whether SpaceX's Class B shares carry any sunset provision. No source confirmed one; absence of evidence, not evidence of absence.
  • The outcome of the DiNapoli/Lander shareholder proposal to repeal Tesla's 3% bylaw at the November 2025 meeting.
  • MSCI governance scores — only aggregator sources found, C 2/5, so not used.
  • Tesla's current lead independent director — the role exists in the governance guidelines; the incumbent could not be verified.

What survives

  • The forum question is the whole question. Texas incorporation plus SB 29 plus a 3% barrier to suing changes the analysis more than any deal term would.
  • Tesla's vote is the only hard constraint — reinforced by Nasdaq's 20%-issuance rule, which operates regardless of state law.
  • SolarCity shows the playbook and its limit: a voluntary majority-of-the-minority vote can carry a controller deal even without a committee.
  • The xAI episode shows the playbook's weakness: a shareholder vote against did not stop the transaction.

What to discount

Correctives in both directions — though more of them temper alarm than temper reassurance, which is itself a signal about where the loose talk on this topic sits.

  • Any analysis invoking MFW, entire fairness or Delaware SB 21 as governing law. Including, until this piece, mine.
  • "Shareholders will get a vote, so it's fine." True at Tesla, false at SpaceX, and the xAI precedent qualifies it even at Tesla.
  • The 28.4% figure for Musk's Tesla stake. It is his 2010 IPO holding and is still widely repeated.
  • The reflex that weak governance means the deal is improper. It does not. Musk has prevailed in these cases — SolarCity outright on appeal; Tornetta on the remedy, with the liability and independence findings left undisturbed and roughly $54m in plaintiff fees still awarded. Bad optics and legal liability are different things.
  • The reflex that disclosed governance risk is unfair to buyers. SpaceX's structure was in the prospectus, institutions objected publicly before the IPO, and investors bought anyway. That is a priced risk, not a hidden one.
  • Treating Texas as uniquely permissive. Delaware relaxed its own controller rules in 2025 and its Supreme Court upheld the change. The direction of travel is national.
