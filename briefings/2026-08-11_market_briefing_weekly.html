Records pause as oil reignites inflation fear and Intel dilutes to fund AI capex

Last week's edition closed on the S&P 500's best week in months, a weak jobs report having erased a hawkish Fed scare. This window is short — genuinely new ground since Monday's edition is really just Monday 10 and Tuesday-morning 11 August — but it already reverses part of that mood: US indices slipped off their records as oil jumped again on a harder Iranian line over the Strait of Hormuz, Intel sold $15 billion of stock in its first offering since going public in 1971, and Bitcoin gave back some of its jobs-report rally ahead of Wednesday's CPI print. The CLARITY Act's Senate momentum, read last edition as a breakthrough, turns out to be more contested than it looked: Polymarket's odds on the bill actually becoming law this year have kept falling.

US equitiesMon 10 Aug

Wall Street's four-session winning streak breaks as an oil spike and Intel's stock sale outweigh a quiet data day

The S&P 500 slipped 0.06% to 7,753, the Nasdaq Composite fell 0.32% to 26,605, and the Dow eased 0.11% to 53,976 on Monday, all pulling back from Friday's record closes. There was no scheduled US macro release to explain the move; instead, oil's jump on renewed Strait of Hormuz uncertainty and Intel's surprise $15 billion share sale (below) did the work. Energy stocks outperformed every other sector on the day, while technology and real estate shares fell the most; Nvidia pulled back in the same session that Meta closed up 0.48%.

Context & background: last edition closed on the S&P's strongest week in months, a rally built entirely on Friday's weak jobs report. Monday is the first red session since that report, and it did not need a new data point to happen — a geopolitical oil shock and a single company's balance-sheet decision were enough to interrupt a four-day run built on a single macro reading, which says something about how thin the conviction behind last week's rally actually was.

The details: the sector split is the tell — energy stocks (which benefit directly from a higher oil price) led, while technology and real estate (both rate- and cost-of-capital-sensitive) lagged, alongside Nvidia's pullback and Intel's post-offering slide. Meta's 0.48% gain in the same session is a reminder the move was rotational rather than a broad risk-off day: money moved between sectors more than it left the market.

Why it still matters: a single quiet-data, two-catalyst pullback of well under 1% does not undo a 3–5% weekly rally, but it does put Wednesday's CPI print in sharper relief — the last time oil and inflation fear moved the tape together this directly was the run-up to Friday's jobs report, which ended up mattering more than the Fed's own meeting the week before.

Macro · CPI previewahead of Wed 12 Aug, 8:30am ET

Wednesday's July CPI becomes the week's real test, and forecasters can't agree how much core inflation accelerates

The Bureau of Labor Statistics releases July's Consumer Price Index at 8:30am ET on Wednesday 12 August — outside this edition's window, but already the dominant forward-looking story of the week. Consensus puts headline CPI up 0.1% month-on-month and 3.4% year-on-year; core CPI forecasts are less settled, with surveyed banks ranging from roughly 0.1% to 0.32% month-on-month and converging closer to 2.5% year-on-year. Prediction-market pricing, per CNBC's review of Kalshi contracts, leans toward a tamer-than-feared print.

Semiconductors · IntelMon 10 Aug

Intel raises $15 billion in its first stock sale since going public in 1971, and the market prices in the dilution immediately

Intel announced an underwritten public offering of $15 billion in common stock on Monday — the company's first share sale since its original 1971 listing. Shares fell 4.6% on the news. The base deal implies roughly 3.1% dilution for existing shareholders, rising to about 3.5% if underwriters fully exercise their option; proceeds are earmarked for "general corporate purposes, capital expenditure and working capital," and the raise equals roughly 75% of Intel's projected $20 billion 2026 capital-spending budget for its AI and manufacturing build-out.

Context & background: Intel's stock had almost tripled over the course of 2026 on AI-buildout optimism before Monday's announcement, per the Motley Fool's coverage — the classic setup for a company to sell equity while its currency is strong rather than while it's cheap. Raising capital this way, instead of through debt, is itself informative about how Intel's board is weighing its balance sheet against the scale of AI-era capital spending.

The details: a roughly 3% actual dilution producing a 4.6% share-price drop is a bigger reaction than the mechanical dilution alone would justify, which is consistent with the market also pricing in a signal — that Intel's own free cash flow isn't covering its stated AI and manufacturing capex plans without external funding, even after a year of share-price strength. The offering size equaling three-quarters of the full-year capex budget is a specific, sourced comparison this briefing had not previously had for Intel.

Why it still matters: this is the same capex-versus-monetization tension this briefing has tracked across Meta and AMD in recent editions, but expressed through a financing decision rather than an earnings reaction — another AI-infrastructure name choosing to raise fresh capital rather than fund its build-out entirely from operations, at a moment when Monday's broader tape (below) was already trading cautiously on inflation risk.

Iran · HormuzMon 10 Aug

Oil jumps again as Iran ties any Hormuz reopening to the US lifting its naval blockade first

Brent settled at $87.72 and WTI at $82.13 on Monday, both roughly 5% higher on the session and up 7.1% and 6.7% respectively from Friday's closes. Iran's Foreign Ministry spokesman said explicitly that "as long as the US naval blockade continues, the necessary conditions for the reopening of the Strait of Hormuz do not exist" — a harder, more publicly stated precondition than the ambiguous draft plan this briefing reported last week. A June 17 memorandum of understanding to reopen Hormuz to commercial shipping, not previously detailed in this briefing, collapsed after fighting broke out over which shipping routes vessels could use.

The Senate confirms there's no August vote on the CLARITY Act after all, and Polymarket's odds keep sliding

Despite Majority Leader John Thune's Saturday cloture filing — read in last edition as the bill's "first real procedural movement in weeks" — the Senate confirmed on 6 August that it will not vote on the CLARITY Act before its recess. The chamber returns 14 September with roughly three working weeks before the bill needs 60 votes (all Republicans plus at least seven Democrats). Polymarket's "Clarity Act signed into law in 2026?" market, on which nearly $6 million had traded as of 11 August, has fallen to 16% — down from a February peak of 82%.

Context & background: last week's edition read Thune's cloture filing as genuine forward motion. The details: per crypto.news, the substantive sticking point is a bipartisan ethics provision targeting government officials holding over $1 million in crypto, which Democrats have rejected in the form of a White House-backed compromise. Why it still matters: with only about three working weeks after the 14 September return before midterm politics start crowding the floor, per crypto.news, the market-implied probability suggests traders see the September window itself — not just August's recess — as the genuine risk to passage. That reframes last week's "first real movement" headline as necessary but not close to sufficient.

lightbulbEditor's view

A short week still found two ways to unsettle last week's conviction: oil, and a stock sale

1. Last week's rally needed remarkably little to interrupt it. No US macro release moved Monday's tape — a geopolitical oil headline and one company's financing decision were enough to snap a four-session winning streak built on a single jobs report. That is not a reversal, but it is a reminder of how thin the conviction behind records can be.

2. Oil is now doing double duty as a geopolitical and a domestic-inflation story. Iran's explicit precondition — lift the blockade before Hormuz reopens — hardened the standoff Monday, and per Kiplinger's CPI-preview coverage, that same oil move is a direct input into Wednesday's inflation print. The two threads this briefing has tracked separately for weeks just merged.

3. AI-capex financing is starting to show up as a market story in its own right. Intel's $15 billion share sale — its first since 1971 — is the same capex-versus-monetization tension this briefing flagged in Meta's and AMD's earnings reactions, now expressed as a financing decision rather than an earnings miss.

4. Gold's driver appears to have flipped from rate-cut optimism to inflation hedging in the space of one session. Last week gold rallied with stocks on dovish jobs data; Monday it rallied while stocks fell, on oil-driven inflation fear. If that holds, Wednesday's CPI matters to gold for a different reason than it matters to the Fed's September decision.

5. "First real movement" and "a punt" were both true about the CLARITY Act, and the market sided with "punt." Thune's cloture filing was genuine procedural progress; Polymarket's odds falling to 16% from a February peak of 82% says traders think the underlying ethics-provision fight, not just the recess calendar, is the real obstacle to passage.

6. Prediction markets called three of four resolved earnings correctly this week — and still missed the one story that actually moved a stock. The AMD beat market got the beat right and had nothing to say about the "not exceptional enough" reaction that actually drove the share price. Confidently right and still incomplete is its own lesson.

Everything this window points at Wednesday: the CPI print now sits at the center of the equity pause, gold's flipped driver, Bitcoin's pullback and the Fed's September dissenters all at once — a rare single release with that much riding on it across every asset class this briefing covers.
