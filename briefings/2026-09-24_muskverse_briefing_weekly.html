Grok 4.7 ships at last as regulators escalate on Cybercab and Starship slips a fourth time

This edition's clearest movement is a model that finally shipped and two deadlines that got firmer. On 21 September, xAI released Grok 4.7 — over two months after Musk's original "four weeks" promise in late July, and after three further missed windows this briefing has tracked in turn. The same week, the safety regulator investigating Tesla's Cybercab robotaxi escalated from an open-ended audit to a formal special order (a binding demand for sworn, on-the-record answers, backed by financial and criminal penalties for ignoring it): Tesla must answer 21 questions under oath by 30 September or face civil penalties running into the hundreds of millions of dollars, and separate criminal exposure. Starship — SpaceX's next-generation rocket — was due to fly again on 22 September, a target already reset twice; it missed that date too, and SpaceX pushed it a further six days, to no earlier than 28 September, with reporting this week tying the delay to a still-pending flight licence from the US aviation regulator. Away from those three threads, Musk's political spending, his two open legal fights beyond the Apple dismissal (X Corp and xAI's antitrust suit against Apple, dropped last edition — see Status quo, below), and his two quietest ventures — the tunnelling company and the brain-implant company — carried over unchanged from the previous edition.

Tesla

TESLARegulator escalatesspecial order 10 Sept, due 30 Sept 2026

Safety regulator orders Tesla to answer 21 sworn questions on Cybercab's certification by 30 September

On 10 September the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration's chief counsel, Peter Simshauser, issued a formal special order compelling Tesla to answer 21 detailed questions, sworn under oath by a responsible company officer, about how it certified the Cybercab as meeting federal vehicle safety rules despite the car having no foot-operated brake pedal, steering wheel, mirrors or turn signals. Tesla must respond by 30 September; noncompliance carries civil penalties of roughly $27,900 per day, capped at about $139.35 million, plus separate criminal penalties of up to 15 years in prison.

The order sharpens the audit the regulator opened on 3 September, days after Tesla began commercial Cybercab rides in Austin, Texas, covering an estimated 1,000 vehicles. Its stated objection is that Tesla "could not certify" a vehicle lacking foot-operated brake controls to Federal Motor Vehicle Safety Standard 135, the federal rule governing service brakes, without a formal exemption — the kind of exemption a rival robotaxi maker, Zoox, obtained in July, capping it at 2,500 such vehicles a year. Tesla has not disclosed a comparable cap or a pending exemption request.

Context & background. The previous edition described this only as an open audit with no findings or timeline. A special order is a distinct, stronger regulatory tool than the audit query that opened it: it compels sworn, individually attributable answers rather than voluntary cooperation, and it is the step a regulator takes when it wants a documented, legally binding record rather than informal correspondence.

The details. The 21 questions target exactly the design choices that make the Cybercab different from a standard car: touchscreen-only controls in place of a steering wheel and pedals, no side mirrors, and no separate turn-signal stalk. The regulator's stated position is that Federal Motor Vehicle Safety Standard 135 requires a foot-operated service brake unless a manufacturer secures an exemption; Zoox's comparable exemption, granted 28 July, caps its exempted robotaxi at 2,500 vehicles a year, a volume ceiling Tesla's self-certified approach does not carry.

Why it still matters. The shift from an open audit to a special order converts an information-gathering exercise into one with a hard deadline and real financial and personal legal exposure for the Tesla officer who signs the sworn response. If the regulator concludes the Cybercab was not properly certified, the likely next steps are a recall order, a demand that Tesla seek a Zoox-style exemption, or both — either of which would affect a vehicle already carrying paying passengers.

Watch items. Whether Tesla files its sworn response by 30 September, whether it pauses or scales back Cybercab rides in the meantime, and whether the regulator upgrades the matter from an audit into a formal investigation.

Grok 4.7 finally ships, two months late, and lands just behind the frontier leader on benchmarks

xAI released Grok 4.7 on 21 September — a model this briefing has tracked missing its release window three times running: "four weeks" on 24 July, "three to four weeks" again on 12 August, and "ten days" on 2 September, before Musk instead named a further model, Grok 4.8, on 13 September without shipping 4.7 at all. The version that finally shipped carries 2.1 trillion parameters, about 40% more than August's Grok 4.6, and xAI describes it as built for coding, multi-step agent tasks (the system working through several steps on its own, rather than answering in a single reply) and knowledge work, with part of its training data drawn from SpaceX's own satellite telemetry (the automated performance data satellites and rockets radio back about themselves) and engineering logs. It is priced at $2 per million input tokens and $6 per million output tokens (tokens are the small chunks of text — roughly ¾ of a word each — that a model reads and writes, and pricing is set per million of them), undercutting rival frontier models (the most advanced AI systems available at a given moment).

On xAI's own published benchmarks, Grok 4.7 places second behind Fable 5.1, a rival flagship model, across several tests of coding and professional knowledge work, and ahead of OpenAI's GPT (generative pre-trained transformer, the technology underlying most AI chatbots)-5.6 Sol, a competing model from a different AI lab. That is a real, shipped model rather than another promise — but it arrives running behind the leading model it was originally promised to "surpass," at a moment when its own maker had already downgraded the claim attached to it twice.

Context & background. This resolves a thread this briefing has carried across four editions: a documented pattern of Musk announcing, then missing, a Grok 4.7 release window, followed by his 13 September pivot to naming Grok 4.8 as the next model rather than shipping 4.7. Grok 4.7 shipped six days after that pivot, under its original name after all — over two months past the "four weeks" Musk first promised on 24 July.

The details. xAI's own release notes describe Grok 4.7 as working longer on difficult problems and checking its own work more carefully than earlier versions, the product of extended reinforcement learning — a further stage of training that adjusts a model's behaviour on hard, time-consuming problems after its initial training run. It scored 46.3% on CursorBench, a benchmark that tests how well a model writes and edits real code, which xAI says gives it the best price-to-performance ratio among the models tested, alongside results in the high 50s to low 70s (out of 100) on other coding and professional-knowledge tests. A faster response variant is available at double the standard price.

Why it still matters. xAI now has a real, priced, shipped model to show for the months of slipped dates — but the underlying competitive gap the delays were meant to close has not closed: Grok 4.7 sits second to Fable 5.1 on the same tests xAI itself chose to publish. The company's strategy now leans on price rather than outright capability, and on Grok 4.8 and a further update after it, Grok 4.9 — both already in progress, per Musk's 14 September comments — to make the larger leap.

Watch items. Whether Grok 4.8, which Musk has called a "meaningful step up," ships closer to its own timeline than 4.7 did, and whether independent, non-xAI benchmark results confirm the ranking xAI has published.

Starship's next flight slips a fourth time, from 22 September to no earlier than 28 September

The 22 September target the previous edition reported — itself the third slip this briefing has tracked for this flight — passed without a launch. On 17 September, SpaceX pushed the target a further six days, to no earlier than 28 September, without stating a reason at the time; reporting later in the window tied the delay to a flight licence still pending from the Federal Aviation Administration, the US regulator that licenses commercial rocket launches, which Flight 14 (the programme's fourteenth test flight) needs because it is attempting a genuine orbit rather than the suborbital arcs (up-and-down test flights that go to space briefly without circling the planet) SpaceX has flown to date. Starship flies in two parts — a Super Heavy booster on the bottom and a Starship upper stage on top. As of 23 September, SpaceX had stacked them (physically joined the upper stage, Ship 41, onto the booster, Booster 21) at the launch pad, a visible step toward the new date, though one that does not by itself confirm the licence has been granted.

Flight 14 is intended as Starship's first attempt at reaching a genuine Earth orbit: it would circle the planet roughly six times at about 275 kilometers before a Pacific Ocean splashdown near Chile, carrying an estimated 26 next-generation Starlink (SpaceX's satellite-internet network) satellites that would be put into active service for the first time on this flight, up from the roughly 20 reported in earlier planning.

Context & background. This is the fourth dated slip this briefing has tracked for this specific flight: a satellite-deployment filing (the schedule SpaceX submits to the Federal Communications Commission for its Starlink satellites) giving a 15 September date, an aviation-planning entry (the same kind of Federal Aviation Administration airspace notice described above) giving an 18–19 September window, a company-described 22 September target, and now a further push to no earlier than 28 September. Each of the first three passed without a launch.

The details. Unlike Starship's thirteen previous test flights, which flew suborbital arcs and came back down within roughly an hour, Flight 14 is built to insert the upper stage into a real orbit for around ten hours across six passes around Earth, testing the heat shield and re-entry systems under a full orbital return. Licensing a genuine-orbit flight involves more Federal Aviation Administration review than a suborbital one, which is the reporting's stated explanation for why this slip, unlike the earlier ones, has a specific external cause attached to it rather than an unexplained internal schedule change.

Why it still matters. An actual orbital insertion remains the technical milestone SpaceX has been building toward across this entire flight-test campaign. A fourth slip, now explicitly tied to a regulatory step outside SpaceX's own control, is a different kind of delay than the previous three, which this briefing could not attribute to any stated cause.

Watch items. Whether the Federal Aviation Administration issues the flight licence in time for 28 September, and whether the flight, once it launches, reaches its planned orbit given the added difficulty of a full re-entry.

edit_noteEditor's view

One promise finally kept, one regulatory threat sharpened, one slip explained for the first time

1. Grok 4.7's release closes a thread rather than extends it. After three missed windows this briefing tracked in turn, xAI shipped a real, priced, benchmarked model on 21 September — but it lands second to the rival model it was originally meant to surpass, so the release resolves the timing question without resolving the competitive one.

2. Tesla's regulatory exposure just became concrete. An open audit with no deadline and no stated penalty became, this week, a sworn special order with a 30 September deadline and a specific dollar figure — roughly $139 million — attached to noncompliance. That is a materially different kind of regulatory risk than the one the previous edition described.

3. Starship's fourth slip is the first one this briefing can attribute to a specific cause. The first three slips arrived with no stated reason; this one is tied, in reporting, to a still-pending flight licence from the Federal Aviation Administration — a narrower, more trackable obstacle than an unexplained internal schedule change.

4. Three threads sat exactly still. Musk's political spending, his bid to pause Minnesota's law, and his dormant appeal against Sam Altman all carried over from the previous edition with no new figures, filings or rulings.

5. Two genuinely quiet ventures stayed quiet. The Boring Company and Neuralink — the latter now quiet for a fifth consecutive edition — produced no new dated development this window, stated here plainly rather than padded into cards that would not hold up.

The next edition should carry: whether Tesla's sworn response, due 30 September, changes the Cybercab's operating status; whether Starship actually flies near 28 September and reaches its planned orbit; and whether Grok 4.8 ships closer to its own timeline than 4.7 did.
