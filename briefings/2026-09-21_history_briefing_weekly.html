CONFLICT2016Sep 26, 2016

Colombia and FARC sign a peace deal ending a 52-year war

President Juan Manuel Santos and FARC commander Rodrigo Londoño, known as Timochenko, signed the accord in Cartagena on September 26, 2016, formally ending Latin America's longest-running internal conflict. Londoño offered a public apology, calling it "a new era of reconciliation."

The deal did not survive first contact with voters: a national plebiscite on October 2 rejected it by a razor-thin 50.2% to 49.8%, forcing a renegotiated text that Congress ratified that November instead.

Context & background. FARC (the Revolutionary Armed Forces of Colombia) took up arms in 1964 as a peasant Marxist insurgency; over five decades it funded itself through kidnapping, extortion and cocaine trafficking while the state, right-wing paramilitaries and the guerrillas fought a war that killed roughly 220,000 people between 1958 and 2013, four-fifths of them civilians, and displaced more than five million. Formal talks between Santos's government and FARC began in Havana in 2012, mediated by Cuba and Norway with Venezuela and Chile as guarantors, and ran for nearly four years before a text was finalised in August 2016.

The details. The Cartagena ceremony was staged for global television: white doves, a musical performance, and dignitaries including UN Secretary-General Ban Ki-moon and US Secretary of State John Kerry among the witnesses. The accord covered rural land reform, political participation for a demobilised FARC as a party, ending the drug trade in guerrilla-held areas, victims' reparations and transitional justice. Santos had bet the government's legitimacy on ratifying it directly by referendum rather than through Congress alone — a decision that nearly sank the whole process when the "No" campaign, led by former president Álvaro Uribe, narrowly won on October 2.

Why it still matters. Santos won the Nobel Peace Prize the week after the referendum defeat, an unusual case of the prize going to a process still in crisis. The renegotiated accord passed Congress in November 2016 without a second popular vote, and FARC formally disarmed and became a political party in 2017 — though a minority of dissident fighters rejected the deal and remain active, and implementation of land and reparations provisions is still incomplete a decade on.

CULTURE2016Sep 24, 2016

Smithsonian opens its National Museum of African American History and Culture

President Obama cut the ribbon on September 24, 2016 to open the Smithsonian's 400,000-square-foot museum on the National Mall, thirteen years after Congress legislated it into existence and four after groundbreaking.

Its bronze, three-tiered "corona" facade — inspired by Yoruba caryatid crowns — was designed to stand out against the Mall's white stone institutions, and the opening drew a week-long celebration led by musicians, historians and surviving civil-rights figures.

Context & background. President George W. Bush signed the legislation establishing the museum in 2003, following a campaign that traced back to Black Civil War veterans lobbying for a memorial in 1915. Architect David Adjaye's design, chosen in 2009, sank three below-grade levels devoted to the history of slavery and segregation beneath five above-ground levels built around contemporary culture, community and sport, with "lenses" in the corona framing views of the Washington Monument and White House.

The details. Inaugural holdings included a slave cabin relocated from a South Carolina plantation, a Pullman-era segregated railway car, Nat Turner's Bible, and Chuck Berry's Cadillac, alongside Emmett Till's casket and artefacts tied to Muhammad Ali and Michael Jackson. Obama's remarks framed the museum as "a clearer view of the American experience," and the opening ceremony ran alongside a free, week-long "Freedom Sounds" festival on the Mall.

Why it still matters. It became the fastest-growing Smithsonian property by visitor demand, requiring timed passes for years afterward, and its exhibits on segregation and racial violence have repeatedly become reference points in later fights over how American history is taught in schools and other museums.

SPORT2016Sep 25, 2016

Arnold Palmer, golf's "King," dies at 87 in Pittsburgh

Palmer died September 25, 2016 of heart complications while awaiting surgery at UPMC Shadyside in Pittsburgh, weeks after a public appearance at his own Bay Hill event had already worried fans with how frail he looked.

He retired with 62 PGA Tour wins and seven majors, but his lasting legacy is commercial: he turned professional golf into a television sport and a licensing business, a template every subsequent golf star has followed.

Context & background. Born in Latrobe, Pennsylvania, the son of a golf-course groundskeeper and pro, Palmer arrived as television began covering golf nationally in the late 1950s, and his hard-charging, go-for-broke style built "Arnie's Army," a fan following that crossed class lines in a sport still coded upper-crust.

The details. With agent Mark McCormack he effectively invented modern athlete marketing — equipment lines, apparel, a namesake drink (iced tea and lemonade, mass-produced from 2001), and ownership of the Bay Hill Club that still hosts a PGA Tour event named for him. He co-founded the Golf Channel and designed or co-designed more than 300 golf courses worldwide.

Why it still matters. Tributes from Jack Nicklaus, Tiger Woods and sitting and former presidents underscored a rare case of a retired athlete remaining the sport's central commercial figure two decades after his last competitive win; the drink and the tournament both still carry his name a decade after his death.

POLITICS2016Sep 26, 2016

Record 84 million watch the first Clinton–Trump debate at Hofstra

NBC's Lester Holt moderated the first of three general-election debates on September 26, 2016 at Hofstra University, drawing 84 million television viewers across 13 networks — a Nielsen record that beat the 80.6 million who watched Carter and Reagan in 1980.

The 90-minute, six-segment format covered the economy, race and cybersecurity, and set the combative tone — interruptions, fact-check sparring, a sniffling controversy — that carried through the rest of the campaign.

Context & background. It was the first debate for both candidates in this race, staged with national polls essentially tied. The Commission on Presidential Debates set a six-segment, 15-minutes-each format with topics announced in advance, a structure meant to encourage direct exchange rather than a string of opening statements.

The details. Clinton opened by noting "we now have the largest audience in the history of American presidential debates," according to contemporaneous estimates put around 100 million counting all platforms; the 84 million Nielsen figure covered linear TV alone, with millions more on YouTube, Facebook and other streams. Exchanges over Trump's tax returns, the "birther" controversy and Clinton's stamina dominated post-debate coverage.

Why it still matters. The 84-million figure held as the modern presidential-debate viewership record for years afterward, and the debate's tone — heavy interruption, real-time fact-checking pressure on moderators — became the template critics and defenders alike cite when arguing over how general-election debates should be run.

DISASTER2001Sep 21, 2001

Chemical-plant blast kills 31 and injures thousands in Toulouse

An explosion at the AZF fertiliser plant on the edge of Toulouse killed 31 people and injured roughly 2,500 on September 21, 2001, ten days after the September 11 attacks in the US — and, for a tense first day, feared by many in France to be connected to them.

It remains one of Europe's worst industrial accidents since Seveso, and a 2006 official investigation attributed it to an accidental mixing of two stored chemicals rather than sabotage, though the finding has been contested in French courts ever since.

Context & background. AZF (Azote de Fertilisants), a subsidiary of what became TotalFinaElf, produced nitrogen fertiliser on a site inside the city rather than on an isolated industrial perimeter — itself later cited as a planning failure. The blast happened at mid-morning on a Friday, ten days after 9/11, and initial fears in Toulouse of a deliberate attack briefly disrupted the city before investigators pointed to an industrial accident.

The details. The official 2006 finding held that sodium dichloroisocyanurate, used to purify swimming-pool water elsewhere on site, was mistakenly mixed into a pile of roughly 500 kilograms of off-spec ammonium nitrate around twenty minutes before the blast, triggering the explosion. Around 70 people suffered eye injuries from shattered glass across the city, and the shockwave was felt for kilometres.

Why it still matters. France passed a new industrial-risk-zoning law (the PPRT framework) directly in response, tightening buffer zones around hazardous plants under the EU's Seveso directive. The cause is still legally disputed — Total's original conviction was overturned on appeal and the case has been retried multiple times — a reminder, sharpened again after Beirut's 2020 ammonium-nitrate disaster, of how differently the same chemical can be handled and stored.

CULTURE2001Sep 21, 2001

Global telethon "America: A Tribute to Heroes" raises over $200 million

A commercial-free, 35-network simulcast aired live from studios in Los Angeles, New York and London on the evening of September 21, 2001, ten days after the attacks, raising more than $200 million for the United Way's September 11 Telethon Fund.

Bruce Springsteen, U2, Stevie Wonder, Paul Simon and Willie Nelson performed while Tom Hanks, George Clooney and Muhammad Ali delivered spoken tributes, with celebrities themselves staffing the phone banks taking donations.

Context & background. Organised in barely ten days by entertainment-industry figures led by George Clooney, the broadcast was designed to run with no commercial interruption across ABC, CBS, NBC, Fox and dozens of cable outlets simultaneously — an unprecedented coordination among competing US networks.

The details. About 21 acts performed across the three linked studios; Neil Young and Eddie Vedder worked phone lines between their own performances. US viewership was estimated around 60 million, with the broadcast distributed onward to more than 210 countries, and proceeds went to the United Way's dedicated September 11 fund for victims' families and first responders.

Why it still matters. It set the modern template for the star-studded, multi-network disaster telethon — repeated for Hurricane Katrina in 2005, Haiti's earthquake in 2010 and the COVID-19 pandemic's "One World: Together at Home" in 2020 — and remains a reference point for how quickly the entertainment industry can be mobilised around a national tragedy.

POLITICS2001Sep 23, 2001

Bush signs order freezing assets of terror-linked groups worldwide

Twelve days after the September 11 attacks, President Bush signed Executive Order 13224 on September 23, 2001, authorising the Treasury, State and Justice departments to block the US assets of any foreign person or group that commits or materially supports terrorism.

It created what became the Specially Designated Global Terrorist list, the legal architecture the US has used ever since to freeze money tied to groups from al-Qaeda to, later, ISIS.

Context & background. The order was the financial front of the "war on terror" Bush had declared in his September 20 address to Congress, invoking the International Emergency Economic Powers Act and the National Emergencies Act to reach beyond the immediate al-Qaeda network to anyone the administration deemed to be providing material support.

The details. It let Treasury's Office of Foreign Assets Control block a target's US assets immediately, without prior notice, and extended the same freeze to banks and charities found to be channelling funds to designated groups. It also gave the president authority to restrict even humanitarian donations of food, clothing and medicine where they were judged to risk aiding a national-emergency threat.

Why it still matters. The order and its designee list became a permanent instrument of US foreign policy, invoked repeatedly in the two decades since against groups ranging from Hezbollah to ISIS-linked financiers, while also drawing sustained criticism from civil-liberties groups over due-process for US-based charities caught up in its freezes.

CONFLICT1976Sep 21, 1976

Car bomb kills Chilean exile Orlando Letelier in Washington

A remote-detonated car bomb killed former Chilean foreign minister Orlando Letelier and his American colleague Ronni Moffitt, 25, at Sheridan Circle on Washington's Embassy Row on the morning of September 21, 1976 — an assassination later proven to have been ordered by Augusto Pinochet himself.

It stands as the only known case of a foreign government's secret police carrying out a political assassination on US soil, and it took nearly two decades of US and Chilean investigation to reach the men who ordered it.

Context & background. Letelier had served as Salvador Allende's ambassador to Washington and then foreign minister before Pinochet's 1973 coup; exiled and stripped of citizenship, he had become the Pinochet government's most visible critic in the US, working at the Institute for Policy Studies alongside Moffitt.

The details. A magnetic explosive device was attached beneath Letelier's car; it detonated at around 9:30 a.m. as he drove past the Chilean embassy compound with Moffitt and her husband Michael, who survived. DINA operative Michael Townley, later extradited from Chile, organised the bombing with anti-Castro Cuban exiles recruited as the actual triggermen, and eventually pleaded guilty and testified against them.

Why it still matters. Declassified US intelligence later confirmed Pinochet personally ordered the killing as part of Operation Condor, the coordinated assassination program among South American right-wing dictatorships; DINA chief Manuel Contreras and his deputy Pedro Espinoza were convicted in Chile in 1993, though Pinochet himself was never charged before his 2006 death. The case became a template for later transnational human-rights prosecutions and for US sanctions targeting foreign state assassins.

POLITICS1976Sep 23, 1976

Ford and Carter's first debate goes silent for 27 minutes on live TV

Gerald Ford and Jimmy Carter met at Philadelphia's Walnut Street Theatre on September 23, 1976 for the first US presidential debate since Kennedy and Nixon in 1960 — and partway through, the audio feed cut out entirely, leaving both candidates standing at their podiums in silence for roughly 27 minutes while technicians worked.

The failure happened as Carter answered a question on oversight of intelligence agencies; neither man moved from his lectern until sound was restored, and the debate resumed as if nothing had happened.

Context & background. No sitting president and challenger had debated on television in sixteen years; the FCC's equal-time rule, which had effectively barred two-candidate televised debates by requiring air time for every minor-party candidate too, was reinterpreted in 1975 to allow broadcasters to treat a debate as a "bona fide news event," clearing the way for the League of Women Voters to sponsor the 1976 series.

The details. Three journalists posed questions on domestic and economic policy, with 3-minute initial answers and 2-minute follow-ups. As the sound cut out during Carter's answer, moderator Edwin Newman later apologised on air for "the technical failure that lost the sound as it was leaving the theatre," but both campaigns agreed afterward the standoff had not been used to signal to either candidate.

Why it still matters. The silent standoff became one of the most-replayed clips in debate history, a preview of how live-TV mishaps could outlast the substance of a debate in public memory — a dynamic repeated two weeks later when Ford's second-debate claim that Eastern Europe was not under Soviet domination overshadowed everything else said that campaign.

Editor's view

The honest thread across these three weeks is how much of each era's "ending" turned out to be provisional. Colombia's 2016 peace deal was rejected by voters five days after the cameras left Cartagena and had to be rewritten; Rhodesia's white-minority government spent three more years fighting the war it had already, on paper, agreed to lose. Even Letelier's killers took until 1993 to be convicted, and only in Chile. History's round anniversaries tend to fall on the signing, the ribbon-cutting or the broadcast — the tidy moment — while the actual resolution, if it comes, lands somewhere else on the calendar entirely.
