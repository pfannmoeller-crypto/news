BUSINESS201622 September 2016

Yahoo admits a hack from late 2014 exposed 500 million user accounts

On 22 September 2016, the internet company Yahoo confirmed that hackers had stolen personal data — names, email addresses, phone numbers, dates of birth and scrambled passwords — from at least 500 million user accounts, in an intrusion Yahoo said had actually happened in late 2014. At the time it was the largest data breach any company had ever disclosed.

The admission landed two months after the telecommunications company Verizon had agreed to buy Yahoo's core internet business, and it turned Yahoo into a textbook case of a hidden security failure surfacing in the middle of a company sale.

Context & background. Yahoo had been losing ground to Google and Facebook for years and had put itself up for sale in 2016; Verizon's offer of $4.8 billion for Yahoo's search and email business, agreed in July 2016, was meant to be the tidy end of that decline. Yahoo said the 2014 intrusion was carried out by what it believed to be a state-sponsored actor, though it never named a country. The company had known about suspicious activity for some time before the September 2016 disclosure, and separately, in December 2016, it disclosed a second, even larger breach dating to August 2013 that had exposed roughly one billion further accounts — meaning Yahoo revealed two historic breaches within three months of each other, both predating the sale talks.

The details. The stolen data did not include unprotected payment-card numbers or bank account details, but it did include security questions and answers in some cases, which mattered because many people reuse the same questions and passwords across sites. Yahoo forced a mass password reset and invalidated unencrypted security questions for the affected accounts. Verizon initially said the breach might unravel the acquisition entirely; instead, in February 2017, the two companies renegotiated the price down by $350 million, to $4.48 billion, and agreed to split certain legal liabilities from the breaches going forward.

Why it still matters. The Yahoo case became the reference point regulators and dealmakers still cite for "cyber due diligence" in company sales — the idea that a buyer needs to price in the risk of breaches the seller has not yet found, not just the ones it has disclosed. Yahoo's own leadership, including chief executive Marissa Mayer, lost part of her bonus over the company's slow disclosure, and U.S. regulators later fined Yahoo's successor entity for misleading investors about the scale of the intrusion.

CONFLICT201626 September 2016

Colombia's government and its largest guerrilla group sign a deal ending a fifty-two-year war

In Cartagena, Colombia, on 26 September 2016, President Juan Manuel Santos and the top commander of the Revolutionary Armed Forces of Colombia (FARC), Rodrigo Londoño — known by his guerrilla name Timoleón Jiménez, or "Timochenko" — signed a peace accord ending a conflict that had run since 1964 and killed an estimated quarter of a million people. Presidents, prime ministers and the United Nations secretary-general attended the ceremony in the walled colonial city.

The celebration was premature: barely a week later, Colombian voters rejected the deal in a referendum, and the government had to renegotiate before the war could actually end.

Context & background. Formal talks between the Colombian government and the Revolutionary Armed Forces of Colombia had run for nearly four years in Havana, Cuba, brokered with Cuban and Norwegian backing and covering land reform, the rebels' political future, drug trafficking and justice for war crimes. The Cartagena ceremony on 26 September 2016 was the public, signed conclusion of those talks, not the negotiating venue itself — the guerrilla delegation and government negotiators had actually finished the text in Havana and then travelled to Cartagena for the formal signing in front of an international audience.

The details. Santos had put the accord to a popular vote he was not required to hold, confident it would pass comfortably; instead, on 2 October 2016, it was rejected by the narrowest of margins, with just over 37 percent of registered voters turning out at all. Opponents, led by former president Álvaro Uribe, objected chiefly to lenient sentencing terms for former guerrilla fighters and to guaranteed congressional seats for the rebels' new political party. Santos went back to the table, produced a revised text by 24 November 2016, and this time sent it to Congress rather than back to voters: the Senate approved it 75–0 and the House of Representatives 130–0, and it took effect on 30 November 2016.

Why it still matters. Santos won the Nobel Peace Prize in October 2016, awarded specifically for his efforts despite the referendum defeat. The deal did not end Colombia's violence outright — splinter factions that rejected the accord, along with other armed groups, have continued fighting in parts of the country since — but it did formally end South America's longest-running internal war and demobilise roughly 13,000 fighters, and it remains the model most often cited when other Latin American governments discuss negotiating with armed groups rather than fighting them.

POLITICS201626 September 2016

The first Clinton-Trump debate draws the largest television audience any debate has ever had

On the evening of 26 September 2016, Hillary Clinton and Donald Trump met at Hofstra University in Hempstead, New York, for the first of their three general-election debates, moderated by the television anchor Lester Holt. Roughly 84 million people watched live across thirteen channels carrying the broadcast — a record for any presidential debate.

The 90-minute debate covered the economy, race relations and national security, and it set the tone — heavy on interruptions and personal jabs — for the two debates that followed it that October.

Context & background. The debate arrived six weeks before election day with the race close in national polling. Both campaigns had spent days managing expectations publicly, and the choice of Hofstra University continued a modern tradition of holding these debates on university campuses rather than in television studios, giving broadcasters a ready-made stage and student audience.

The details. Over the ninety minutes, independent counts found Trump spoke for roughly 44 minutes to Clinton's 41, using about 2,200 more words overall, while the audience — instructed beforehand to stay silent — audibly reacted more than a dozen times anyway. Trump interrupted Clinton and the moderator far more often than she interrupted him, a pattern flagged by nearly every post-debate analysis and one both sides tried to spin to their advantage afterward.

Why it still matters. The 84-million-viewer figure stood as the record for any presidential debate broadcast for years afterward, and the debate itself became a fixture of 2016 campaign retrospectives — frequently cited, for instance, in later disputes over whether television networks give show-style interruptions an advantage over substantive answers, a debate-format argument still current in American elections held since.

CULTURE200121 September 2001

Every major American broadcaster clears its schedule for one telethon, ten days after the September 11 attacks

On the evening of 21 September 2001, all four major American broadcast television networks and more than thirty-five other outlets simultaneously carried "America: A Tribute to Heroes," a commercial-free, ninety-minute benefit broadcast for families of the September 11 attacks. Twenty-one musicians performed, including Bruce Springsteen, Stevie Wonder, U2, Neil Young and Céline Dion, filmed in stripped-down, candlelit settings rather than a normal concert stage.

Viewers pledged more than $150 million that night alone, and the fund it created went on to raise over $200 million in total for victims' families.

Context & background. The broadcast was organized in barely a week by television and music-industry figures working with the major networks, an unusually fast turnaround that required competitors to agree to share a single commercial-free feed rather than each running its own coverage. It aired live from studios in New York, Los Angeles and London, with the destroyed World Trade Center site and the ongoing search-and-rescue effort as the unstated backdrop for every performance.

The details. There was no laugh track, no studio audience applause and no on-screen telephone-number graphics competing for attention with the performers; hosts, including several well-known actors, read the names of the dead and missing between songs rather than interviewing guests. Donations were funnelled through the United Way's September 11th Telethon Fund, which later merged its distribution efforts with other victims'-relief charities to avoid duplicating payments to the same families.

Why it still matters. The broadcast set the template American television has repeated for every major disaster response since — a joint, commercial-free, network-spanning telethon, rather than each channel running its own appeal — used again after Hurricane Katrina in 2005 and other disasters. It is also remembered as one of the only times every major U.S. network broadcaster agreed to hand over an entire prime-time evening to the same, unbranded feed.

BUSINESS200122 September 2001

Congress passes an airline rescue and a victims' fund eleven days after the attacks

On 22 September 2001, President George W. Bush signed the Air Transportation Safety and System Stabilization Act, which Congress had passed within days of the September 11 attacks that had grounded American aviation and pushed several airlines toward collapse. The law provided $5 billion in direct cash grants to airlines and up to $10 billion in federal loan guarantees.

The same act, in the same bill, created the September 11th Victim Compensation Fund, a separate federal program to pay compensation directly to the families of people killed or injured in the attacks, in exchange for those families giving up their right to sue the airlines.

Context & background. Every commercial flight in the United States had been grounded for two days after the attacks, and even once flying resumed, passenger numbers collapsed as travellers stayed home; several major airlines were warning within days that they faced bankruptcy without federal help. Congress moved unusually quickly for major legislation, passing the bill within eleven days of the attacks with almost no opposition in either chamber.

The details. Independent analysis afterward found the airlines drew on only a fraction of what the act made available — about $1.6 billion of the $10 billion in loan guarantees was ever actually used — because carriers found it cheaper to raise money elsewhere once the immediate crisis passed. The victim compensation fund, by contrast, ultimately paid out to the families or estates of nearly all of the roughly 2,900 people killed on the ground and in the four hijacked aircraft, plus people injured in the attacks or in the rescue and cleanup effort.

Why it still matters. The September 11th Victim Compensation Fund became the model for the federal government's response to later mass-casualty and toxic-exposure claims, including a second, permanent fund for first responders and survivors suffering long-term illness from the attack site, which Congress had to fight to keep funded for years afterward. It remains one of the largest one-time compensation programs the federal government has ever run.

SPORT200125 September 2001

Michael Jordan ends his retirement to play for the Washington Wizards, and gives away the paycheck

On 25 September 2001, the retired basketball star Michael Jordan announced he would return to play professional basketball, at age 38, for the Washington Wizards — the team where he had been working as an executive, president of basketball operations, since January 2000. He said he would donate his entire salary for the coming season to relief efforts for families affected by the September 11 attacks.

It was Jordan's second comeback after two earlier retirements, and it drew huge attention even though, as a player for a struggling team well past his prime, the results on the court would prove modest.

Context & background. Jordan had retired from playing in January 1999 after winning six championships with the Chicago Bulls, then taken a partial ownership stake and the top basketball-operations job with the Washington Wizards in January 2000. Rumours of a playing comeback had circulated for months before the September 2001 announcement, and he had reportedly been training intensively that summer, well before the attacks gave the announcement its unusual timing and tone.

The details. To play again, Jordan had to give up his executive role and his ownership stake in the team, since league rules barred him from being both a player and a team owner or top executive at once. His first season back was cut short by a knee injury that limited him to 60 games, though he still managed one signature moment: on 29 December 2001, at age 38, he scored 51 points against the Charlotte Hornets, becoming the oldest player in the league's history to reach 50 points in a game. The Wizards sold out every home game across his two comeback seasons but never made the playoffs with him on the roster.

Why it still matters. Jordan's Wizards years are now mostly remembered as a coda rather than a triumph, but they mattered commercially — ticket sales and television audiences for a struggling franchise jumped wherever he played — and the comeback fixed his image, for a generation of fans too young to have watched his championship years live, as a player who kept competing well past the point of certain success. He retired for a third and final time in 2003 and returned to team ownership, later buying a majority stake in the Charlotte franchise.

CONFLICT197621 September 1976

A car bomb on a Washington street kills a former Chilean minister and his American colleague

On the morning of 21 September 1976, a bomb hidden under the car of Orlando Letelier, a former Chilean government minister living in exile, detonated on Embassy Row in Washington, D.C., killing him along with Ronni Karpen Moffitt, his 25-year-old American colleague at the Institute for Policy Studies. Her husband, Michael Moffitt, was in the back seat and survived.

Letelier had served under Chile's socialist president Salvador Allende and, after Allende's overthrow in a 1973 military coup, become the most outspoken critic of Chile's new military government while living in exile in the United States — making him a target of the government led by General Augusto Pinochet.

Context & background. The killing was later tied to Operation Condor, a coordinated campaign among South American military governments of the 1970s to track down and kill political exiles who opposed them, wherever those exiles had fled. Letelier had been Chile's ambassador to the United States and later a cabinet minister under Allende; after the 1973 coup he was imprisoned for a year, then exiled, and had spent 1976 lobbying the United States Congress to cut aid to Pinochet's government and writing a widely read essay attacking the free-market economic policies Chile's new rulers had adopted.

The details. Michael Townley, an American operative working for Chile's secret police, built the bomb and recruited anti-Castro Cuban exiles living in the United States to help place it; he was extradited from Chile, pleaded guilty in a U.S. court and testified against his accomplices, receiving a reduced sentence in exchange. It took nearly two decades for the intelligence chain to reach the top: Chile's former secret-police director, Manuel Contreras, and his deputy were convicted in Chile in 1993 and served prison terms, but documents declassified only in 2015 showed that U.S. intelligence agencies had known since 1978 that Pinochet himself had personally ordered the killing.

Why it still matters. This was the first case of a foreign government's agents assassinating someone on United States soil in the modern era, and the FBI treated it as a landmark state-sponsored terrorism investigation. Pinochet was never charged over Letelier's death and died in 2006 while still facing separate corruption and human-rights prosecutions; Chile eventually paid compensation to the victims' families through international arbitration in the early 1990s, and Sheridan Circle, the site of the bombing, was later renamed Letelier-Moffitt Plaza in their memory.

POLITICS197623 September 1976

Ford and Carter revive the presidential debate after sixteen years — then the sound cuts out for 27 minutes

On 23 September 1976, President Gerald Ford and his Democratic challenger Jimmy Carter met at the Walnut Street Theatre in Philadelphia for the first televised presidential debate held since the Kennedy-Nixon debates of 1960 — a sixteen-year gap during which no major-party nominees had agreed to debate on television at all. About 69.7 million people watched.

Eighty-one minutes into the planned ninety-minute broadcast, the audio failed completely; the two candidates stood silently at their lecterns for 27 minutes while technicians fixed the fault, before finishing the debate as if nothing had happened.

Context & background. Televised general-election debates had stopped after 1960 partly because federal broadcasting rules at the time required equal air time for every candidate on the ballot, making a two-person debate legally complicated until Congress created a temporary exemption for 1976. Ford, who had never won a national election himself — he became president through the resignations of Richard Nixon and Vice President Spiro Agnew (Agnew over a bribery and tax case in 1973, Nixon over Watergate in 1974) rather than at the ballot box — agreed to debate partly because he was trailing Carter badly in the polls and needed a way to reach voters directly.

The details. This first of three 1976 debates was devoted to domestic and economic policy; a faulty audio amplifier in the theatre's sound system caused the total silence, and network cameras kept broadcasting the two candidates standing motionless and saying nothing for the full 27 minutes rather than cutting away, which became as memorable as anything either man said. Carter had been in the middle of answering a question about oversight of intelligence agencies when the sound failed, and had to pick the answer back up once it returned.

Why it still matters. The debate reintroduced a format that has been a fixture of every U.S. presidential election since, and this specific episode is why debate organizers still build in equipment redundancy and contingency plans for live technical failure. Carter went on to narrowly win the November 1976 election, and later research on the 1976 debates fed the enduring argument — still made about televised debates today — over how much a single televised moment can move an otherwise closely fought race.

POLITICS197624 September 1976

Rhodesia's government accepts a plan for Black-majority rule — and then does not deliver it

On 24 September 1976, Rhodesian prime minister Ian Smith announced in a televised broadcast that his government had accepted the outline of a plan, brokered by U.S. Secretary of State Henry Kissinger, to hand over political control of the British colony to its Black majority within two years. It appeared to be the first real crack in nearly a decade of white-minority rule that Smith had vowed would last, in his own words, "a thousand years."

The acceptance did not bring the peace it promised: within months the plan collapsed, the guerrilla war against Smith's government escalated instead of ending, and Zimbabwe did not achieve independence under Black-majority rule until 1980, on terms fought out separately over the following four years.

Context & background. Rhodesia's white-minority government had unilaterally declared independence from Britain in 1965 rather than accept Black-majority rule, and had fought a guerrilla war against Black nationalist movements ever since, while surviving on trade with apartheid South Africa despite international sanctions. By 1976, with neighbouring Mozambique newly independent and backing the guerrillas, and South Africa's own government pressing Smith to settle, Kissinger flew through the region on a shuttle-diplomacy mission (flying back and forth between the parties to negotiate) and secured Smith's public acceptance of a majority-rule timetable — the announcement this card describes.

The details. Smith's broadcast committed only to the principle of majority rule within two years, not to any agreed mechanism for getting there, and a follow-up conference convened in Geneva that October to work out the details broke down within weeks over exactly who would govern during the transition. Rather than winding down, the war intensified: the two main Black nationalist guerrilla movements, led by Joshua Nkomo and Robert Mugabe, merged their forces into a united Patriotic Front and stepped up the fighting Smith's own announcement had been meant to end.

Why it still matters. Smith did eventually sign a settlement in March 1978 with more moderate Black leaders, but the guerrillas who were actually doing the fighting were not party to it, and the war continued for another two years until Britain brokered the Lancaster House Agreement in December 1979 (the British-brokered deal that set elections under majority rule and led to independent Zimbabwe in 1980). Zimbabwe finally became independent in April 1980, with Mugabe — one of the guerrilla leaders Smith's 1976 broadcast had been designed to sideline — elected as its first prime minister. The September 1976 broadcast is remembered less as the moment Rhodesia's war ended than as proof that announcing acceptance of a peace plan and actually delivering one are very different things.

Editor's view

Two of this week's nine stories are governments choosing to change course under outside pressure rather than keep fighting: Rhodesia's government accepting a Black-majority-rule timetable in 1976, and Colombia's government signing a peace deal with the Revolutionary Armed Forces of Colombia in 2016. What's honest here is how differently those two went. Colombia's deal was rejected by its own voters within a week of the signing ceremony, got patched up, and held. Rhodesia's 1976 acceptance produced no such ending at all — the follow-up talks collapsed within weeks, the war it was supposed to end got worse, and Black-majority rule did not actually arrive until 1980, under Zimbabwe's different name and on terms fought out over four more years of war. A signature is not a settlement; Colombia and Rhodesia are the same week, forty years apart, showing both ends of that gap.

A second thread needs less qualifying. Ford and Carter revived the televised presidential debate in 1976 after a sixteen-year gap; forty years later, in the same week of the calendar, Clinton and Trump's first debate on the same format drew the largest television audience any debate has ever had. The tradition those two men restarted with a stalled amplifier and 27 minutes of dead air is still, four decades on, appointment television.

The 2001 stories don't fit into either thread, and that's worth saying plainly rather than forcing a connection: that week was still the immediate, improvised American response to an attack nine days earlier — a telethon, an emergency spending bill, one athlete giving away a season's salary — and it reads truest on its own terms.
