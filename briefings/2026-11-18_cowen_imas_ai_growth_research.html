Cowen and Imas: Why Faster AI Won't Mean Double-Digit Growth

Economists Tyler Cowen and Alex Imas accept the San Francisco view of what AI can do, then argue the economy will move at a pace "quite a bit more normal". Their case first, then six claims checked.

1

The guests' joint case

Written so both guests would sign it. No verdicts here.

"I don't think Tyler and I really disagree with like the San Francisco consensus on like the capabilities." Imas

Capabilities are real and accelerating; the economy is a different object

Imas sees real firm-level productivity gains in the agentic era; Cowen changed his own view in August because of progress in mathematics.

"Benefits will exceed costs, but it will take some time." Cowen
"And the path will be quite a bit more normal than many people would have you believe." Cowen

Speed in one place moves the bottleneck elsewhere

Explosive growth needs knife-edge conditions in growth theory, and the estimated returns to ideas sit below one. Automated sectors get cheaper and shrink as a share of spending, so value migrates to whatever is slow. One guest, hedging that he did not know, put the slow-moving share of the US economy near 40%: government, nonprofits, regulated health care, higher education. Imas adds that agents cannot yet coordinate.

"often the faster that some things move, that just means you hit the real bottleneck more quickly." (ASR-corrected) Cowen

Demand, prices and politics all brake the curve

Lab revenue forecasts need other sectors to grow fast enough to pay. Prices are competitive, markets look ordinary, and voters in fifty states can slow data-centre build-out without stopping it. Cowen adds that his mind is stocked with centuries of growth data, Bay Area minds with Bay Area conversations.

The hedges they keep

Imas says a coordination breakthrough could change wages and employment abruptly, and calls its likelihood a separate question. Cowen says he is still bullish on the sector.

The argument in six checks

In conversation order. C 5/5 primary source · 4/5 two independent sources · 3/5 single credible source · 2/5 aggregator only. Check boxes link to the scorecard.

1 · Markets do not price a fast takeoff Cowen

"I trust the judgment of the market more than the judgment of the Bay Area people" Cowen

Since August Cowen had moved toward faster progress because of the maths results, then slightly back this week. His reasons for being a little more bearish: Nvidia, the IPO markets, the revenue numbers and institutions not running smoothly. The midterms came up separately, in the regulation discussion. He said real rates and stock prices look normal, and that nobody in the Bay Area answered his demand question.

"So I'd say I'm like 1% more bearish over the course of the last week" Cowen
"I wouldn't say I was underwhelmed, but they're not numbers that you would expect under the super fast takeoff scenarios." Cowen
"And no one had an answer for that question." Cowen

His hedge:

"That doesn't make me bearish on the sector as a whole. I'm still bullish." Cowen
CheckMostly true (IPO) · Open (real rates) C 3/5 overall. The Anthropic IPO price range of $70–78 implies about $1.26–1.41T fully diluted (S-1/A, C 5/5; see our Anthropic IPO companion piece). A Polymarket first-day snapshot on 18 Nov: under $1.25T 33%, $1.25–1.5T 39%, $1.5–1.75T 18% (financefeeds, aggregator, C 2/5). The price range is set by the issuer and underwriters, so first-day trading may differ. So "not flying above 2 trillion" holds. Anthropic's S-1 shows Q3 2026 revenue of $17.0B, up 47% quarter on quarter (C 5/5); annualised that is roughly 4.7x, so Cowen's remark that these are not fast-takeoff numbers is arguable, and we did not test it further. But in September Anthropic traded above $2T on thin crypto-derivative markets (Hyperliquid about $2.15T, roughly $6M daily volume; Euronews 17 Sep, C 3/5); that talk faded. On real rates, the 10-year TIPS real yield was 2.66% on 9 Nov, up from 1.84% a year earlier, with a series high of 2.95% on 5 Oct; the September auction at 2.653% was the highest in nearly 18 years (247wallst, tipswatch, C 3/5). In the Chow–Halperin–Mazlish framework (Jan 2023, C 5/5 for the argument), rising real rates are what transformative-AI expectations would produce, so "normal" is contestable. High rates have many other causes (the Fed held at 3.75–4.00% with hike dissents on 28 Oct). Back to scorecard

2 · Prices fall and revenue must come from other sectors Imas

"you need to start seeing the productivity numbers and the rest of the economy to justify the revenue increases." Imas
"And we haven't really seen too much evidence of that." Imas

Imas argues willingness to pay must come from productivity gains elsewhere, and, in the context of price competition, cited Ramp card-spend data as showing rising token use with flat revenue. He hedged the forward view.

"you saw exponential increases in token usage and flat revenue numbers, right?" Imas
"Not to say that it won't happen" Imas
CheckMostly true (price competition) C 3/5 · Unverifiable (flat revenue) C 2/5. Ramp's AI Index: effective price per million tokens $0.68 in September 2026, down 41% from a $1.15 March peak; OpenAI is down 38% and Anthropic 22% since 1 Aug, and OpenAI cut one model price by 80% (Fortune 9 Sep, citing Ramp; C 3/5). We found no Ramp statement of exponential token usage alongside flat revenue. Ramp shows falling prices, top-1% spenders' median spend down 9.7% from July to August, and adoption still rising. Ramp's 41% fall in price per token fits the price-competition reading. Lab revenue such as Anthropic's (Q3 2026 $17.0B, up 47% quarter on quarter, C 5/5; companion piece) is a different measure, not a refutation of a Ramp spend finding. Back to scorecard

3 · Returns to ideas are below one, so no knife-edge blow-up Imas

"it is decidedly less than one. So this could change." Imas

The returns-to-ideas parameter is estimated below one, he said, including in the METR report, though full recursive self-improvement could change that. On Baumol effects he cited a paper using 200 years of data.

"the thing that's growing least will be contributing most to GDP." Imas
CheckMostly true for the aggregate economy; contested for AI and software C 4/5. Aghion, B. Jones and C. Jones (NBER w23928, 2017) is the standard knife-edge reference (C 4/5); a Chad Jones note of Jan 2026 says a blow-up needs the parameter above 1 (C 5/5). Bloom et al. estimate about 0.32 for aggregate US research productivity, Epoch's replication median about 0.25 ; Epoch's software estimates exceed 1 in several domains but not significantly, and Epoch calls the evidence inconclusive (C 4/5). METR's 5 Nov report on Anthropic estimated benchmark progress about 1.9x faster since Oct 2025 (95% CI 1.5–2.8x), about 80% attributed to AI, with core evidence largely redacted (C 4/5). METR did not itself estimate this parameter; Imas's estimate draws on its figures. Garicano and Imas's accounting (Marginal Revolution, 13 Nov): capability growth 1.91x faster while R&D spend rose about 6.2x, so capability per dollar fell about 69% (C 3/5; accounting, not causal). Counter: Davidson, Halperin, Houlden and Korinek (NBER w35155, May 2026): full automation of software research plus 5% elsewhere gives a singularity in about six years, though with bottlenecks it is "a race between bottlenecks and automation" (C 4/5). The Baumol paper is probably Comin, Lashkari and Mestieri (Econometrica 2021; 39 countries, postwar); "200 years" is Unverifiable C 3/5. Back to scorecard

4 · Coordination is the missing piece Imas

"what you actually need for real economic transformation is not just competence" Imas
"these agents are still very bad at coordinating." Imas
"They're mostly just doing parallel processing." Imas

This is the thesis of Imas's paper with Andrew Koh and Shane Legg. In a "minimal AGI" world (average-human level everywhere) wages change but structure barely does. A coordination breakthrough could break the Coasean ceiling on firm size and shift wages and employment abruptly. That is his worry.

"To be honest, I don't know." Imas, on what blocks coordination
"likelihood is kind of a different story." Imas, on the phase-transition scenario
CheckMostly true (description consistent; primary not read) C 2/5. A Google DeepMind working paper of 29 Oct 2026, about 108 pages, by Imas, Andrew Koh (Columbia) and Shane Legg, with an essay version titled "The Economics of Intelligence". Our only source is an aiwiki summary (aggregator, C 2/5); we did not read the PDF. The summarised results match his on-air account (minimal-AGI regime, single agent losing to a structured team, a "supercooperative" threshold breaking the Coasean ceiling, abrupt shifts); the authors call these model outcomes, not forecasts. No named direct opponent found; the Epoch/Mechanize view that compute and physical build-out bind, not coordination, is our reading of their position. Back to scorecard

5 · Growth spurts are small, commodity or rebound cases Cowen

"Guyana grew 40% in a year. That's crazy. But it's a tiny place selling one thing oil." (ASR-corrected) Cowen

Cowen's historical high-growth cases: Guyana, the US briefly above 8% in the early-1980s recovery, post-war and pandemic rebounds. On a one-year 10%, his case was a war or pandemic rebound on top of an AI burst, and he added that the effect would be temporary.

"could we for a year get to 10%, I'll say yes, I bet heavily against it" Cowen
CheckTrue (Guyana) C 5/5 · Mostly true (US) C 2/5. IMF: Guyana 33.8% in 2023, 43.6% in 2024, about 47% a year over 2022–24 (C 5/5). For the US we have 1984 annual real GDP growth of 7.2% (secondary source, C 2/5); quarterly annualised growth above 8% in the 1983–84 recovery is not verified by us. Back to scorecard

6 · Politics slows the build-out but does not stop it Cowen and Imas

Cowen said backlash slows but does not stop deployment, because there are fifty states. Imas added that political reaction is endogenous and would brake any extreme scenario. Cowen's example was Texas.

"even the new Texas governor is already backtracking." Cowen

His claim was softer than a moratorium reversal: he said the new governor wants union labour and good wages, existing projects go ahead and the others get a second look. Imas agreed that governors are backtracking.

He also said Waymo is still not in Washington, DC. Asked what an "explosion" would mean, he said agents at 6% of GDP would be enormous, and that if forced to guess he could see about 1.5% within five years.

"If you ask me the question, when will agents be 10% of GDP? I don't think it's soon." Cowen
CheckMixed: True (Texas flip, Waymo) · Unverifiable (Texas backtracking remark) C 3/5. Democrat Gina Hinojosa beat Gov. Greg Abbott, and James Talarico beat Ken Paxton for Senate (KUT, election night, C 3/5). Hinojosa had campaigned on an immediate moratorium; we found no source either way after 5 Nov (C 2/5), so the backtracking claim is Unverifiable. Bloomington, Illinois approved a six-month data-centre moratorium on 26 May (WGLT, C 3/5). Waymo is not in DC: framework legislation pending, council members signalling delay (WJLA 12 Nov, C 3/5). Back to scorecard

Where it is contested

Host first, then named voices on both sides, then splits within camps.

First other view: the hostTorenberg asked the guests to steelman the fast-growth side. In a separate exchange the host put a16z's view that supply is the binding scarcity and demand is larger than it looks. He noted that heavy users are few, and himself conceded the counter that the impact on companies is not yet measurable. He also mentioned a new a16z fund for data centres and infrastructure.
"can we steelman the other side?" (ASR-corrected) Torenberg
"the super users are really a small percentage of people at a small percentage of companies." Torenberg
Fast-growth side Leopold Aschenbrenner (Situational Awareness, Jun 2024): growth of 30% or more "follows fairly straightforwardly", via Tim Cunningham's forecast table (C 3/5). Asked about the essay's legacy, Cowen called it important "in anything, not just technology", but said Aschenbrenner is too enamoured of fast takeoff and that simulation substitutes for experiment only "in some areas". His sharper line:
"He's assuming simulation can substitute for experiment." Cowen, on Aschenbrenner
Epoch AI (Ho and Potlogea, Jun 2025): GATE model, 30% of tasks automated could give growth above 20% (C 5/5). Davidson, Halperin, Houlden and Korinek (check 3). Ege Erdil and Tamay Besiroglu (Epoch/Mechanize, Dwarkesh 2025) expect explosive growth but say "Research is harder than people think", so they are partly in Cowen's camp (C 5/5). Dario Amodei (Feb 2026): 5–15% a year for developed economies, and he says there is "no science" behind the number (C 3/5); he leads Anthropic, the compiler's maker. Erik Brynjolfsson (Feb 2026): US productivity about 2.7% in 2025 against 1.4% the prior decade, needing confirmation (C 4/5).
Normal-growth side Daron Acemoglu (2024: at most 0.71% TFP, total factor productivity, over ten years; Jun 2026: about 0.55% TFP, 1–1.5% GDP over a decade; C 4/5) is more sceptical than Cowen. Chad Jones and Christopher Tonetti (Jan 2026): about 0.2 points a year (C 3/5). Narayanan and Kapoor ("AI as Normal Technology", 2025; C 3/5). Carl Benedikt Frey (May 2026): "verification tax" (C 4/5). Cowen himself (Feb 2025): about +0.5 percentage points a year (C 5/5).
Splits within camps Moll and Imas, "Will AI Soon Lead to Double-Digit Growth?" (Sep 2026): double digits within 10–15 years is "extremely unlikely", yet their baseline is 4–5% (C 4/5), far above Cowen's +0.5 points and Acemoglu's figure. Brad DeLong calls 4–5% "way optimistic" yet transformative. The Anthropic-affiliated scenarios paper (Korinek, C. Jones and others, Sep 2026) spans under 0.5 points to 15% a year (C 5/5, via Cowen's post). Basil Halperin co-authored both the 2023 real-rates argument Cowen echoes and the 2026 explosive-growth paper. None of the fast-growth camp's claims received a check box here; its forecasts are model outputs, not observed data.

No direct published response to this episode was found (searched 16–18 Nov).

Stepping back

The error direction in this run. The guests' factual anchors mostly held: Guyana, the Texas flip, Waymo, the IPO below $2T, aggregate returns below one. Where checks failed or stalled: the Ramp "flat revenue" line (unverifiable), "200 years of data" (unverifiable), and one contestable reading (real rates "normal" while TIPS sit near an 18-year high). The Texas "backtracking" remark was not found either way, which is not the same as wrong. Only the guests' claims were checked, so the direction partly reflects selection. Unverifiable is not the same as wrong. Six checks are not a base rate.

What survives

  • The IPO is below $2T, aggregate returns to ideas are below one, and Guyana, the Texas flip and Waymo all check out.
  • Token prices are falling fast, supporting the price-competition half of the demand argument.

What to discount

  • Cowen: his market reading leans on real rates that are near an 18-year high, and on a Texas backtracking remark we could not find a source for. He also has a long public record on one side.
  • Imas: the flat-revenue line is unverified (Ramp spend data, not lab revenue); his coordination paper was checked through a summary only, and his employer is a frontier lab.
  • Host / a16z: the supply-scarcity view comes from an investor in AI companies and infrastructure.
  • Fast-growth camp: forecasts of 30% or more rest on assumptions (simulation substituting for experiment, weak bottlenecks) contested even within the camp. None of its claims received a check box here; its forecasts are model outputs, not observed data.
  • The compiler: Claude is an Anthropic model, Anthropic is a subject of checks 1 and 2, and verdicts and scores are our judgment from a short read.
