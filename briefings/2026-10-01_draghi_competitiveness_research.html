Draghi's Competitiveness Report, Two Years On

In September 2024 Mario Draghi diagnosed why Europe is falling behind and prescribed a new industrial strategy, a minimum of €750–800bn a year of additional investment and a faster way of governing. This is his argument at full strength, the numbers it rests on, and what Europe did with it.

Report: September 2024, Parts A and B15 claims checked1 October 2026

Read: both parts of the report as published by the Commission (Part A, 73 pages, read in full; Part B, 328 pages, read chapter by chapter via digests checked against the full text), plus Draghi's own one-year review speech of 16 September 2025.

Checked: 15 load-bearing claims against primary sources where available (OECD data and AMECO, the Commission's annual macro-economic database, via the report; the Commission's Joint Research Centre, the International Energy Agency, the EU energy regulators' agency ACER, the European Defence Agency, the European Court of Auditors, economic-history literature). What this report is

The subject first, before any assessment.

“The future of European competitiveness” is a two-part report that the President of the European Commission, Ursula von der Leyen, asked Mario Draghi to write, and that was presented in September 2024. In the acknowledgment Draghi thanks her “for her constant support” and thanks the President of the European Central Bank, Christine Lagarde, for “lending valuable resources”. Draghi was President of the European Central Bank from 2011 to 2019 and Prime Minister of Italy from 2021 to 2022. The acknowledgments credit Paolo D’Aprile and Pauline Rouch with coordinating all the work contained in the report and Jonathan Yiangou with drafting extensive parts of it.

Part A, “A competitiveness strategy for Europe”, is the argument: a diagnosis, three areas for action (innovation, decarbonisation with competitiveness, security), a chapter on financing and one on governance. Part B, “In-depth analysis and recommendations”, is the evidence and the proposals. It has ten sectoral chapters (energy, critical raw materials, digitalisation and advanced technologies including AI and semiconductors, energy-intensive industries, clean technologies, automotive, defence, space, pharmaceuticals, transport) and five horizontal ones (innovation, skills, investment, competition policy, governance).

Across the chapter tables there are about 170 numbered proposals by our count; the EPIC tracker, one of the two outside trackers (EPIC, JEDI) used below, splits them into 383 measures. The two figures count different things and are not interchangeable.

The cover carries this disclaimer:

“This document should not be considered as representative of the European Commission’s official position.”

Part A, cover page

The acknowledgment lists a long roll of people and bodies consulted in meetings or by written contribution. It includes companies such as Amazon, Google, Meta, Microsoft, ASML and Siemens, think tanks, consumer groups, the European Trade Union Confederation (ETUC) and environmental NGOs. Every claim below is the report's, not the Commission's, and each sits where the report puts it.

0

The argument in brief

The report's case, in its own terms, before any checking. No verdicts in this section.

Slow growth has stopped being a nuisance and become a threat

For most of this century slow growth was seen, in the report's words, as “an inconvenience but not a calamity”. Three supports of the post-Cold War era carried that: open world trade, cheap Russian gas and the US security umbrella. All three are going. The workforce will shrink by close to 2 million people a year by 2040, and at the productivity growth of recent years GDP would only be kept constant until 2050. The report's conditional: “If Europe cannot become more productive, we will be forced to choose.” Its verdict: “This is an existential challenge.” It adds that “the only way to become more productive is for Europe to radically change”.

The gap with the US is mostly a technology gap

Europe largely missed the internet wave and is stuck in what the report calls the “middle technology trap”: a static industrial structure of mature firms, led by carmakers, that invest less in research. Its young innovators scale up, and move their headquarters, in America.

Energy and dependencies have turned into vulnerabilities

Industrial electricity costs two to three times and gas four to five times the US level. Raw materials and clean technology depend on China; defence, cloud and chips depend on the US. The report says “These dependencies are often two-way”, but warns that without action “we risk being vulnerable to coercion”.

Europe has the savings but not the focus

On the report's figures (unverified, see c10) households save more than Americans, yet Europe sets objectives but few priorities, dilutes its spending across many national and EU instruments and takes 19 months on average to pass a law.

The answer is a strategy: private finance is critical, public support essential, some joint funding necessary

Align innovation, energy, trade, competition and security policy; raise investment by about 5% of GDP a year; use majority voting and coalitions of the willing where unanimity blocks. Private financing is critical, and a real capital markets union is meant to unlock it, but in the report's chapter 1 wording the private sector is “unlikely to be able to finance the lion’s share of this investment” without public sector support. Some joint funding is, in the report's word, necessary for key European public goods such as breakthrough innovation; only the projects for defence procurement and cross-border grids carry the condition “If the political and institutional conditions are met”. The case carries its other hedges too: no policy of defending national champions, and defensive trade measures not applied systematically.

Diagnosis: Europe is falling behind

Seven sections, in the order the report builds its case. Confidence (C) scale: 5/5 primary source · 4/5 two independent sources · 3/5 single credible source · 2/5 aggregator only.

Verdicts: True (the figure holds as stated) · Mostly true (holds, with caveats) · Misleading (the figure or its framing creates a false impression) · Wrong figure (a plain factual slip) · Unverifiable (not checked against a source here) · Open (the evidence does not settle it).

The gap, and where it comes from Part A, ch. 1–2

“Around 70% of the gap in per capita GDP with US at PPP is explained by lower productivity in the EU”

Part A, chapter 1

The report measures the gap three ways. At constant 2015 prices the EU–US gap in the level of GDP has widened from slightly more than 15% in 2002 to 30% in 2023; on a purchasing power parity (PPP) basis a gap of 12% has emerged; per head in PPP terms it has risen from 31% to 34%. Real disposable income per head has grown almost twice as much in the US as in the EU since 2000. Its footnote on the GDP-level gap (not on the 70% productivity decomposition) is a hedge the author makes himself:

“The value of the gap in GDP in any given year is only indicative.”

Part A, chapter 1, footnote 2

Where does productivity fall behind? The report points to technology. It says EU productivity diverged from the US in the mid-1990s because Europe failed to capitalise on the internet revolution, both in creating tech companies and in spreading digital technology through the economy. And it adds a statement that cuts against any simple story of general decline:

“if we exclude the tech sector, EU productivity growth over the past twenty years would be broadly at par with the US”

Part A, chapter 2

Box 2 gives the numbers for 2000–2019 (US 0.8% against 0.6% for the EU once the main ICT sectors, meaning information and communication technology, are excluded) and concedes that the analysis may understate the effect of ICT, because US professional services and finance also draw high productivity growth from digital diffusion. The report's explanation is structural. EU firms are specialised in mature technologies, spend about EUR 270 billion less on research and innovation (R&I) than their US counterparts as of 2021, and the top three R&I spenders in Europe have been carmakers for two decades. The Foreword states the consequence about new firms:

“no EU company with a market capitalisation over EUR 100 billion that has been set up from scratch in the last fifty years”

Part A, Foreword (the sentence continues: all six US companies above EUR 1 trillion were created in the period)

Part A also says that “Only four of the world's top 50 tech companies are European.” and that close to 30% of the unicorns founded in Europe between 2008 and 2021 moved their headquarters abroad, mostly to the US. A unicorn is a start-up valued above USD 1 billion; the chapter counts 147 founded and 40 relocated.

Check · c1Mostly true C 4/5 — The figures are as the report cites them (OECD and AMECO data). The result depends on the yardstick: the Banque de France has EU income per head falling from 68% to 65% of the US level between 2000 and 2023, which is consistent with the report, while a 2023 Bruegel analysis in PPP terms shows convergence from 67% to 72% over 1995–2022. The 2026 argument between Krugman and Aghion, Bergeaud and Garicano turns on the same choice, and a Richmond Fed brief concludes that both sides are factually right and the difference is the price deflation of IT goods. Sources: Part A, Figures 3–4; Banque de France; Bruegel; Richmond Fed Economic Brief 26-30.

This is the report's own analysis of EU KLEMS data (a Commission-supported industry-level productivity database) on ten EU countries, and the report itself says it may understate ICT diffusion effects. Source: Part A, Box 2; one source, the report's own.

It is contested at the edges: Inditex (roughly USD 190–206bn) traces to Zara, founded in 1975 with the holding company dated 1985, and Spotify, founded in 2006 and domiciled in Luxembourg, passed USD 100bn in 2025 (USD 120bn at the end of 2025), after the report was written. Sources: Part A; companiesmarketcap, an aggregator.

Check · c5Mostly true C 5/5 — The Commission's Joint Research Centre (Testa et al., 2022) counts 40 of 147 EU unicorns, or 27%, that moved abroad: 32 to the US, 7 to the UK, 1 to Israel, on Dealroom data to the second quarter of 2021. The report's “close to 30%” is a fair rounding. Source: JRC127712.
Other viewsPaul Krugman (Substack, 2026) argues that in PPP terms Europe has held steady against the US since 2000 and that the American lead is concentrated in IT production, where prices fall. Philippe Aghion, Antonin Bergeaud and Luis Garicano (Project Syndicate, 29 May 2026) answer that PPP is the wrong yardstick and that the gap is widening. The Richmond Fed brief (2026) sides with neither: both are factually right. Bruegel (October 2023) had earlier argued convergence in PPP terms. Jacques Pelkmans (CEPS, 2024) says the report never defines competitiveness, so its success cannot be judged.
Two years on. EU GDP grew 1.1% in 2024 and 1.5% in 2025, against 3.0% and 2.3% in the US (Eurostat; BEA; C 5/5). Two years is too short to settle an argument about two decades.

Weight: the figures are as cited, but whether Europe is diverging depends on the yardstick, and the report's own footnote concedes that the GDP-level gap is only indicative. Chapter 1 describes what competitiveness should mean but sets no single measurable target.

2

Why now: trade, energy, security, demographics

The premises of urgency. This section is context for the argument, not a measurable claim, so it has no check box.

“But the foundations on which we built are now being shaken.”

Part A, Foreword

The Foreword names three supports of the post-1990 era. First, world trade: between 2000 and 2019 trade rose from 30% to 43% of GDP in the EU, against 25% to 26% in the US, and the IMF now projects slower growth in world trade than the 2000–19 average. Second, energy: pipeline gas supplied around 45% of EU gas imports in 2021, a source the report says has disappeared at huge cost. Third, security: the US umbrella let Europe spend its peace dividend on domestic goals, while Russia's war and US attention on the Pacific have changed that.

Demography is the fourth pressure and the one that turns a slowdown into a squeeze. The workforce is projected to shrink by close to 2 million workers a year by 2040, and the ratio of working to retired people to fall from about 3:1 to 2:1. The report writes:

“If the EU were to maintain its average productivity growth rate since 2015, it would only be enough to keep GDP constant until 2050”

Part A, Foreword; chapter 2 specifies this rate as 0.7% a year for labour productivity

At the same time Europe faces new spending needs for digitalisation, decarbonisation and defence. The Foreword then states the choice it sees, in five words that the rest of the report keeps returning to:

“This is an existential challenge.”

Part A, Foreword

Without higher productivity, the report says, Europe cannot be at once a leader in new technologies, a model of climate responsibility and an independent actor on the world stage, and it will not be able to finance its social model. Draghi's reasoning about values follows: if Europe can no longer provide prosperity, equity, freedom, peace and democracy, it has lost its reason for being.

One year on (speech) and two years on. A year after the report, in his speech of 16 September 2025, Draghi said:
“Over the past year, each of these challenges has grown more acute.”

Draghi, one-year review speech, 16 September 2025

He cited the highest US tariffs since the Smoot-Hawley era (the 1930s US tariff act) and a rise of almost 20% in China's trade surplus with the EU since December 2024. He also said that reliance on the US for defence was quoted as one reason Europe accepted a trade deal largely on American terms. These are his words about 2025; the two-years-on tracker below covers what was done.

Weight: these are the report's premises, and Draghi's own 2025 speech says they have strengthened; they frame the argument rather than test it, which is why the checks sit in the sections that follow.

3

The innovation gap: research, AI, cloud, telecoms, skills

The report calls this the first and most important area for action. Its Part B chapters on digitalisation, innovation and skills carry the detail.

The report's reasoning Part A, ch. 2

The chain runs as follows. A static industrial structure produces low investment and low innovation. Public research money is spread too thin and too bureaucratic. Companies that do reach the growth stage meet fragmented markets and heavy regulation, and so they seek American venture capital and scale up in the US. The report makes the public-money point with one comparison:

“a budget of EUR 256 million for 2024, compared with USD 4.1 billion for US Defence Advanced Research Projects Agency (DARPA)”

Part A, chapter 2: the European Innovation Council's Pathfinder instrument against DARPA, the US defence research agency

On regulation it describes a precautionary approach that dictates business practices in advance, and picks the AI Act as its example:

“the AI Act imposes additional regulatory requirements on general purpose AI models that exceed a pre-defined threshold of computational power”

Part A, chapter 2 (the report adds that some state-of-the-art models already exceed the threshold)

It adds that national “gold plating” of EU law (adding national requirements beyond the EU minimum) and limits on data storage and processing raise costs, so that only larger and often non-EU firms can bear compliance. On cloud computing the report recommends against trying to build challengers to the US providers, but wants a competitive European sovereign-cloud segment:

“It is too late for the EU to try and develop systematic challengers to the major US cloud providers”

Part A, chapter 2; the report still wants a competitive European industry for “sovereign cloud” segments where security demands it

The numbers behind the sector are: around 70% of foundation models (large general-purpose AI models trained on broad data) developed in the US since 2017, three US hyperscalers (very large cloud-computing providers) with over 65% of the European cloud market, and the largest European cloud operator at 2%. For telecoms, Part A says there are

“34 mobile network operator groups in the EU and only a handful in the US or China”

Part A, chapter 2 (Part B counts 34 mobile network operators against three in the US and four in China)

and that this fragmentation, in part the result of a negative stance on mergers, makes network investment harder to finance. Part B adds that “the ambitions of the EU’s GDPR and AI Act are commendable”, but that their complexity and risk of overlaps and inconsistencies can undermine AI development by EU industry. The proposals follow. Reform the next Framework Programme (FP), the EU's research budget, and double it to EUR 200 billion per seven years, conditional on reforms; turn the European Innovation Council (EIC) into a genuine ARPA-type agency (modelled on US agencies that fund high-risk research); double the support to the European Research Council; adopt the Unitary Patent in all member states; create an optional EU-wide company statute, the “Innovative European Company”, which Draghi's 2025 speech calls a “28th regime”; set up an AI Vertical Priorities Plan across ten strategic sectors; define telecom markets at EU level and give investment commitments more weight in merger review. On skills the report finds, among other things, that the EU turns out about 850 STEM (science, technology, engineering and maths) graduates per million inhabitants a year against more than 1,100 in the US, and that roughly EUR 64 billion of EU spending on skills has produced limited results; it proposes systematic skills data, common certification, a reform of adult learning and a Tech Skills Acquisition Programme to attract talent from outside the EU.

Draghi, speech of 16 September 2025, on the AI Act's high-risk stage

4

Decarbonisation and competitiveness: energy, clean tech, cars, heavy industry

The report's second area for action: a joint plan so that climate goals and industrial strength point the same way.

The report's reasoning Part A, ch. 3

“EU companies still face electricity prices that are 2-3 times those in the US. Natural gas prices paid are 4-5 times higher.”

Part A, Foreword

The report attributes the gap primarily to Europe's lack of natural resources, but also to market design. Europe is the world's largest gas buyer yet bargains weakly and relies on spot prices; gas derivative trading is concentrated; taxes and network charges add a wedge. The market-design point has a number attached. In 2022, at the peak of the energy crisis, gas set the price of electricity

“63% of the time, despite making up only 20% share of the EU’s electricity mix”

Part A, chapter 3

so that even a growing share of cheap renewables and nuclear did not reach the bill. The proposals: joint purchasing at least for liquefied natural gas; long-term power purchase agreements and two-way contracts for difference (CfDs) extended to all renewable and nuclear assets; lower energy taxation through a common ceiling on surcharges; faster permitting; and, for grids, a “28th regime” for cross-border interconnectors, a larger Connecting Europe Facility and a permanent European coordinator.

On China the report refuses both extremes. Copying the US habit of shutting out Chinese technology would raise the cost of the transition, but a pure laissez-faire approach would risk jobs and economic security. It writes:

“Black-and-white solutions are unlikely to be successful in the European context.”

Part A, chapter 3

It then sorts industries into four cases. In the first, Europe's cost disadvantage is too large to compete, so it should import the technology and diversify suppliers. In the second, the concern is where production happens but not whose technology it is, so inward investment plus trade measures. In the third, Europe wants to keep know-how and capacity for a crisis, so local-content requirements and joint ventures. In the fourth, “infant industries” where Europe has an edge, a full range of protective measures until scale is reached. In clean tech the main proposal is a minimum quota for locally made components in public procurement and CfD auctions, since the Net Zero Industry Act sets manufacturing targets without quotas. On cars the report writes:

“applying a climate policy without an industrial policy”

Part A, chapter 3: the automotive sector as an example of a lack of EU planning

and proposes an industrial roadmap with a technology-neutral path to emission cuts. For energy-intensive industries (chemicals, metals, minerals, paper) it estimates decarbonisation costs of EUR 500 billion over 15 years and proposes earmarking more revenue from the EU emissions trading system for them. Its hedge on trade is explicit: “Emulating the US approach of systematically shutting out Chinese technology would likely set back the energy transition”. Defensive measures are reserved for specific cases (see section 5).

  • Clean-tech local content. The Industrial Accelerator Act, proposed on 4 March 2026, introduces made-in-EU rules that critics say go beyond targeted quotas (Latham; EPRS; Bruegel; 4/5). Proposed
  • Cars. On 16 December 2025 the Commission proposed cutting the 2035 CO2 target from 100% to 90%, with the rest offset; not adopted as of the last check (Commission; Euronews; 5/5). Proposed

In his 2025 speech Draghi calls the Clean Industrial Deal and the Action Plan for Affordable Energy “both consistent with the report’s agenda”. His assessment is that the main step so far, relaxing state aid so governments can subsidise prices, may offer temporary relief but:

“It does not fix the structural reasons why energy in Europe is so expensive.”

Draghi, speech of 16 September 2025

He asked for a technology-neutral review of the car rules in the same speech. The Commission's December 2025 proposal relaxes the 2035 target to 90%; the speech predates it and does not comment on that figure.

Weight: the price gap itself is solid, and the structural causes his 2025 speech lists (the gas price level, gas setting the electricity price most of the time, and high charges and taxes) have not been addressed by the main step taken; the four-case trade logic is a policy judgment that critics contest on cost grounds.

5

Security and dependencies: raw materials, chips, defence, space

The third area for action. The report separates dependencies that are two-way from those that can become instruments of coercion.

The report's reasoning Part A, ch. 4

Around 40% of Europe's imports come from a small number of suppliers and are hard to substitute, and about half of those come from countries it is not strategically aligned with. Critical raw materials are the clearest case: China is the largest processor of nickel, copper, lithium and cobalt, with between 35% and 70% of processing, and export restrictions from China grew ninefold between 2009 and 2020. In chips, the EU has no foundry producing below 22 nm (a chip-manufacturing process size; smaller means more advanced) and relies on Asia for 75–90% of wafer fabrication capacity. The report is realistic about how far ownership should go:

“While EU ownership of large foundries may be unrealistic at this stage”

Part A, chapter 4: the sentence continues that this is owing to the required investment levels, and that Europe should still maximise joint efforts in innovation and advanced segments

Its trade rule is also hedged:

“Unless there is an overriding geopolitical imperative, defensive measures should therefore not be applied systematically.”

Part A, chapter 1, Box 1 on trade policy

On defence the case is about scale and fragmentation. Aggregate EU defence spending is about one third of US levels; EU defence research and development was EUR 10.7 billion in 2022, 4.5% of spending, against 16% in the US. The industry is split along national lines:

“twelve different types of battle tanks are operated in Europe, whereas the US produces only one”

Part A, Foreword

Money is also spent apart:

“European collaborative procurement accounted for only 18% of expenditure on defence equipment procurement in 2022”

Part A, chapter 4 (the sentence continues: well below the European Defence Agency benchmark of 35%)

“78% of procurement spending went to non-EU suppliers, out of which 63% went to the US”

Part A, chapter 4, for June 2022 to June 2023; Part B calls the choice to buy American part of the legacy of the Second World War and the Cold War, concedes it may be justified where Europe lacks the product, and says that in many other cases a European equivalent exists

For space, public spending was USD 15 billion in Europe in 2023 against USD 73 billion in the US, and the European Space Agency's geographical-return rule, under which each country receives contracts in proportion to its contribution, fragments supply chains. The proposals: a Critical Raw Materials Platform for joint purchasing and EU-level stockpiles; a centralised EU budget line and a fast-track project of common interest for semiconductors; aggregation of defence demand, European preference in procurement, consolidation where scale brings efficiency, and more common funding for defence research; and removing the geographical-return rule, plus a Space Industrial Fund acting as an anchor customer.

6

Financing: EUR 750–800 billion a year, a capital markets union, common debt

The chapter in which the argument turns from what Europe should do to who pays.

The report's reasoning Part A, ch. 5

“a minimum annual additional investment of EUR 750 to 800 billion is needed”

Part A, chapter 5: based on the latest Commission estimates, 4.4–4.7% of EU GDP in 2023, lifting the investment share from about 22% to about 27% of GDP

The report compares the effort with the Marshall Plan, the US aid programme for postwar Europe:

“the additional investments provided by the Marshall Plan between 1948-51 amounted to around 1-2% of GDP annually”

Part A, Foreword

Part B adds a caveat on the total itself:

“the aggregate total is likely to be an underestimate”

Part B, investment chapter: it does not fully capture economic security, skills, climate adaptation or environmental protection

Who pays? Historically about four-fifths of productive investment in Europe is private and one-fifth public. Commission and IMF simulations of a sustained investment push of around 5% of GDP find it feasible, raising output by about 6% within 15 years with transitional inflation pressure that fades. But the report says the private sector is unlikely to carry it alone. Chapter 1 puts it this way:

“the private sector is unlikely to be able to finance the lion’s share of this investment”

Part A, chapter 1 (the sentence continues: without public sector support)

The Foreword is firmer:

“the private sector will not be able to bear the lion’s share of financing investment without public sector support”

Part A, Foreword

Productivity is the lever that makes the public part affordable. In the report's illustration, a rise of 2% in total factor productivity (TFP, the part of output growth not explained by more labour or capital) within ten years

“could already be sufficient to cover up to one third of the fiscal spending”

Part A, chapter 5 (the spending in question is investment subsidies and government investment)

The capital markets union (CMU) is the second lever: a single securities regulator along the lines of the US SEC, unified clearing and settlement, harmonised insolvency law, revived securitisation, a stronger pension pillar and a completed banking union. The third is common funding. The report's reasoning is that a common safe asset would help the CMU, and its language is firm on that point:

“It is unquestionable that the issuance of a common safe asset would make the CMU much easier to achieve and more complete.”

Part A, chapter 5

Part A treats common funding in two steps. For key European public goods such as breakthrough innovation it says:

“some joint funding for investment in key European public goods, such as breakthrough innovation, will be necessary”

Part A, Foreword

For defence procurement and cross-border grids it adds a condition:

“If the political and institutional conditions are met, these projects would also call for common funding.”

Part A, Foreword

Part A adds that issuing such assets more systematically would require stronger fiscal rules matching more common debt with a more sustainable path of national debt, and Part B says issuance

“Issuance would also have to remain mission and project specific.”

Part B, investment chapter

The report also says that if reforms fail and productivity does not pick up, a broader issuance of public debt may be needed. It suggests that member states could consider deferring repayment of the NextGenerationEU recovery borrowing.

7

Governance: focus, speed, simplification

The report says a new industrial strategy will not succeed without changes to how the EU decides. One critic, Jacques Pelkmans (CEPS), calls it a little too easy.

The report's reasoning Part A, ch. 6

The diagnosis names three barriers: Europe lacks focus (it sets objectives but few priorities), it wastes common resources (spread across national and EU instruments) and it does not coordinate where it matters. Decisions are made issue by issue with multiple veto players, and the report puts a number on the delay:

“an average time of 19 months to agree new laws”

Part A, Foreword and chapter 6: from the Commission's proposal to the signing of the act, measured in the first half of the 2019–2024 parliamentary term

The Foreword's own call to action is stated in plain language:

“We should abandon the illusion that only procrastination can preserve consensus.”

Part A, Foreword

The remedy is deliberately short of treaty change:

“Strengthening the EU requires Treaty changes, but it is not a precondition for Europe to move forward”

Part A, chapter 6

It proposes three things. Refocus: a Competitiveness Coordination Framework in which the European Council sets a few priorities, with Competitiveness Action Plans, a Competitiveness Pillar in the next EU budget and more rigorous use of the subsidiarity principle (that the EU should act only where it adds value). Speed: extend qualified majority voting (QMV, decisions by a weighted majority instead of unanimity) through the treaties' passerelle clause (a clause allowing a switch from unanimity to qualified majority), which needs a unanimous upfront decision; where action is still blocked, use enhanced cooperation among willing member states. Simplify: a new Commission Vice President for Simplification, a single method for measuring regulatory cost, and the full implementation of the announced 25% cut in reporting obligations with a further cut of up to 50% for small and medium-sized firms.

The regulatory-volume comparison is where the report reaches for a number: around 13,000 acts passed by the EU against around 3,500 pieces of legislation and around 2,000 resolutions at US federal level over 2019–2024. The two parts of the report hedge the comparison in slightly different words. Part A says:

“While direct comparisons are obscured by different political and legal systems”

Part A, chapter 6

“While direct comparisons are prevented by different political and legal systems”

Part B, governance chapter

Stepping back

The error direction in this run. Three of the 15 checked claims are adverse (c9, c11, c12), and they were identified after checking, in a single run: this is not a base rate. Two of the three lean toward making the urgency look larger. c9: the report says “the additional investments provided by the Marshall Plan” while the check measures total aid; the gap is about 0.5–1.5 percentage points, and the report's point that a 5-point rise is unprecedented still holds. c12: Bruegel and the Kiel Report argue the 78% is not a reliable measure. c11 is different: the direction depends on what each count includes, and one critic puts the EU count higher, at about 30,000. Two other claims moved the opposite way, with the report understating: c8 (Part B says the total is likely to be an underestimate; the ECB now puts the need at about EUR 1,200 billion, though on a different method, a 2025–31 horizon and driven by defence) and c13 (collaborative defence procurement fell from 18% to 11%). The diagnosis numbers that carry the argument (c1, c5, c7, c13) hold, with caveats that the report partly states itself.

The messenger

Draghi was President of the European Central Bank from 2011 to 2019 and Prime Minister of Italy from 2021 to 2022. The report was written at the request of the Commission president, with thanks to her for her constant support and to the central bank for resources, and its cover says it does not represent the Commission's official position. The acknowledgments credit D’Aprile and Rouch with coordinating the work and Yiangou with drafting extensive parts of it, for the whole report. Those consulted include companies (among them Amazon, Google, Meta, Microsoft, ASML and Siemens), trade unions and NGOs.

The report's warning about fragmentation, against the record

Part A warned that member states were responding to the new environment with more assertive policies but, in its words,

“doing so in a fragmented way that undermines collective effectiveness”

Part A, chapter 1

The two-year record mostly shows that pattern: state-aid relaxation as the main energy step and collaborative defence procurement down to 11% (24% including third-country partners). Draghi's 2025 speech describes the same risk as two traps:

“uncoordinated national efforts, or blind faith that market forces will build new sectors”

Draghi, speech of 16 September 2025

What changed the premises

Three things moved after September 2024: US tariffs and the 2025 trade deal, defence spending (NATO's 5% target), and the European Central Bank's higher estimate of investment needs. The 2025 speech cites the tariffs and the European Central Bank figure directly, and describes defence commitments as adding to already vast financing needs.

What survives

  • The direction of the diagnosis. The productivity gap is mostly a technology gap: this holds in the report's own analysis excluding ICT (c2), and the headline gap figures hold as cited, though the size of the gap depends on the yardstick (c1).
  • The well-sourced structural facts. Start-up flight (c5), cloud concentration (c6, cloud part), the energy price gap (c7), the shortfall in collaborative defence procurement (c13) and the chip share (c15) each rest on primary or two independent sources.
  • The investment estimate. EUR 750–800 billion a year is a Commission estimate that Part B says is likely an underestimate; the European Central Bank's later, higher figure uses a different method and horizon (c8).
  • The hedges were on the page. The gap is only indicative, common funding is conditional, national champions are rejected, defensive trade measures are not systematic.
  • The warning about fragmentation. The report said fragmented national responses would undermine collective effectiveness; the status table mostly shows national and ad hoc steps.

What to discount

  • The report's comparisons (c9, c11, c12). The Marshall Plan figure (c9) and the defence-import share (c12) lean toward larger urgency; the direction of the legislation-count comparison (c11) depends on what each count includes. These are 3 of 15 checked claims, found after checking, in one run; c8 and c13 moved the other way, with the report understating.
  • The trackers' headline percentages. They count different units (383 measures, 30 recommendations) on different scales and cannot be set against one another.
  • Critics' measurement arguments, where they target figures the report itself labels indicative.
  • The Commission's self-reported count of 33 flagship initiatives. It counts proposals adopted, not outcomes.
