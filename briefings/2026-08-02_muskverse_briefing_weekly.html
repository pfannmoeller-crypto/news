Musk's "I got carried away" lasted six days: America PAC is back for the midterms, xAI sues Minnesota over its deepfake law, and Grok 4.6's spec quietly shrank while its ship date kept sliding

The week that followed the 28 July edition delivered fewer binary events than a market or launch catalyst — SpaceX's actual earnings and lock-up expiration are still two days out — but it delivered a clear political turn. Grok 4.7, previously described as an unconfirmed 4–6 trillion parameter model, is now reported at 2.1 trillion parameters — a similarly smaller figure than the earlier range — with a stated arrival "a few weeks" after 4.6. There was no update in this window on Grok Build's unresolved "out of scope" security-exploit classification from the prior data-exfiltration scandal.

Starship Flight 14 is now billed around a first tower-catch attempt of the ship itself — but two trackers disagree on when it flies

Following Flight 13's confirmed booster failure but clean upper-stage splashdown (see last edition), Musk said SpaceX will attempt its first-ever tower catch of the Starship upper stage — not just the booster — on the next flight, using the "Mechazilla" launch-tower arms at Starbase. Flight 14 will fly new Block 3 hardware, Booster 21 and Ship 41, and is expected to be a longer, multi-hour or multi-day mission given its more ambitious orbital profile, a departure from the roughly hour-long flights this briefing has covered to date. TechTimes reported on 30 July that the ship-catch attempt has been cleared to proceed.

Sourcing disagrees sharply on timing, in a pattern this briefing has now seen twice with Starship flight dates: one report cites SpaceX President Gwynne Shotwell's "fly every month" cadence target, implying a launch attempt before the end of August once Booster 21 reaches flight readiness, while a separate flight-tracking service lists a placeholder date of 30 September. This briefing treats both as unconfirmed and is not picking a side pending an official SpaceX launch-window announcement.

Context & background. Last edition's Flight 13 card closed with a watch-item asking "whether Flight 14 attempts another landing burn or reverts to an expendable-booster profile while the issue is diagnosed." The answer that has emerged is more ambitious than either option: rather than simplifying the mission profile after two consecutive V3 booster failures, SpaceX is reportedly adding an entirely new, harder objective — catching the ship itself, something it has not previously attempted — on top of an already-unresolved booster-landing problem. That sequencing is notable on its own terms, independent of whether the date holds.

The details. A tower catch of the upper stage is mechanically a different and, by most public technical commentary, harder problem than catching the booster, which SpaceX has demonstrated successfully in the past: the ship is lighter, faster, and returns from a higher-energy trajectory, and previous flights have relied on ocean splashdown for the ship specifically because that capability had not yet been attempted. Layering that milestone onto a booster design that has now failed its landing burn twice in a row (Flight 13) and failed at stage separation once (May) means Flight 14 carries two separate unresolved reliability questions rather than one.

Context. This is consistent with a pattern this briefing has now tracked across Starship, Grok 4.6, and HW3 remediation: ambitious public framing of the next milestone arrives before the previous one's problems are fully diagnosed or resolved. Whether that reflects genuine engineering confidence or optimistic public communication is not something this briefing can adjudicate from the sourcing available; it can only note that the pattern is now recurring across multiple Musk-linked ventures, not isolated to one.

Watch-Items. (1) An official SpaceX launch-window announcement that resolves the "end of August" vs. "30 September" discrepancy. (2) Whether Booster 21 reaches stated flight readiness on the timeline Shotwell described. (3) Whether SpaceX addresses the booster landing-burn failure mode before Flight 14, or flies with the same unresolved issue. (4) Whether the ship-catch attempt proceeds as planned, given it is a genuinely new capability rather than a repeat of a previously-attempted maneuver.

XAINew suit filedfiled 27 Jul 2026

xAI sues Minnesota to block the country's first state law against AI "nudification" apps, days before it takes effect

xAI filed a federal lawsuit on 27 July in the U.S. District Court for the District of Minnesota against state Attorney General Keith Ellison, seeking to block HF 1606 — a law, passed in April and taking effect 1 August, that bans apps and models used to generate nonconsensual sexualized "deepfake" images and carries civil penalties of $500,000 per violation. xAI argues the law is unconstitutionally overbroad under the First Amendment and imposes strict liability with no exception for platforms that already deploy technical safeguards, regardless of intent. The company states in its filing that it "strictly prohibits" users from generating nude or sexualized images without consent and has previously sued users who evaded its blockers to do so anyway. The law's sponsors have said nudification victims are "overwhelmingly women and children."

The suit arrives against a specific backdrop this briefing has not previously reported: xAI is separately being sued by Ashley St. Clair — the mother of one of Musk's children — over sexual images Grok reportedly generated of her, a case that predates this window but that multiple outlets covering the Minnesota suit cited as relevant context.

Context & background. This is a new legal thread this briefing has not previously tracked, distinct from Musk v. Altman and the xAI-OpenAI trade-secrets suit covered below. It sits at the intersection of two ongoing stories this briefing has followed separately: xAI's Grok product and its recurring content-moderation controversies (the "out of scope" security-exploit classification from the prior data-exfiltration scandal remains unresolved, as noted in the xAI section above), and Musk's broader pattern of using litigation to challenge regulation he views as overbroad, seen previously in disputes with advertisers and regulators over X.

The details. Minnesota's HF 1606 is described by multiple outlets as the first state-level law specifically targeting AI "nudification" tools, as opposed to broader deepfake or non-consensual-imagery statutes that already exist in many states. xAI's central legal argument is a strict-liability objection: the law, as xAI's filing describes it, attaches liability regardless of whether a platform has deployed state-of-the-art technical guardrails or explicitly prohibits the conduct in its terms of service, and xAI argues the $500,000-per-violation penalty could, at scale, expose it to fines in the tens of billions of dollars for content generated by users evading its safeguards. Separately and not part of this suit, Ashley St. Clair's litigation against xAI over Grok-generated images of her is the kind of case the Minnesota law's sponsors have cited as exactly the harm the statute is meant to address — a juxtaposition several outlets covering this story noted explicitly.

Context. Beyond the immediate legal question, this suit is a portfolio-relevant test case for how xAI intends to respond to state-level AI regulation generally, at a moment when Grok's own safety and moderation track record is already under scrutiny from the unresolved Grok Build classification and the St. Clair suit. A company simultaneously defending against a suit alleging its product generated harmful content, while suing to block a law meant to penalize exactly that outcome, is a stance this briefing expects to draw continued attention regardless of the suit's legal merits.

edit_noteEditor's view

A week where the political story moved fastest, and the product and market stories kept drifting rather than resolving

1. "I got carried away" turned out to describe a pause, not a change. Six days after that comment, Musk authorized reviving a PAC that spent over a quarter-billion dollars in the last cycle. This briefing declined last week to read the remark as a substantive walkback, and the America PAC revival is about as direct a confirmation of that caution as this briefing is likely to get.

2. xAI is now litigating on both sides of the same underlying problem. The company is being sued over Grok allegedly generating nonconsensual sexual imagery, and in the same window it sued a state to block a law penalizing exactly that outcome. Whichever way the Minnesota case resolves legally, the juxtaposition is the more durable story for anyone tracking xAI's regulatory posture.

3. Grok 4.6's credibility problem compounded rather than resolved. Last week it was a slipping date; this week the model's own reported size changed too, with no xAI statement addressing either shift. Two consecutive weeks of unreconciled changes to the same unreleased model is now itself the more reportable fact than any single spec number.

4. Both Musk-linked stocks are drifting toward, not away from, their weakest levels. TSLA tested within 1.4% of its 52-week low; SPCX fell another 4% into its own catalyst window. Neither has broken down further, but neither has found real support either — both remain unresolved bets heading into confirmed near-term events (SpaceX's 4 August earnings, and whatever comes next for Tesla).

5. Starship keeps adding ambition before subtracting risk. A first-ever ship-catch attempt, layered onto an unresolved two-flight booster failure pattern, is the same "announce the harder next step before the last one is fully understood" pattern this briefing has now observed across Starship, Grok, and Tesla's HW3 remediation in recent editions.

A week that was quieter on hard corporate catalysts — SpaceX's actual earnings and lock-up are still two days out at the time of writing — but louder on the political and product-communication fronts, where this briefing has the least ability to independently verify claims and the most reason to keep flagging exactly what is and isn't confirmed. The next edition should have SpaceX's actual 4 August earnings and 6 August unlock reaction to report, along with, possibly, a firmer date for both Grok 4.6 and Starship Flight 14.
