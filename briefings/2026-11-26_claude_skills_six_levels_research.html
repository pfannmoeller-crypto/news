A popular explainer says Anthropic’s engineers stopped building a new agent for every task and load one general agent with “skills” instead. What a skill is, the six levels the video teaches, which claims hold, and what the research and the multi-agent camp say.

Key takeaways

  • The idea. Instead of building many specialised agents, use one general agent (such as Claude Code) and give it expertise through skills: folders with a SKILL.md file of instructions, plus optional examples, templates and scripts. The framing comes from an Anthropic talk, “Don’t Build Agents, Build Skills Instead” (late 2025).
  • The six levels. Let Claude write skills with Anthropic’s skill-creator; do a task once and then turn the session into a skill; record your screen; use skills that are not step lists (data pointers, design systems, one-line definitions); install other people’s skills after vetting them; and sometimes run without skills to see what a newer model does unaided.
  • The facts mostly hold. Launch date, open standard, the screen-recording feature, the partner directory and Anthropic’s 80% cut of Claude Code’s system prompt are confirmed. One explanation is oversimplified: relaying Anthropic’s chart, he calls the agent “harness” “in its essence just a set of system prompts” plus your own context; Anthropic’s descriptions also include tools, code execution and a file system. The title overstates the talk; his spoken line, that the engineers stopped building a new agent “for every single task”, is close to it.
  • The research is more cautious than the video. 2026 preprints find curated skills help on average, self-written skills often do not, and large skill libraries make the right skill harder to pick. That supports levels 5 and 6 more than “skill max it”.
  • Who is talking. The presenter sells a $97-a-month community and courses on building agents with Claude Code; the video largely builds on Anthropic’s own talking points and adds his practitioner recipe.
Part I

The video

What the presenter says, in his order, with a check box wherever a factual claim carries the argument. Level names are his.

Who is speaking

Jay E runs RoboNuggets, a paid online community on the Skool platform, and says he spent over a decade working with well-known brands and has worked in AI since a master’s in data science (not verified). The video promotes his community, a Claude Code masterclass and “Rubric”, his software for working with agents.

The claim in one paragraph

Building a new agent for every job is like buying a new computer for every task. The model is the chip, the agent is the operating system, and skills are the apps: install one and the same agent can do something new. One well-equipped general agent beats a “fleet of hundreds”, the way a manager would rather lead a small team of A-players than 100 people.

“The fewer agents that you have in principle, the better.” — Jay E
1

Why one agent with skills 0:00

He cites Barry Zhang of Anthropic, who said the team used to build many agents until they found that “the agent underneath is actually more universal than we thought”: a general agent like Claude Code can already work in several fields, and what is usually missing is your own expertise. That expertise is what a skill supplies.

Check 1 · The Anthropic talk · C4/5Holds, with a headline paraphrase. Barry Zhang and Mahesh Murag of Anthropic gave “Don’t Build Agents, Build Skills Instead” at the AI Engineer Code Summit in November 2025 (online from 8 Dec 2025). The quoted sentence is in the talk, as is the comparison of models to processors and of the runtime to an operating system; the “skills are applications” step was not seen in the transcripts we could check. The video’s title, that Anthropic’s engineers “stopped building AI agents”, overstates the talk, which argues for skills on top of one general agent, not for abandoning agents; his spoken line (“stopped building new agents for every single task”) is close to the talk.

He then explains Anthropic’s layer picture: the model at the core; around it the “agent harness”, which he describes as “in its essence just a set of system prompts” injected into every chat, plus your own context such as CLAUDE.md files and memory; and on top the skills. In practice, he says, “don’t make a lot of agents” means not creating many separate workspace folders, each with its own instructions and memory. He keeps one workspace for everything, with by his count 600,000 files.

Check 2 · “The harness is just system prompts” · C3/5Oversimplified. He hedges with “in its essence” and adds your own context and memory to the layer. In the talk the runtime is a model coupled to an environment, with scaffolding “as thin as just bash and file system”: the agent reads and writes files, runs code and connects to external tools. Anthropic’s own descriptions of its harness include the agent loop, tools, code execution and context management. System prompts are one part of it.
2

What a skill is, and what changed in a year 2:05

In Zhang’s words, skills are “essentially just folders”. The key file is SKILL.md. At its top sit a name and a description, which he says “probably” matter most, because Claude matches your request against them to decide whether to open the folder at all: the label on the toolbox. Inside are steps in plain English, and optionally examples, templates or custom software.

Check 3 · How skills load · C5/5Holds. Anthropic’s documentation requires a name (up to 64 characters) and a description (up to 1,024). Only these, roughly 100 tokens per skill, sit permanently in the agent’s context; the body is read when a request matches, and bundled files only when needed. Anthropic calls this progressive disclosure.
Background · skills, agents, MCPAn agent here is a model running in a loop with tools, able to act on files and software. A skill is packaged know-how the agent loads when relevant. MCP (Model Context Protocol) is a separate Anthropic-led standard that connects agents to outside services such as a CRM or a database. Skills teach the agent how; MCP gives it access.

Two things changed since launch, he says. Skills became an open standard that more than 70 platforms adopted, including ChatGPT, Cursor and GitHub Copilot, so a skill built for Claude can work elsewhere and survives a switch of provider. And skills grew from written standard operating procedures into something broader.

Check 4 · Launch and open standard · C4/5Holds. Anthropic announced skills on 16 Oct 2025 and published the format as an open standard on 18 Dec 2025. In October 2026 it handed the standard to the Agentic AI Foundation under the Linux Foundation and cited more than 70 platforms, including ChatGPT, Cursor, GitHub Copilot and VS Code, plus 1 million published skills and about 280 million installs. The dates are primary; the adoption figures are self-reported by Anthropic and were not independently verified.
3

Levels 0 to 3: how to make a skill 5:24

Level 0, writing a skill by hand, he skips: “I never recommend this to anyone.” Level 1 is asking Claude to write skills with Anthropic’s skill-creator skill, and telling Claude to use it by default so every new skill follows Anthropic’s best practices. His example: a consultant asks for a skill that turns call notes into a one-page proposal and attaches their best past proposals for layout and tone.

Check 5 · skill-creator · C3/5Partly holds. Anthropic offers a skill-creator skill for guided skill building. We found no Anthropic page confirming that it is preinstalled in every Claude app; in Claude Code it may require adding Anthropic’s skills collection.

Level 2 is his recommended route, because most needed automations only show up during the work: do the task once with Claude, correct it as you would an intern, and at the end ask it to turn the session into a skill. Each later run with new edge cases refines it. He uses this for the animations in his own videos, with a skill he keeps refining, and says this is how he and many advanced users he knows work.

“Do it once, then skillify it so that the next time you do this same task, it is already automated at a certain degree.” — Jay E

Level 3 is for visual, click-heavy tasks: in the Claude desktop app, choose “record a skill”, do the task while narrating as if training a new hire, and Claude proposes a skill from the recording. His example: logging into a supplier website that has no connector.

Check 6 · “Record a skill” · C4/5Holds, with a scope note. The feature launched on 21 Jul 2026 in Cowork in the Claude desktop app for Mac, under the + menu, for paid plans. It is not available on the Free plan.
4

Level 4: skills that are not step lists 10:16

His point: a skill “can be as big as a library or even as small as one sentence”. He names four types from his own practice:

  • Database skills point to your own data, such as each customer’s purchase history, communications and lifetime value, so automations can personalise messages.
  • Artifact skills hold a design system (colours, fonts, layouts); his on-brand PDF guides are made this way.
  • Definition skills pin down what a word means to you; his “wildcard” skill tells Claude what he means by wild ideas.
  • One-liners, such as Matt Pocock’s popular “grill me” skill, a few lines telling Claude to interview you about your plan one question at a time.
Check 7 · The skill types · C3/5The example holds; the taxonomy is his. Matt Pocock’s grill-me skill exists in his published collection; it interviews the user about a plan, though we could not confirm the “one question at a time” detail. The four type names are the presenter’s own; no external source uses them.
5

Level 5: other people’s skills 12:20

Many skills already exist for free. He points to Anthropic’s directory, with partner skills from Notion, Figma and Atlassian, and to skills.sh from Vercel, which ranks skills by installs. Because of the open standard, skills written for other tools usually work in Claude. His advice: let Claude browse a directory and recommend what fits, use only trusted sources, have the agent check a skill for safety before installing, and adapt it to your setup.

Check 8 · Directories · C5/5Holds. Anthropic’s directory launched on 18 Dec 2025 with Notion, Canva, Figma and Atlassian among the partners. Vercel launched skills.sh on 20 Jan 2026 as a directory and leaderboard; it tracks installs, though the page does not state the ranking rule.
Check 9 · Is vetting needed? · C4/5Yes, and the risk is measured. Snyk’s “ToxicSkills” scan of 3,984 skills from ClawHub and skills.sh (Feb 2026) found at least one flaw in 36.8% and a critical issue in 13.4%, with 76 confirmed malicious payloads. The top 100 on skills.sh had no critical findings; the open ClawHub marketplace was worse. Snyk sells security tooling, which is worth knowing when reading its numbers.
6

Level 6: sometimes, no skills at all 13:31

The last level reverses the rest. He cites Boris Cherny, creator and technical lead of Claude Code, who said in an interview that every six months you should delete your CLAUDE.md files, skills and hooks and see what the model does. The reason: with newer models such as Fable and Opus 5.5, detailed instructions written for older models can hold the output back. He says he “probably” would not recommend deleting files; instead, run a task once with and once without the skill and compare.

“Instead of helping you become more ambitious, your skills can actually end up holding the model back.” — Jay E

He calls it the bell-curve meme: simple at the start, ever more knowledge piled on in the middle, simple again at the end. He says Cherny has a name for it, “hobbling”, when the model could do something and you are getting in its way, and that the Claude Code team cut its system prompt by 80% for this reason. His “wildcard” skill serves the same purpose: inviting the model to surprise him.

Check 10 · Cherny’s advice · C3/5Mostly holds. In a Y Combinator interview around the Opus 5 release (summer 2026), Cherny recommended deleting CLAUDE.md files, skills and hooks every six months because the model may not need the instructions. His advice was aimed at Claude Code users. We found no source attributing the word “hobbling” to him; coverage uses the AI-research term “unhobbling”, so the captions may have dropped a syllable. The sense, a model held back by its instructions, matches.
Check 11 · The 80% cut · C4/5Holds. Anthropic’s Thariq Shihipar wrote on 24 Jul 2026 that more than 80% of Claude Code’s system prompt was removed for the newest models with no measurable loss on Anthropic’s own coding evaluations (self-reported), because old rules often conflicted with what the models now do by default. Anthropic’s advice there is to keep CLAUDE.md files and skills lean, not to abolish them.
Check 12 · The models named · C5/5Holds. Anthropic released Claude Opus 5.5 on 22 Sep 2026; Claude Fable is its top model tier.

His hedges: skills “usually” work across tools; level 2 gets him “mostly good results”; and having fewer agents is “probably a better idea”, not a rule.

A · “One general agent, packaged expertise” primary texts · C4–5/5

Barry Zhang and Mahesh Murag (Anthropic); Simon Willison; Armin Ronacher; Walden Yan (Cognition, 2025)

Willison called skills “maybe a bigger deal than MCP” at launch, because they are cheap in context and simple to write. Ronacher moved his MCP use to skills. Cognition’s 2025 essay “Don’t Build Multi-Agents” argued that parallel agents make conflicting hidden decisions.

Difference from the video: none on the core idea; the video adds the practitioner recipe and a sales pitch for his community.

B · “Multi-agent where it pays” primary posts · C4–5/5

Anthropic’s research-system team; LangChain; Cosine; Walden Yan (April 2026 update)

Anthropic’s own multi-agent research system, a lead agent with subagents, beat a single agent by 90.2% on an internal research evaluation, at about 15 times the tokens of a chat; Anthropic says coding is a poor fit for that design. Yan’s 2026 follow-up keeps one agent writing but finds read-only reviewer agents pay off.

Difference from the video: the video’s “fewer agents is better” is right for most individual users, but even Anthropic uses several agents for broad research. Anthropic argues both positions for different jobs.

C · “Skills help, less than claimed” 2026 preprints via summaries · C3/5

SkillsBench (summarised by O’Reilly); Skill-Use benchmark; “Demystifying Agent Skills”; Atlassian guidance

Curated skills raised task pass rates by 16.2% on average, from about 4.5% in software engineering to about 52% in healthcare, while skills that models wrote for themselves gave no consistent benefit. How reliably a model loads the right skill ranged from about 32% to 97% depending on the model. In one study, as the library grew from 5 to 100 skills, correct-skill use fell from 29.6% to 3.3%, and skills were misapplied in 10% of runs against 0.8% without skills. Atlassian recommends fewer than five skills per agent.

Difference from the video: supports vetting, pruning and level 6, puts limits on “skill maxing” one agent, and qualifies level 2: model-written skills underperform, though none of the studies tested the human-corrected “do it once” route the video recommends. Most tested non-Claude models.

D · “MCP still matters” blogs · C3/5

Mintlify; David Coffee; The New Stack

Skills describe how to do a job; connectors give access to live systems with permissions. A “database skill” that points to customer data still needs a connector or files behind it.

Difference from the video: small; the video’s supplier-website example only works because there is no connector.

E · “Shared skills are a supply chain” security research · C4/5

Snyk; Palo Alto Networks Unit 42

Skills can run shell commands and carry hidden instructions; malicious ones have been found in open marketplaces, mostly using prompt injection.

Difference from the video: agrees on vetting, but “have your agent check it” relies on the same agent the attack targets.

Where they really disagree

  • 1 · One agent or many? One for most users and coding; several for broad, parallel research.
  • 2 · How many skills? The video says one agent can be “skill maxed” but ends by urging pruning; the studies say routing degrades as libraries grow.
  • 3 · Who writes them? Curated by experts helps; written by the model alone often does not.
  • 4 · How long do they stay useful? Anthropic now says instructions age with each model generation; the video’s level 6 agrees.

Who is speaking, and with what stake

Jay E sells a $97-a-month community (about 1,500 members), a Claude Code masterclass and an agent bootcamp; his claim that mastering skills puts you ahead of “99% of people” is unquantified; the video gives no basis. Anthropic is the source of the talk, the adoption figures and most checks, and benefits when skills become a standard. Snyk sells security tooling. The compiler is an Anthropic model.

◆

The big picture

Our synthesis of the positions above, not a forecast.

The video packages a real shift accurately in its main lines: agents have become general enough that the scarce input is the user’s own know-how, and skills are the format for handing it over. Where it goes beyond the evidence is in quantity. The research so far rewards few, focused, curated skills, and Anthropic itself now tells users to strip instructions back with each model generation. The video’s own level 6 is the strongest part of its argument, and it limits the earlier levels.
