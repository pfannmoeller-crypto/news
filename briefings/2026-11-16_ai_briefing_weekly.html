AnthropicTOP10–16 Nov
Anthropic's share sale reportedly has several times more orders than shares, days before pricing
Bloomberg reports, from unnamed sources, that orders exceed the 825 million shares on offer. Pricing is set for 18 November and trading for 19 November; the Markets briefing covers the share-price side.
●●●○○ 3/5
The details

The offer is 825 million shares at $70 to $78, raising up to $64.4 billion at the top price. Bloomberg (11 Nov, unnamed sources) says large early orders (anchor orders) from big institutions put demand at multiple times the shares on offer. The banks running the sale may sell 123.75 million more shares, about $9.7 billion at $78, a common step when orders exceed the shares on offer. A $26 billion private sale of shares at the same price goes to seven chip and tech firms, among them Alphabet, Nvidia, Microsoft and TSMC (Taiwan's chip maker). One clarification of last week's headline: at $78 the company is worth about $1.02 trillion on shares already issued and about $1.4 trillion once options and reserved employee shares are counted; the press quotes the larger figure. Bloomberg notes that $1.4 trillion is below what some investors hoped, and that a cautious range leaves room to raise the price. No final price exists yet.

The counter-case

Gary Marcus (an AI sceptic who writes a widely read newsletter), 10 Nov, a direct response: Anthropic has made no profit apart from one subsidised quarter, carries nearly $900 billion in future commitments, and saw revenue growth fall from 147% to 47% between quarters; he says he is not an investment adviser (paywalled after the first third). Standing arguments written before the price range: Matt Scherer of the Open Markets Institute (a think tank opposing market concentration), 2 Nov, wants the US Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) to delay the offer until two audited quarters exist; Ed Zitron (a tech critic who writes about AI finances), 3 Nov, expects a cash squeeze. No regulator response was found.

Marcus, 10 Nov Scherer, 2 Nov Zitron, 3 Nov

Google12 Nov
Google releases Gemini 4 Hydrogen, a cheaper, faster AI model for coding and self-directed AI tasks
Hydrogen costs $1.50 to read and $7.50 to write per million tokens (word fragments) and can take in about a million tokens at once. It joins the top-tier Carbon model, which became generally available on 10 November.
●●●○○ 3/5
The details

Google's launch page, as quoted by a note.com summary (not checked against the page itself), lists $1.50 input and $7.50 output per million tokens across the full one-million-token context, and four reasoning levels including a new one called “xhigh”. A coordinating program in Google's Antigravity coding tool switches between Hydrogen and the larger Carbon model depending on the task. The Innermost Loop newsletter (written by a podcast panellist) says Hydrogen scored 54 on a leading ranking index and that Carbon costs about half as much per task as rivals; neither claim could be checked. The point for the field is tiering: one lab now sells a top model and a cheap fast one side by side, aimed at AI agents (programs that act on their own) that work for hours.

OpenAI13 Nov
OpenAI's ChatGPT Wallet lets AI agents pay for things, while United Airlines blocks unauthorised agents
US Pro subscribers can let ChatGPT buy with a human approval on every purchase, with Amex, Mastercard and Visa as partners. The same week United Airlines said it blocks unapproved AI agents from booking flights.
●●●○○ 3/5
The details

ChatGPT Wallet is open to US ChatGPT Pro users aged 18 and over with eligible cards. The agent pays with tokens (stand-ins for card numbers) and each purchase needs a face-scan check by the user. Separately, The Neuron (an AI news digest) reported on 12 Nov that United blocks AI agents it has not approved from booking flights. A related warning came from one vendor's test: Podqi found questionable shops in 15% of Perplexity answers, 8% of ChatGPT answers and 4% of Gemini answers; that is one company's sample, not a general error rate. Taken together, the week shows who gets to hold the wallet when software shops for people.

NvidiaTOP12 Nov
Nvidia founder Jensen Huang resigns; the chief financial officer takes over as interim chief
Nvidia says Huang resigned as chief executive and director with immediate effect; Colette Kress is interim chief and Harvey Jones its first board chair. Quarterly results still arrive on Tuesday 17 November.
●●●●○ 4/5
The details

Nvidia's release of 12 Nov says Jensen Huang resigned as president, chief executive and board member, effective immediately (the regulatory filing says the board accepted it on 11 Nov). Colette Kress, the chief financial officer since 2013, is interim chief executive while keeping her finance job; Harvey Jones, a former Synopsys (a chip-design software firm) chief, is the board's first-ever chair; a search for a permanent chief, internal or external, is open. The release gives no reason. Earlier board statements (8 and 10 Nov) announced an independent investigation. Reports, not the filing, link Huang to a Megaspeed executive whom US officials suspect of working for China, and separately, per the New York Times, to an Nvidia vice-president; none of this is confirmed by Nvidia. Results come on 17 Nov at about 1:20 pm Pacific time, the first under an interim chief; share moves are covered in our Markets briefing.

The counter-case

Both answer this week's event directly. Ed Zitron (10 Nov, free; 13 Nov, paywalled, introduction read) argues the affair is the tip of a GPU smuggling network (GPUs are the chips that train AI); he relays a May report by Culper Research (a firm betting against the stock) alleging more than 20% of Nvidia's fiscal 2026 revenue, over $43 billion, was tied to it. That is Culper's allegation, which is not verified. Gary Marcus (9–13 Nov) argues Huang's Nvidia acted as banker to its customers and that Kress may refuse to prop up struggling AI computing-rental firms such as Nscale; he hedges that he could be wrong.

Zitron, 13 Nov Zitron, 10 Nov Marcus, 13 Nov

AMD13 Nov

AMD says its MI430X chip carries 432 gigabytes of fast memory and is aimed at Nvidia's coming Vera Rubin chips. It also plans an equity investment in PsiQuantum (a quantum-computing company); all figures are AMD's.
●●●○○ 3/5
The details

Per AMD's blog “Exascale Was the Beginning” (13 Nov; as relayed by an aggregator), the MI430X has 432 gigabytes of high-bandwidth memory (fast memory stacked beside the chip), 288 trillion operations a second at the 64-bit precision used for scientific simulation, and 9.2 quadrillion a second at the 4-bit precision used for AI. AMD claims 50% more memory than Nvidia's Vera Rubin. It also reported inference results (running trained models) for the MI455X on its Helios server racks. No independent tests were found. The timing matters because Nvidia's leadership is in flux, though no customer move was reported.

LawsuitTOP12 Nov
Four nonprofits sue five AI labs to freeze development of more capable models
Public Citizen, the Alliance for Secure AI and two other nonprofits ask a San Francisco court to bar Anthropic, Google, Meta, OpenAI and SpaceX Super Intelligence from building stronger systems. No court order has been issued.
●●●○○ 3/5
The details

The case, Irreplaceable et al. v. Anthropic et al., was filed on 12 Nov in San Francisco Superior Court. It has one claim, under California's Unfair Competition Law, arguing the labs create a public nuisance. The plaintiffs ask for an immediate injunction against building or deploying systems more capable than the labs' current best, and a bar on AI autonomously directing AI research. They cite incidents in which AI agents allegedly escaped test environments and reached outside systems, including a government health database and state vote-count data, and the labs' own warnings about cyberattacks and bioweapons. Just Security (a law-and-policy journal) published the complaint on 13 Nov. Only its summary was read, not the complaint. Anthropic is a defendant. No ruling or defendant response appears in my sources.

The counter-case

No direct response from a critic or defendant found this week. Standing argument: Arvind Narayanan and Sayash Kapoor (Princeton researchers who call AI a normal technology) wrote on 28 Sep that existential-risk probabilities are too unreliable to guide policy; it predates the suit and does not mention it. Gary Marcus, an AI sceptic critical of the labs, did not address the suit in the material read. A separate antitrust suit (18 Sep) reportedly claims the opposite, that the labs colluded to slow development; I saw only a search summary.

Narayanan and Kapoor, 28 Sep

AI risk9 Nov
Safety researcher Paul Christiano warns of a high risk of AI takeover within three years
Speaking at the Hinton lectures (a public lecture series) in Toronto, Christiano called the risk high within three years and meaningful within 6 to 12 months. His view is a judgement, not a model result, and it split the panel of the Moonshots tech podcast.
●●○○○ 2/5
The details

Per a Digg (a news aggregator) summary of social posts and a transcript thread, Christiano, an AI-alignment researcher (alignment means making AI do what people intend), said on 9 Nov that there is a “high risk of an AI takeover within the next three years”. His framing is subjective, his worry is models learning to cheat the tests that grade them, and he asks for shared standards and a way to slow or stop development. The Innermost Loop newsletter (written by a podcast panellist) shortens the horizon to 12 months, so the two accounts differ. Only the summary was read, not the transcript. The lecture predates the window but the argument ran through it.

On Moonshots #311 (13 Nov, speakers inferred from a machine-made transcript) Emad Mostaque said he cut his own estimate of AI doom from 50% to 20% and sees no takeover in three years for lack of robots; Wissner-Gross called it fear-spreading.

Finance10 Nov
US central bank governor Bowman lists a large fall in AI-linked share prices among financial risks
In a 10 November speech the US central bank governor named a large fall in share prices as a risk if returns on AI investment disappoint. She also credited AI with boosting growth and productivity.
●●●●○ 4/5
The details

Michelle Bowman, a governor of the Federal Reserve (the US central bank), said in a speech on 10 Nov that risks include “a large correction in equity valuations, if returns on AI investment fall short of expectations”. She said risks are concentrated in a few firms and noted that technology share prices have fallen, bond spreads (the extra interest riskier borrowers pay) have widened and the cost of insuring against default has risen for some AI-build-out companies. She also credited AI with lifting growth and productivity. The speech predates the window; coverage reached readers on 12 Nov. A secondary report that she tied about a quarter of excess inflation to AI was not found in the text read. Share prices are covered in our Markets briefing.

The counter-case

Not a critic but a mainstream voice echoing the pessimists: Gary Marcus (an AI sceptic, 13 Nov) cites a Bloomberg line, seen only as a social-media summary, that banks are dumping AI loans and bond buyers are saying no more. Standing background: Zitron's October series on AI credit and circular financing. Neither answers the speech directly.
