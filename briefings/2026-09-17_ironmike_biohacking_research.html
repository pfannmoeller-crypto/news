His argument in brief

This piece reports what was claimed and what the evidence shows, not what anyone should do — no dosing guidance, no protocol, no recommendation to take or avoid anything. Six theses follow, stated at the strength Steiner would sign them to. No verdicts land in this section; the check comes in Section 2.

Steiner holds no medical, pharmacological or nutritional-science qualification — a fact he states about himself on his own site, though he does not raise it in this conversation. The six theses below are this piece’s strongest reconstruction of his position, not a transcript of it. His stated standard of evidence is his own — anecdote and observation across roughly a thousand coaching clients, weighed above randomised trials — and it is presented as his, not the piece's.

The reference range describes a population, not a person

A man at the bottom of a lab's normal range, symptomatic, is being told he is fine by a system built to describe averages, not to diagnose him. If the number is technically in range but the man is not functioning, the range has failed him.

Hormone therapy done properly is replacement, not enhancement

The fear around testosterone therapy comes from conflating a clinical replacement dose, aimed at restoring a low value to normal, with a bodybuilding dose aimed at exceeding it. At the right dose, under supervision, it corrects a deficiency; it does not create a superhuman.

Peptides are where regeneration medicine is heading, and these are molecules the body already makes

BPC-157 comes from gastric juice. Thymosin β4 and thymosin α1 come from the thymus. On his account these are synthetic versions of compounds the body produces anyway, which is why he does not put them in the same category as a foreign drug, and why he thinks the reflexive alarm around the word peptide is misplaced.

The absence of a large trial is not the absence of evidence

Hundreds of people observed closely over years, by someone paying attention to what changes, is real information. Treating the randomised trial as the only currency worth accepting discards a category of knowledge that is imperfect, not worthless.

Saying nobody knows the long term is not the same as refusing to act

He says repeatedly that the long-term safety data does not exist, and goes on using and recommending these substances anyway. On his account that is not a contradiction but the actual choice in front of anyone: certainty is not on the menu, so what is left is disclosure, close observation, and a decision taken case by case with a doctor willing to engage.

Optimisation before diagnosis is a choice, not obviously the wrong one

Waiting for a number to fall far enough to trigger a diagnosis, before addressing how a person actually feels, is itself a decision with a cost. Acting earlier, on symptoms and sub-clinical decline, is a defensible alternative to waiting.

BPC-157

Steiner on the state of the human evidence, as extracted from a German transcript: Die Menschenstudien existieren. Sie sind nur lächerlich schlecht gemacht. — the human studies do exist, they are just laughably badly done.

Steiner's own entry point is a shoulder injury from tennis: inflammation, an injection near the joint, and being able to play again. From there he builds the case for BPC-157 — gastric-juice derived, 15 amino acids, anti-inflammatory and tissue-regenerating, said to heal bone fractures, protect nerve tissue and help IBS, with a following he says includes Huberman and Attia. His central evidence: twelve women with bladder pain syndrome, a single 10 mg injection, ten of twelve symptom-free and the other two around 80% improved. He volunteers, unprompted, that the trial had no control group, used only a subjective questionnaire, and was dismissed for it — then generalises, in the fragment above, to a blanket verdict on the whole literature.

CheckMostly true C 3/5 — The pilot trial is real: 12 women who had failed standard treatment, complete resolution in 10 of 12 and ~80% in the other two after a single 10 mg injection, no adverse events. His own caveats — no control group, a subjective questionnaire only, dismissed by others — match the actual, abstract-level design exactly. Dose and route were not independently confirmable beyond it. Source: PubMed PMID 39325560.
CheckUnverifiable C 3/5 — The "375 studies" figure could not be confirmed against any index. What is established: the literature concentrates heavily in one research group, Sikirić and colleagues in Zagreb, who first described the peptide in 1993 and remain its dominant authors; a 2025 review calls the human studies "scarce". Source: Jóźwiak et al., Pharmaceuticals 2025.
CheckMostly true C 3/5 — The thin human record is broadly as he describes: a small retrospective knee study, the uncontrolled bladder-pain pilot above, and a cancelled Phase 1 trial never published. But one trial he leaves out undercuts the blanket dismissal: NCT07437547, a randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled Phase 2 study of BPC-157 for acute hamstring strain (~120 participants) is actively enrolling. That is not badly done — it is unpublished, a different failing, and the omission cuts against his own case. Source: ClinicalTrials.gov NCT07437547.
CheckMostly true C 3/5 — The gastric-juice origin and 15-amino-acid sequence are correct and well established. The bone-healing, neuroprotective and bowel effects exist only at rodent and in-vitro level — bone-defect, brain/spinal-cord injury and colitis models — with no human randomised-trial evidence for any of the three. Source: Jóźwiak et al. 2025.
CheckMostly true C 3/5 — BPC-157 no longer appears on the FDA's Category 2 bulk drug substances list; it now sits under "nominated but withdrawn", citing immunogenicity concerns. The exact 15 April 2026 removal date he gives could not be confirmed against a primary FDA document. Either way it has no FDA marketing approval and no German authorisation. Source: FDA bulk drug substances list.
Other viewsDr. Andreia Mombach, a biomedical scientist whose affiliation is not stated in the source material (flagged): peptides marketed as "research use only" are not approved for human use; the practical risk is wrong dosages, wrong molecules, contamination and non-sterile vials — a supply-chain problem, not a pharmacology one. A companion piece on this Research tab, "A Physician Makes the Case for Peptides — His Argument, Walked Through" (23 August 2026), covers BPC-157 from a physician's argument; see the Sources section below for what this piece adds.

Two things are true about the same ten minutes of tape: his caveats on the bladder study are exactly right, and his verdict on the wider literature is harsher than the record supports — an error that runs against his own commercial interest rather than for it.

TB-500 / thymosin β4

TB-500 is the synthetic form of thymosin β4, a peptide first isolated from the thymus. Steiner describes it driving blood-vessel growth through two separate signalling pathways, reducing cell death, and interacting with hair follicles — useful, he suggests, after a hair transplant. He typically combines it with BPC-157, and concedes the data is mostly animal studies.

CheckMostly true Unverifiable C 3/5 — Angiogenesis through multiple pathways and anti-apoptotic action are supported preclinically, including one pathway protecting endothelial progenitor cells — none confirmed in humans (mostly true, as mechanism). For the hair-follicle claim, no peer-reviewed human study was found, only vendor content (unverifiable). RegeneRx, which holds the active clinical thymosin β4 programme, has moved it into Phase 3 eye-drop trials — the clinical effort went to ophthalmology, not injury repair or hair. Sources: Philp et al., Angiogenesis 2007; RegeneRx.
Other viewsNo named critic addressed TB-500 specifically. Mombach's caution about unapproved, research-grade peptides applies here too; TB-500's separate legal exposure under German anti-doping law is covered in the law card below.

The mechanism is not invented; the human evidence and the hair-transplant application are two different kinds of missing, and he does not distinguish them.

GHK-Cu

Steiner on what GHK-Cu is, as extracted from a German transcript: he calls it a Genom Resetter, and says Genetischer Code wird resetted — a genome resetter, the genetic code is reset.
And on where he ranks it, in a separate remark as extracted: eins der Superpeptide überhaupt — one of the super-peptides, full stop.

GHK-Cu gets the most sustained airtime of any peptide in the conversation. He describes it resetting genetic expression, acting on the immune system, raising collagen synthesis, and working as a mild myostatin inhibitor via the proteoglycan decorin — illustrated with myostatin-deficient cattle. He cites topical studies showing reduced wrinkles over twelve weeks, and calls a cell-level COPD result a breakthrough. Personally, he says injecting it turns the skin neon blue and can burn, with a brief mild rush after, and notes the US cosmetics industry has made it a bestseller.

Check — the central correction of the pieceMisleading C 2/5 — The underlying work concerns gene expression — which genes are turned up or down — not the DNA sequence. The companion COPD paper is a gene-signature and connectivity-mapping analysis, not a rewrite of anyone's genome. Calling that a genetic-code reset conflates expression modulation with genomic alteration; a lay listener hears the second. A real finding, overstated, not invented. The primary text could not be opened directly (publisher-blocked), capping the score. Sources: Pickart 2014, BioMed Research International; Campbell et al. 2012.
Check — the cleanest negative result in the runUnverifiable C 2/5 — No published study links GHK-Cu to decorin induction, or GHK-Cu directly to myostatin inhibition. Decorin's own ability to bind and modulate myostatin is real — but that is a decorin finding, not a GHK-Cu finding, and the first link of his chain is missing entirely. No human evidence GHK-Cu increases muscle mass. Source: decorin–myostatin literature exists independently, e.g. PMID 16380093; no GHK-Cu–decorin paper located.
CheckMostly true C 2/5 — A long-standing cosmetic literature line supports topical GHK-Cu reducing the appearance of wrinkles, tied to lead researcher Pickart's own commercial affiliations (ProCyte, Skin Biology). The funding of the specific trials cited was not re-confirmed this run, so the conflict is noted, not asserted. Source: not independently re-verified this run.
CheckMisleading C 3/5 — The COPD result is a 127-gene expression signature from lung tissue of transplant patients, cross-referenced against the Connectivity Map, then tested in vitro on cultured lung cells, where GHK reversed the pathological pattern. The authors themselves called it preliminary, with animal work "now underway". Fourteen years later, no human clinical outcome in COPD was found — a cell-culture result, not the breakthrough a listener would take it to mean. Source: Campbell et al. 2012.
Other viewsNo named critic addressed GHK-Cu specifically. The closest applicable voice remains Mombach's general point about unapproved peptides marketed as research products; here, the underlying COPD and cosmetic papers largely serve as their own check.

c7 is the shape the whole run takes in miniature: a real finding, stretched by the word chosen to describe it.

Thymosin α1

Thymosin α1 is his entry point into peptides altogether. He describes a post-COVID immune deficiency no doctor could treat, and finding a drug whose name reaches the transcript garbled as something like SXin — almost certainly Zadaxin, thymalfasin's brand name — developed for severe COVID and other immune deficiencies including DiGeorge syndrome and hepatitis. He presents it as US-approved as an orphan drug, for conditions with an incidence he gives, garbled, as between 200 and 200,000 cases a year. From there he improved steadily; he now recommends it in his coaching, with disclosure — while conceding, unprompted, that his recovery could have been coincidence.

CheckWrong C 3/5 — Thymalfasin is not FDA-approved for any indication. It holds orphan drug designations — designation is not approval — and is approved as a medicine in some other countries, not the US or the EU. A completed COVID-19 trial led to no approval or emergency authorisation. The US orphan threshold is a prevalence ceiling — fewer than 200,000 people in the US — not an annual-incidence figure, and there is no 200-person floor. Sources: FDA orphan drug programme; NCT04487444.

The same angiogenesis mechanism that makes BPC-157, TB-500 and GHK-Cu attractive for healing is what worried Eric Demuth enough to ask, unprompted, whether vessel-growth peptides could also feed a tumour. Steiner's answer cites an Instagram account, "Scientific Snitch", arguing the risk is if anything reduced, then falls back on his many long-term clients not visibly all getting cancer.

Steiner, asked by the host about the tumour risk, as extracted from a German transcript: wir wissen es nicht — we do not know. It is a phrase he returns to on the long-term question, in one instance extending it to whether you might grow a third eye in four years.
Check — the sourcing is the findingOpen C 2/5 — Preclinically mixed for BPC-157: it promotes angiogenesis broadly, yet one in-vitro study found it inhibits growth and VEGF signalling in a human melanoma line. No human cancer-outcome data exist for any of these peptides; for thymosin β4 no tumour studies at all were located. An Instagram account is evidence in neither direction — but his concession that nobody knows is the accurate half. Sources: melanoma in-vitro study — ResearchGate; commentary — MDPI.
Other viewsPeter Attia and Andrew Huberman, both named in the episode as figures he aligns with, in fact gate hormone and peptide use on measured deficiency and symptoms, not on early or prophylactic use with normal labs — the strongest form of his own side, and where it parts from him, covered fully below.

His regulatory claim is his most wrong; his own caveat, that his recovery could be coincidence, is among his most honest — and the tumour answer shows both traits in the same breath.

GLP-1 agonists

Semaglutide and tirzepatide, in his framing, belong at the end of the chain — for people who already have diet and training right and are still failing from extreme emotional or appetite problems, not as a first resort. He attributes "Ozempic face", the gaunt look of rapid weight loss, to inadequate protein rather than the drug, and describes a low-dose protocol with protein shakes to protect muscle. Low-dose use, he adds, also stabilises blood sugar and helps focus.

CheckMisleading Unverifiable C 2/5 — Clinical sources attribute the facial volume loss primarily to rapid drug-induced fat/tissue loss, poor protein an aggravating, not causal, factor — his framing inverts it. For low-dose focus or blood-sugar claims in non-diabetics, no controlled human trial was found; only commercial microdosing programmes and an unreported trial. Source: Harvard Health.
Other viewsEric Demuth's own account, offered on air, cuts directly against the guest's framing: he tried semaglutide at the lowest available dose and found it made him tired and nauseous — a host's data point contradicting a guest's claim, volunteered rather than solicited.

The one piece of contrary evidence in this section came from the co-host's own body, not from a study — and he offered it himself.

The question nobody asked: anti-doping

In three hours covering BPC-157, TB-500 and thymosin α1 by name and by dose, competitive-sport eligibility never comes up. It is worth stating plainly, because a listener training for anything sanctioned would need to know it and would not learn it here.

CheckTrue C 5/5 — Under the WADA Prohibited List 2026, in force since 1 January 2026: BPC-157 is named under S0, non-approved substances — anything not currently approved by a government health authority for human therapeutic use. TB-500 and thymosin β4 fall under S2.3, growth factors and modulators affecting protein synthesis/breakdown and vessel formation in muscle, tendon and ligament. Both are prohibited at all times, in and out of competition. GHK-Cu and thymosin α1 are not named. USADA maintains a standing warning page on BPC-157 specifically. Sources: NADA translation of the 2026 list; USADA.
Other viewsNo individual voice raised this point; the finding is institutional — WADA sets the list, USADA publishes the warning — and its absence from a three-hour conversation about these exact substances is the notable fact.

This is the one claim in the run that nobody in the room made, checked against a document nobody in the room opened.

Testosterone replacement

Steiner on starting testosterone therapy, as extracted from a German transcript: mein Leben hat sich seitdem komplett verändert — my life has completely changed since then.

His own story is the strongest thing in the episode. Despite training seriously, he could not build muscle; he measured 0.6–0.8 ng/ml against a lab range he gives as 8–12; tried vitamin D, zinc and clomifene, the last causing severe headaches he abandoned it over; developed severe erectile dysfunction and took up to 80 mg tadalafil with no effect beyond nasal congestion. He started TRT, and describes the change in the fragment above. He has since become a father twice, offered against the fertility fear he goes on to dismiss. Demuth answered with his own family: his grandfather went on testosterone and improved, then stopped at his grandmother's request — a data point the host volunteered that cuts both ways at once.

CheckWrong figure C 4/5 — On the range, not his own case. A German lab gives total testosterone for men 20–49 as 2.49–8.36 ng/ml (≥50: 1.93–7.40); the harmonised US range converts to roughly 2.64–9.16 ng/ml. "8–12" is not a reference range on either scale — closer to the top of one, or a unit mix-up. The clinical substance survives intact: 0.6–0.8 ng/ml is about a quarter of the lower limit on any scale, severe hypogonadism regardless. Sources: Endokrinologikum reference values; Endocrine Society 2017.

He gives typical replacement doses of 90–150 mg a week ester weight, middle dose around 125 mg (80–90 mg active), against natural production of roughly 50 mg a week.

CheckMisleading C 3/5 — The endogenous figure is broadly fine: production runs about 3–10 mg a day, i.e. 21–70 mg a week. But guideline replacement is typically around 75–100 mg a week; his 90–150 mg range with a 125 mg middle sits at or above that band, toward what the literature calls supraphysiological — presented here as ordinary replacement. Sources: Endotext, Androgen Physiology; Endocrine Society guideline.

On fertility, he says testosterone reduces sperm count but is not per se infertility, and states flatly, in German:

Steiner on testosterone and fertility, as extracted from a German transcript: es ist kein Verhütungsmittel — it is not a contraceptive.

He recommends a baseline sperm analysis first.

Check — the most consequential error in the hormone halfWrong C 4/5 — Exogenous testosterone suppresses LH and FSH and with them spermatogenesis. WHO male-contraceptive trials used testosterone precisely because it reliably produces azoospermia or severe oligozoospermia in most men. Recovery after stopping takes a median of roughly 3–12 months, sometimes longer than 24. His narrower point — reduced sperm count is not per se infertility — is defensible, and the baseline-spermiogram advice reasonable. The flat denial that it functions as a contraceptive is not, and of everything checked here it is the claim most likely to harm a listener who wants children. Sources: World J Mens Health; Fertil Steril recovery data.

On screening he names haematocrit, a history of depression, and blood lipids at excessive doses, and says high blood pressure and heart disease need case-by-case assessment, not a blanket bar. His hedges run throughout: checked case by case, several doctor conversations, working with private physicians, and on depression history — most feel better, but which came first is unclear. He admits his own past use of higher doses, and, describing another physician's testosterone regimen, calls it, in German:

Steiner on a testosterone regimen he was shown, as extracted from a German transcript: das ist Körperverletzung — that is bodily harm.
CheckMisleading by omission C 4/5 — The individualised handling of uncomplicated hypertension and stable heart disease is broadly right. But guideline absolute contraindications he never mentions include active or suspected prostate or breast cancer, severe untreated sleep apnoea, elevated PSA, and a recent heart attack, stroke or revascularisation. Mandatory PSA monitoring is also absent from his list. Sources: EAU Male Hypogonadism guideline; VA clinical recommendations, Jan 2026.
CheckMostly true C 5/5 — But it undersells what remains. TRAVERSE, ~5,246 men with cardiovascular risk or disease, found testosterone non-inferior to placebo for major cardiac events, and on that basis the FDA removed the class-wide boxed cardiovascular warning, 28 February 2025. The same trial found more atrial fibrillation, pulmonary embolism, venous thromboembolism and acute kidney injury, and the FDA simultaneously added a new blood-pressure warning — not the clean all-clear his framing implies. Source: FDA class-wide labelling change.

On hair loss, he attributes baldness to DHT, not testosterone, dependent on genetic 5-alpha-reductase activity, and says low-dose dutasteride can mitigate it. He reports minimal loss of his own and an early hair treatment. He also describes clomifene, tried before starting testosterone, causing severe headaches.

CheckTrue C 4/5 — DHT, not testosterone, driving male pattern baldness through genetically variable 5-alpha-reductase activity is well established. Source: Cleveland Clinic.
CheckMostly true C 3/5 — With a caveat he does not give. Dutasteride is approved only for benign prostatic hyperplasia; use for hair loss is off-label in the US and EU, where only finasteride 1 mg is approved for androgenetic alopecia. Documented effects include erectile dysfunction, reduced libido, ejaculation disorders and gynaecomastia, with persistent-symptom reports after stopping. He offers no caveat. Source: Dutasteride prescribing information.
CheckMostly true C 3/5 — Clomifene is licensed for ovulation induction in women; use in men is off-label. Meta-analyses show it raises endogenous testosterone via LH and FSH, with less suppression of spermatogenesis than exogenous testosterone. Headache is a recognised adverse effect. Source: Andrology meta-analysis.
Other viewsProf. Matthias M. Weber (Universitätsmedizin Mainz, DGE spokesman) argues the fall in hormone levels is a consequence of ageing, not its cause — a challenge to the premise under the whole group. Prof. Sabine Kliesch (andrology director, Universitätsklinikum Münster), on a 118% rise in prescriptions to men 20–29, is reported as saying a man with no deficiency does not benefit from treating one. Prof. Frank König (urologist and andrologist, Berlin) routinely refuses testosterone requests from young men seeking muscle, citing fertility risk. Deutsches Ärzteblatt, citing DGE data, reports only 3–5% of men 60–79 have levels low enough to indicate therapy. Against those critics, Peter Attia and Andrew Huberman — the two authorities he invokes — gate therapy on symptomatic deficiency confirmed by labs, and neither endorses starting before decline with normal levels.

His own case is the most persuasive thing in the piece; his flattest denial, on fertility, is the one most capable of causing harm to someone who believes it.

hCG and hMG

If fertility is a priority, he says, hCG and hMG can maintain testicular function during testosterone therapy — pure biohacking, in his words, mostly practised in the US, rarely in Germany — and every case so far, he claims, was better than the starting point, unquantified.

CheckMisleading C 3/5 — Low-dose hCG has retrospective, observational support for preventing azoospermia during testosterone therapy, not randomised evidence. hCG is licensed in the EU for hypogonadotropic hypogonadism and cryptorchidism, hMG for infertility — using either alongside exogenous testosterone is off-label. Nothing published supports outcomes better than baseline in every case; response varies. Sources: Fertil Steril 2010; review.
Other viewsKliesch's and König's broader position — testosterone reserved for documented deficiency, not requested for effect — applies equally to fertility-preservation add-ons: an off-label combination is still off-label regardless of the underlying therapy.

"Better than before" without a number is a coaching outcome, not a clinical one — the one claim here nobody outside his practice can check.

The adjunct drugs, compact

Status, licence and documented risk — one tight card per substance, no protocol.

TadalafilMostly true C 3/5 — He took 80 mg with no effect beyond nasal congestion. The maximum approved dose is 20 mg as needed or 5 mg daily; 80 mg is four times the labelled maximum. Efficacy plateaus near 20 mg while adverse-effect rates rise, so no extra benefit is plausible — but exceeding the label adds risk, particularly hypotension with nitrates, rare priapism and vision or hearing events. Nasal congestion is a documented label effect. Source: FDA label.
TelmisartanMisleading C 3/5 — He says it has advantages worth having anyway alongside TRT. Licensed for hypertension and cardiovascular risk reduction in high-risk patients intolerant of ACE inhibitors. Its partial PPAR-gamma activity appears in vitro and small mechanistic trials — not outcomes evidence, not a licensed indication. No credible evidence supports taking an antihypertensive when not hypertensive; the effect in normotensive people is minimal while hypotension risk is real. Source: Telmisartan PPARγ studies.
TrazodoneMisleading C 3/5 — Low-dose, for sleep, attributed to Peter Attia; Christian Wolf says he uses it too. Licensed as an antidepressant; low-dose sleep use is off-label, resting on old, small, low-quality trials. The AASM's 2017 guideline recommends against its routine use for insomnia, citing insufficient evidence. Risks: priapism, orthostatic hypotension, next-day sedation. Credit due: Steiner explicitly said he does not recommend it and was only describing his own use — the Attia attribution is plausible but unconfirmed. Source: AASM 2017 guideline.
Methylene blue — the most safety-relevant item in the pieceWrong C 4/5 — He describes taking it after intense cognitive work to restore serotonin. Methylene blue is a potent, reversible monoamine oxidase A inhibitor; its only FDA-approved indication is methaemoglobinaemia. Combined with serotonergic drugs — SSRIs, SNRIs, MAOIs, tricyclics, tramadol — it carries a documented serotonin syndrome risk serious enough for a formal FDA safety communication. "Restores serotonin" describes the mechanism behind that toxicity as the benefit, with no dose given and no mention of the interaction. Sources: MAO-A inhibition; FDA warning.
CaffeineWrong figure C 4/5 — He gives caffeine's half-life as ~12 hours rather than 6, and says a double espresso affects sleep up to 13 hours before bed. The mean half-life is about 5–6 hours (range ~1.5–9.5), driven mainly by CYP1A2 genotype, smoking, pregnancy and oral contraceptives. A 2025 randomised crossover trial testing caffeine 4, 8 and 12 hours before bed found 100 mg — about one double espresso — caused no significant sleep disruption at any interval; only 400 mg did, as did the classic 2013 study he may be citing. Source: Sleep 2025 RCT.
Eric Demuth, on caffeineOpen C 4/5 — Countering the guest, he can drink a Monster four hours before training without losing sleep. A personal report, not checkable — but closer to the 2025 trial's findings than the guest's 13-hour claim. Source: as c28.
PCSK9 and SGLT-2 inhibitorsOpen C 2/5 — Named in passing as longevity tools. PCSK9 inhibitors are licensed for hypercholesterolaemia and cardiovascular risk reduction; SGLT-2 inhibitors for type 2 diabetes, heart failure and chronic kidney disease. Neither is approved for metabolically healthy people; no randomised evidence of benefit or safety exists in low-risk individuals. Source: drug labelling; StatPearls PCSK9 review.
Other viewsNo named critic addressed these seven substances individually; the documented risk in each card comes from the drug's own label or clinical guideline rather than a named opponent. The FDA's own serotonin-syndrome warning is the operative other view on methylene blue; the AASM guideline plays the same role for trazodone.

Every item here is a licensed drug used off-label for a purpose its label does not cover; that alone is not damning, but none carries the caveat a prescriber would be required to give.

The law: Germany, Austria, Switzerland

None of this is a claim Steiner made. It is the context the episode never supplies, and the part a German reader most needs.

Austria, 27 May 2026. BASG, the Austrian medicines agency, classified many "peptides" sold online as medicinal products without marketing authorisation, warning buyers of legal consequences. Source: BASG notice. C 5/5.

A "research use only" label carries no legal weight in Germany: function determines medicinal-product status under the Arzneimittelgesetz, and a vendor's stated purpose is irrelevant. BPC-157 has no German or EU authorisation, and personal import runs against §73 AMG (C 3/5, single legal-practice source). TB-500 is listed under the Anti-Doping-Gesetz as a growth factor, making possession above a trivial quantity criminal rather than administrative (C 3/5, single source, flagged).

Pharmacy compounding is lawful as a mechanism but unavailable in practice: German pharmacies may compound only from certified pharmaceutical-grade starting materials, which do not exist for most of these peptides. Thymosin α1 is unauthorised anywhere in the EU; the only lawful route is a §73(3) AMG single-patient import through a licensed pharmacy on an individual prescription — doctor-initiated, never an online order.

BfArM, 2025. A BfArM-initiated study found a marked rise in testosterone prescriptions billed to statutory health insurance, possibly outside the approved indication. Source: BfArM risk information. C 5/5.

Swissmedic is reported to have stopped 46 suspect peptide shipments — a title-level source only, flagged and not re-confirmed here.

Stepping back

The error direction in this run. Of the thirty claims: 2 True, 10 Mostly true, 8 Misleading, 5 Wrong or Wrong figure, 2 Unverifiable, 3 Open. Twelve of the thirteen misleading, wrong and wrong-figure verdicts lean the same way — toward the substances appearing better supported, safer or more legally settled than the record shows. The thirteenth runs the other way: on caffeine (c28) he overstates a substance's downside rather than understating it. One further item, the reference range in c15, fits the pattern only indirectly — an inflated ceiling makes his own deficiency look more dramatic — and is counted here on that reading rather than a self-evident one. Worth saying plainly rather than hiding in a table. Two things complicate it: his hedging is real and repeatedly cuts against his own commercial interest — he names the bladder study's missing control group himself, concedes nobody knows on the cancer question, and declines to recommend trazodone — and his single most dismissive error, calling the human BPC-157 literature badly done, understates a well-designed trial that might vindicate his own position. One run on one episode. A handful of runs is not a base rate.
