SPACEXConfirmed24 Jul 2026, reported 24 – 25 Jul

TechCrunch resolves last week's disputed booster outcome: Starship Flight 13's Super Heavy did fail its landing burn, again

Last week's edition flagged sharply conflicting sourcing on Super Heavy's Gulf of Mexico landing burn — one account describing only 5 of 13 engines firing and a hard impact, another calling the splashdown "flawless." TechCrunch's own reporting, filed the night of the launch, resolves that discrepancy in favour of the harsher account: "the booster made it farther into its planned flight but wasn't able to properly fire up all of the engines required for its simulated landing burn," and it "exploded after a faster-than-expected impact with the water." This is the second consecutive failure of a Super Heavy V3 booster, after May's flight, where the booster failed during stage separation rather than landing.

The upper stage performed better than in May: Starship's six Raptor engines had no issues this time (versus losing one engine on the May flight), and all 20 third-generation Starlink V3 satellites deployed cleanly before their planned atmospheric burn-up roughly 20 minutes later — Starship still cannot reach stable orbit. SPCX fell a further 2% in after-hours trading following the booster failure before paring some of the loss.

Context & background. Last week's edition explicitly declined to pick a side between the two conflicting booster-outcome accounts, flagging one source's SPCX price figures (a claimed 74% drawdown, $1 trillion in erased value) as internally inconsistent with every other outlet's much smaller ~50%-off-peak figure, and weighting the more internally consistent account — successful ship splashdown, troubled booster — as more credible without treating it as confirmed. TechCrunch's own reporting, from a named reporter with direct sourcing on SpaceX and Tesla coverage, now removes that ambiguity: the booster failure is a stated fact, not a disputed claim between anonymous accounts.

The details. The mechanism matters: this was a booster engine-ignition failure during a simulated landing burn, the same general failure mode — insufficient engines relighting for a controlled descent — that has now occurred on both flights of the V3-generation Super Heavy booster, albeit at different stages (May's failure was during stage separation; July's was during the landing attempt itself). SpaceX had replaced six engines on Booster 20 specifically to fix the ignition problem that aborted the first Flight 13 attempt on 16 July — meaning this week's landing-burn failure is a different failure mode from the one SpaceX explicitly addressed, not a recurrence of the same fixed problem. That distinction is significant for assessing SpaceX's diagnostic accuracy: the company correctly fixed the problem it identified, but a related problem it had not identified then surfaced in a different phase of flight.

Context. This confirms, rather than revises, the reading this briefing offered last week: the flight's one load-bearing objective for NASA's Artemis timeline — the in-space Raptor relight — succeeded cleanly and without dispute, keeping that dependency chain alive. But Starship's second consecutive Super Heavy landing-adjacent failure on the V3 vehicle is now a documented pattern, not a one-off, and it directly bears on the economic premise behind Starship's entire Starlink V3 capacity step-change: rapid booster reusability. A booster that has failed its return in two straight flights, for two different reasons, is a harder engineering problem than "fix the engines that failed last time," and the market's sub-2%-after-hours reaction suggests investors had already substantially priced in booster risk after May's failure and this week's pre-launch abort.

Watch-Items. (1) SpaceX's own technical post-flight report, if one is published, and whether it identifies a root cause distinct from May's separation failure. (2) Whether Flight 14 attempts another landing burn or reverts to an expendable-booster profile while the issue is diagnosed. (3) Any NASA statement on how this affects Artemis schedule confidence, given the successful relight. (4) Timeline to Flight 14, and whether it slips as Flight 13 did. (5) Whether SPCX's after-hours dip becomes a larger move once a full trading session prices in the confirmed failure.

Context & background. Last week's edition closed its SPCX coverage noting that the entire week's price action predated the evening Starship launch, and that Monday, 27 July would be "the first real reaction window." That window has now passed, and the stock did not rally toward the $150 level retail positioning was reportedly betting on — instead it continued the multi-week downward drift this briefing has tracked since the IPO, with the confirmed booster failure (see card above) adding a fresh negative data point rather than reversing the trend.

The details. The lock-up mechanics are the more structurally important story than any single week's price move: SpaceX's IPO in June left only roughly 4–5% of total shares in the public float, which this briefing has repeatedly cited as the reason SPCX's quote data and volatility behave differently from a normal large-cap listing. The 6 August unlock of ~911.5 million shares — worth an estimated $116 billion at recent prices — is, per multiple sources, the largest single lock-up expiration in market history, and it arrives two days after SpaceX's first public earnings report, meaning the company's first quarterly disclosure and its first genuine test of price discovery under a much larger float land almost simultaneously. The price-contingent second tranche (455.8 million shares, requiring ≥$175.50 for 5 of 10 sessions) is, at current levels around $112, a roughly 55% rally away from triggering — a gap wide enough that this briefing treats that tranche as unlikely to unlock on the current trajectory, though a single strong earnings surprise could change that quickly given the stock's demonstrated volatility.

Context. The Black-versus-Gerber split captures the genuine uncertainty here better than either position alone: a five-fold increase in tradable float, landing right after a mixed earnings report and a second consecutive booster failure, is exactly the kind of event that could produce either a valuation reset toward fundamentals (Black's framing) or a buying opportunity if the sell-off overshoots (Gerber's framing) — and this briefing has no basis yet to prefer one over the other. What is clear is that 4–6 August, not this week, is the actual test of SPCX's post-IPO price discovery, and everything observed in this window is pre-positioning ahead of it.

edit_noteEditor's view

A quiet four days that mostly resolved last week's open questions rather than raising new ones

1. Tesla found a floor, not a recovery — and that gap to consensus is the story. Four flat sessions in a narrow $308–313 band, against a $404.95 analyst consensus, is neither the continued crash nor the rebound either side of this briefing's running Tesla debate was hoping for. Cathie Wood buying twice in four days is a genuine signal of conviction, but it is a bet on a gap closing, not evidence that it already has.

2. The Starship booster story got worse, not better, once the sourcing resolved. Last week this briefing declined to pick a side between a "5 of 13 engines" account and a "flawless" one. TechCrunch's own reporting settled it: the booster failed its landing burn for the second straight V3 flight, a different failure mode than the one SpaceX explicitly fixed after the 16 July abort. The in-space relight's success is real and matters for Artemis — but Starship's path to rapid reusability is now a documented two-flight pattern of booster failure, not a one-off.

3. Grok 4.6's timeline slip is the fastest this briefing has caught a Musk-stated date move. "About two weeks," stated 24 July, had become "late August to mid-September" within three days of coverage — before the original deadline was even close to arriving. That speed matters more than the size of the slip: it suggests the "two weeks" framing was never a firm commitment in the first place, which has implications for how much weight this briefing should give future Musk-stated ship dates.

4. SPCX's real test hasn't happened yet. Everything observed in this window — the continued drift to $112–113, the Black-versus-Gerber valuation debate — is positioning ahead of 4–6 August, when SpaceX's first public earnings and the largest lock-up expiration in market history land two days apart. This briefing has no basis yet to call that outcome either way.

5. Silence across DOGE, legal, Neuralink, X and Boring is itself informative. Five separate threads generated zero new reporting in four days — not because this briefing stopped looking, but because the portfolio's political, legal and quieter-venture stories are genuinely between events right now. That is worth stating plainly rather than manufacturing cards to fill the section.

Four days that mostly answered last week's questions rather than posing new ones: Tesla stalled instead of recovering, Starship's booster problem got confirmed instead of resolved in SpaceX's favour, and Grok 4.6's release date moved before it was even due. The next edition, assuming a fuller week, should have SpaceX's actual earnings and lock-up event to judge, and possibly a first look at whether Grok 4.6's new window holds any better than its first one did.
