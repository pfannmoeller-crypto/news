DISASTER2016Oct 6–9, 2016

Hurricane Matthew passes just off Florida, then comes ashore in South Carolina after killing hundreds in Haiti

Hurricane Matthew hit Haiti on 4 October as a Category 4 storm, the second-highest step on the five-step Saffir-Simpson wind scale. On 7 October its centre stayed about 55 kilometres off Florida's east coast, and on 8 October it came ashore at a coastal town in South Carolina as a Category 1 storm, the lowest step.

The US government's National Hurricane Center counts 546 deaths in Haiti and 34 in the United States that the storm itself caused, with about $10 billion of damage in the US.

Context & background.The storm crossed Haiti on 4 October and then struck Cuba around midnight into 5 October, still at Category 4 strength. It then ran along the US east coast: it stayed offshore from Florida on 7 October and reached South Carolina on 8 October, where it weakened to a Category 1 storm with winds of about 140 kilometres per hour.

The details.The National Hurricane Center's final report counts only deaths that the storm itself caused, such as wind, storm surge and flooding, and not later indirect ones. By that count the toll was 546 in Haiti, 34 in the United States, 4 in the Dominican Republic and 1 in St Vincent and the Grenadines, a Caribbean island state: 585 in all. The US damage figure of about $10 billion carries a margin of $2 billion either way. Early US news reports on 7 October had said that more than 800 people had died in Haiti.

Why it still matters.Death tolls from the first days of a disaster are often revised. Here early reports of more than 800 dead in Haiti became a final count of 546 deaths caused directly by the storm, a reminder to read the first figure as provisional.

POLITICS2016Oct 7, 2016

US intelligence says Russia's government directed hacks of Democratic Party emails before the 2016 election

On 7 October 2016, a month before the US presidential election between Donald Trump and Hillary Clinton, the Department of Homeland Security (the US domestic-security ministry) and the Office of the Director of National Intelligence (the body that coordinates the US spy agencies) issued a joint statement. It said US intelligence was confident that “the Russian Government directed the recent compromises of e-mails from US persons and institutions, including from US political organizations.”

The statement said the stolen emails were appearing on websites such as DCLeaks.com and WikiLeaks, and that the leaks were “intended to interfere with the US election process.” It also said it would be extremely difficult for anyone to change actual vote counts by hacking.

Context & background.Until then, blaming Russia had rested on unnamed officials. Earlier in 2016, news reports said US agencies had “high confidence” that Russia was behind the hacking of the Democratic National Committee, the Democratic Party's national organisation, but no agency had said so in its own name. The October statement made it the official position of the US government and described the leaks as an attempt to interfere with the election, not as ordinary spying.

The details.The statement names the places where stolen material had appeared: DCLeaks.com and WikiLeaks, two websites that publish leaked documents, and a hacker persona calling itself Guccifer 2.0, which US officials tied to Russia. In words quoted in news reports, it also said that only Russia's most senior officials could have authorized such activity. On election security, the Department of Homeland Security offered to check state election systems for weak points and to advise on protecting voter registration databases. The same evening two other stories broke. At 22:05 German time the Washington Post published a 2005 recording, later known as the Access Hollywood tape, in which Trump said crude things about women. At 22:32 WikiLeaks began publishing thousands of emails from the account of John Podesta, chairman of Clinton's campaign. The recording dominated the news coverage, and the Russia statement received less attention.

Why it still matters.The statement put the accusation on the record as the US government's own finding. On 29 December 2016 the administration of President Barack Obama followed it with sanctions on Russia and a technical account of the hacking.

BUSINESS2016Oct 10–11, 2016

Samsung ends production of its top smartphone, the Galaxy Note 7, after replacement phones also catch fire

On 11 October 2016 (10 October in the US), Samsung told South Korea's government it would stop making the Galaxy Note 7, its flagship smartphone, a day after halting sales and exchanges. The phones' batteries overheated, and a first recall of about 2.5 million phones in early September had not solved the problem, because the replacement phones also caught fire.

Samsung's share price fell 8 percent that day, its biggest one-day fall since October 2008, which cost the company about $18 to $20 billion in stock-market value.

Context & background.The Note 7 was Samsung's top smartphone, with a large screen. After the first recall in early September, Samsung supplied replacement phones. When these also caught fire, including one on a flight in the US, Samsung stopped sales and exchanges on 10 October and ended production the next day.

The details.Samsung's statement read: “For the benefit of consumers' safety, we stopped sales and exchanges of the Galaxy Note7 and have consequently decided to stop production.” Owners were offered a full refund, or a refund towards another Samsung phone plus a $25 gift certificate or other add-on. A South Korean government official said the cause was harder to find this time because the fires had no single pattern. Analysts estimated that the total cost to Samsung could reach $17 billion.

Why it still matters.The Note 7 came to be treated as a safety hazard at airports and on flights around the world. Analysts warned of lasting damage to Samsung's brand and to its relationships with the mobile providers that sell its phones.

CONFLICT2001Oct 7, 2001

The United States begins bombing Afghanistan, opening a war that lasts twenty years

On 7 October 2001, 26 days after the 11 September attacks on New York and Washington, President George W. Bush announced in a televised address that “the United States military has begun strikes against al Qaeda terrorist training camps and military installations of the Taliban regime in Afghanistan.” Al Qaeda is the terror network led by Osama bin Laden that carried out the attacks; the Taliban were the Islamist group ruling most of Afghanistan. Britain joined the action, and Canada, Australia, Germany and France pledged forces.

Bush told the Taliban to close the training camps, hand over al Qaeda's leaders and release all foreign nationals held in the country.

Context & background.The US called the campaign Operation Enduring Freedom. US intelligence had concluded that the 11 September terrorists had trained in al Qaeda's camps in Afghanistan, and the aim was to dismantle that network and overthrow the Taliban government that sheltered it.

The details.Alongside the military announcement, Bush made a humanitarian promise: “As we strike military targets, we'll also drop food, medicine and supplies to the starving and suffering men and women and children of Afghanistan.” His three demands to the Taliban were to close the terrorist training camps, to hand over the leaders of the al Qaeda network, and to return all foreign nationals, American citizens included, unjustly detained in Afghanistan.

Why it still matters.The war lasted about twenty years and ended when the last US troops left on 30 August 2021, as the Taliban returned to power. About 2,300 US soldiers died, along with about 1,100 allied troops, roughly 70,000 Afghan soldiers and police and at least 46,000 Afghan civilians. The war cost the US about $2.3 trillion.

SPORT2001Oct 5–7, 2001

Barry Bonds hits his 71st, 72nd and 73rd home runs of the season, a baseball record

In baseball, a home run is a hit that clears the outfield fence and scores at least one run; a player who hits 30 in a season is a star. On 5 October 2001 Barry Bonds of the San Francisco Giants hit his 71st and 72nd of the season, passing the previous record of 70, set by Mark McGwire in 1998. The Giants lost that game 10–11 to their rivals, the Los Angeles Dodgers, which ended their hopes of reaching the play-offs, the post-season competition.

Two days later, in the season's last game, Bonds hit number 73, in a 2–1 Giants win over the Dodgers.

Context & background.The 7 October game was a postponed one: it had been scheduled for 16 September, but American baseball paused after the 11 September attacks. So the record ended in a make-up game, played after the Giants had already missed the play-offs.

The details.Bonds's 73rd came on the last day of the regular season. The Giants finished the year with 90 wins and 72 defeats, which was not enough to reach the play-offs.

Why it still matters.The new mark of 73 was three above McGwire's 70, set only three seasons earlier. The postponed final game is also a small reminder of how the 11 September attacks disrupted American life.

DISASTER2001Oct 8, 2001

Fog and a broken ground radar turn a wrong turn at Milan's city airport into a collision that kills 118

At about 08:10 local time on 8 October 2001, a Scandinavian Airlines passenger jet taking off from Linate, Milan's city airport, in thick fog hit a small German business jet that had driven onto the runway in use. All 110 people on the airliner died, as did all 4 on the small jet and 4 airport staff on the ground: 118 in all.

Italy's air-accident investigators found that visibility was only 50 to 100 metres, that the airport's radar for tracking planes on the ground was not working, and that the markings on the taxiing routes were worn.

Context & background.The airliner was starting its take-off while the small jet, on its way to another part of the airport, took a wrong turn and crossed onto the runway in use. The investigators concluded that the small jet drove onto the runway while the airliner was accelerating for take-off.

The details.The investigators named several factors rather than one cause. Besides the fog, there was no working system to track planes on the ground, although a ground radar had been approved in 1995; the markings on the taxiing routes were worn and the route the pilots took was poorly identified; and alarms that warn of a plane on the runway had been switched off. The two aircraft were talking to different controllers, the instruction given to the small jet was ambiguous, and the controllers did not coordinate the two planes' positions. In April 2004 an Italian court sentenced the airport's director and a controller to eight years each and two former senior officials to six and a half years; Italy's top court upheld the convictions in 2008.

Why it still matters.The investigators recommended systems to track planes on the ground, signs that meet international standards, procedures for low visibility, tighter coordination between controllers and a review of runway safety at other European airports. Aviation experts still debate whether criminal trials discourage staff from admitting mistakes.

50 years ago1976Bangkok coup · Cubana bombing · Gang of Four
POLITICAL VIOLENCE1976Oct 6, 1976

Thai police and pro-monarchy militias storm a Bangkok university, and the military seizes power the same day

On 6 October 1976 police and pro-monarchy armed groups attacked students occupying Thammasat University in Bangkok. The students were protesting the return of Thanom Kittikachorn, the military ruler driven out by a student uprising in 1973. The official count was 46 dead and 167 wounded, with more than 3,000 arrested; survivors say about 100 or more were killed.

That evening the armed forces, led by Admiral Sangad Chaloryu, took power from the elected civilian government. On 8 October the king appointed Thanin Kraivichien, a judge known for his hostility to communism, as prime minister. Communist governments had recently come to power in neighbouring Vietnam and Laos, and the military feared the same in Thailand.

Context & background.The trigger came on 5 October, when students staged a mock hanging that re-enacted a recent killing. A newspaper published a photograph claiming that the actor playing the victim resembled Crown Prince Vajiralongkorn, today King of Thailand, and accusations of insulting the monarchy followed; under Thai law, insulting the monarchy (lèse-majesté) is a serious crime. Army-controlled radio stations then called on pro-monarchy groups to gather near the campus: the Red Gaurs and Nawaphon, which were right-wing militias, and the Village Scouts, a state-backed civilian movement.

The details.The attack began around 5:30 in the morning on 6 October and went on until about 11:00 local time. Forces surrounded the university and fired assault rifles, machine guns, pistols and grenade launchers; after an order to shoot at will at about 7:30 to 8:00 they stormed the campus. Some protesters who fled into the Chao Phraya, the river through Bangkok, were shot at from naval vessels, and mobs killed others. Student leaders who tried to negotiate with the civilian prime minister, Seni Pramoj, were arrested instead, and the military then removed him. Eighteen organisers were later charged with rebellion and communist affiliation. Estimates of the dead run from 100 to more than 500.

Why it still matters.The first amnesty law, on 24 December 1976, legalised the coup and shielded those involved; a second, on 16 September 1978, freed the remaining detainees and dropped the charges. At the 40th anniversary in 2016 no state official had been held to account, and Thailand's military-run government detained and deported the Hong Kong democracy activist Joshua Wong shortly before he was due to speak at a commemoration.

TERRORISM1976Oct 6, 1976

A bomb brings down a Cuban airliner off Barbados, killing all 73 aboard

On 6 October 1976 a Cubana de Aviación airliner, Flight 455 of Cuba's national airline, flying from Barbados to Jamaica, was destroyed by two time bombs eleven minutes after taking off from Barbados. It fell into the Caribbean about 8 kilometres west of Bridgetown, the capital, and all 73 people aboard died, including Cuba's national fencing team.

The pilot's last radio call was: “We have an explosion aboard – we are descending immediately!”

Context & background.The attack came four months after the founding, on 11 June 1976, of a coalition of five groups of Cuban exiles who opposed Fidel Castro, Cuba's ruler; the coalition is widely seen as responsible. Declassified documents of the CIA, the US spy agency, show that it had concrete advance intelligence, as early as June 1976, of plans by such groups to bomb a Cuban airliner.

The details.One bomb exploded in the rear toilet and destroyed the control cables, and the other started a fire in the passenger cabin, so the crew could not turn back. The device was a tube of toothpaste hiding plastic explosives with a timer. An FBI report to Henry Kissinger, then US Secretary of State, said that Luis Posada Carriles, a Cuban exile militant and former CIA operative, had attended meetings in Caracas, the capital of Venezuela, where the bombing was planned; a CIA source overheard him saying “We are going to hit a Cuban airliner.” After the attack, one suspect phoned Orlando Bosch, another Cuban exile leader, and said “a bus with 73 dogs went off a cliff and all got killed.” The two Venezuelans who planted the bombs were arrested in Trinidad and Tobago; they had first phoned the office of Posada's security company.

Why it still matters.Venezuelan courts convicted the two bombers in 1985 and sentenced them to twenty years each; they were released in 1993. Neither of the two men named as organisers was convicted in the bombing case: Bosch was acquitted because of problems with the evidence, and Posada escaped from prison before sentencing and reached the United States, where immigration courts blocked his deportation because he risked torture. Memorials stand in Barbados (1998) and Guyana (2012), and Caribbean officials have proposed making 6 October an international day against terrorism.

POLITICS1976Oct 6, 1976

China's leaders arrest Mao's widow and three allies a month after Mao's death

On 6 October 1976, 27 days after Mao Zedong died on 9 September, prime minister Hua Guofeng, together with army marshal Ye Jianying and Wang Dongxing, the head of Mao's guard, moved against Mao's widow Jiang Qing and three other radical Communist Party leaders: Zhang Chunqiao, Yao Wenyuan and Wang Hongwen. The four became known as the Gang of Four.

At about 8:30 that evening Wang Hongwen and Yao Wenyuan were told they would be held for investigation; Zhang was detained soon after, and Jiang at her home. Early on 7 October the Politburo, the Communist Party's ruling committee, named Hua party chairman.

Context & background.The four had been Mao's closest supporters during the Cultural Revolution, his 1966–76 mass campaign of purges and violence. After Mao's death they tried to take control by demanding that major issues be reported to them and by spreading rumours against rivals such as Deng Xiaoping, later China's leader and the architect of its economic reforms. Hua, who had been named prime minister, chose to strike first.

The details.The arrests took place in the leaders' compound in Beijing. Hua started the planning and decided to act “no later than the day after tomorrow.” Wang Dongxing and Ye Jianying, both deputy heads of the army's top command, the Central Military Commission, carried it out. Mao's nephew Mao Yuanxin was arrested that night in north-east China. After the arrests Hua called an emergency Politburo meeting at Ye's home. The party leadership announced the arrests only on 18 October, and nationwide denunciations began on 21 October.

Why it still matters.From 1980 to 1981 a special court tried the four. Jiang Qing and Zhang Chunqiao were sentenced to death, reduced to life imprisonment; Wang Hongwen got life and Yao Wenyuan twenty years. The prosecution recorded about 750,000 people persecuted and 34,375 deaths between 1966 and 1976. Many observers saw the trial as political rather than purely legal. Blaming the four let the party condemn the Cultural Revolution while protecting Mao's reputation.

Editor's view

Three of the nine stories turn on a number or an announcement that was revised after the first day. Early US news reports put the death toll of Hurricane Matthew in Haiti above 800, while the National Hurricane Center's final count is 546; the official death toll at Thammasat University (46) is well below what survivors say (about 100 or more); and the arrest of the Gang of Four in Beijing was announced only twelve days later.
