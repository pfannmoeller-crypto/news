"Lighthouse or Landgrab?" — a16z partner Joe Schmidt lays out a two-question framework for AI go-to-market strategy
A16z partner Joe Schmidt and investing intern Julian Marx published "Lighthouse or Landgrab? How to Pick Your AI Sales Strategy" on 27 July, arguing that AI startups waste months chasing marquee Fortune 100 "lighthouse" logos when their actual market calls for a fast, volume-driven "landgrab" instead — and vice versa. The framework rests on two questions: how exposed is the buyer who signs (regulated industry, system-of-record replacement, external-facing output — all raise the stakes of a vendor mistake), and does social proof travel in that market (concentrated, status-conscious markets like law or finance let one marquee win unlock the rest; fragmented markets like mid-market accounts receivable do not). The essay uses a16z's own portfolio to illustrate both sides: legal-AI company Harvey (lighthouse: Allen & Overy and Paul Weiss signed first, now hundreds of millions in ARR at an $11 billion valuation) and financial-research platform Hebbia (lighthouse: KKR and BlackRock among clients now covering over 40% of the largest asset managers by AUM) against accounts-receivable automation startup Stuut (landgrab: sub-week deployment, 40% cash-flow increase for customers) and customer-support company Decagon (landgrab: zero to eight figures in ARR in 18 months, valued at $4.5 billion).

Context & background

Schmidt is an a16z partner focused on software, fintech and insurtech, with a running body of enterprise-strategy essays for the firm ("Avoiding Death on the Yellow Brick Road," a co-write on Workday's competitive position). This piece explicitly builds on colleague Alex Rampell's older "Distribution vs Innovation" framework — Schmidt and Marx borrow Rampell's line that startups need "distribution before the incumbent gets innovation" as the underlying logic for why speed matters in landgrab markets — and sits alongside a broader a16z Enterprise essay series from 2026 including Seema Amble and James da Costa's "Need for Speed in AI Sales," which the piece does not directly cite but clearly continues in tone and subject.

The details

The essay names four current a16z portfolio companies as case studies without disclosing any new funding, valuation, or deal terms for any of them — this is a strategy essay, not an announcement. It also cites Affirm's early "mattress company" playbook (landing Casper, then every other mattress company, then adjacent big-ticket categories) as the historical template for sequencing from lighthouse to landgrab once a category is established. The piece names specific named "traps" for each mode: for lighthouse, "becoming a hostage to the logo" and "pilot purgatory" (unconverted six-month proofs-of-concept); for landgrab, "dying of indigestion" (qualifying in unprofitable accounts at volume) and "mistaking the valley for the market" (canvassing dense metro areas instead of the tens of thousands of companies outside normal venture networks). No round sizes, dollar figures tied to this essay's publication, or new a16z capital commitments appear anywhere in the piece.

Context

Read against last week's physical-AI cluster (Atoms, Etched, Applied Intuition's Dana), this essay extends the same underlying a16z instinct — speed and deployment discipline beat theoretical capability or prestige — from hardware and infrastructure into go-to-market execution specifically. It is also notable for what it is not: unlike the prior three weeks, this window produced no new capital deployment at all, so a16z's public content is carrying the entire week rather than supplementing a deal. That is worth naming as a pattern rather than reading too much into any single essay's substance.
