AnthropicTOP10 Nov
Anthropic prices its stock-market debut at up to $1.404 trillion, below the $2 trillion once reported
An updated filing sets 825 million shares at $70 to $78 each, raising up to $64 billion, with trading expected on Nasdaq around 19 November. The value at the top of the range is about 45% above May's private round but below the earlier target.
●●●●○ 4/5
The details

Anthropic's updated filing (10 Nov) offers 825 million Class A shares (the ordinary, voting kind) at $70 to $78, raising $57.75 to $64.35 billion. At the top price the company would be worth $1.404 trillion counting all shares and options, against $965 billion in a May private round. Separately it will sell $26 billion of non-voting stock at the same price to AMD, Broadcom, Google, Micron, Microsoft, Nvidia and TSMC (Taiwan's chip maker), all suppliers or partners; Google and Nvidia put in $10 billion each. Net proceeds could reach $84.6 to $93.7 billion.

The filing also shows third-quarter revenue of $17 billion, a nine-month net loss of $104 billion, commitments to buy computing power of $900.9 billion and about $132 billion in cash. Yearly revenue pace (the latest sales rate stretched over a year) reached $81 billion at the end of October, up about $10 billion in a month. Up to 20% of shares go to retail buyers. Each of the seven co-founders sells about 3.4 million shares, roughly $250 million each at the middle of the price range. Founders hold a special share with 50.1% of the votes, and a trust elects the board majority, so ordinary buyers get little say.

Disclosure: this briefing is drafted with Claude, made by Anthropic.

The counter-case

Matt Scherer of the Open Markets Institute (a think tank that opposes market concentration) argued on 2 November that the US Securities and Exchange Commission should delay the listing until two audited quarters exist. He cites about $895 billion in commitments, unaudited 2026 figures and revenue growth that "slowed to a crawl" in the third quarter. This is a standing argument written before the 10 November terms; it answers the first filing, not this price range. Gary Marcus (an AI sceptic who writes a widely read newsletter) raised omissions in the draft filing on 26 October (also before the terms; I could not read the full text). No critic reacting to the $1.404 trillion figure itself was found.

Scherer, Tech Policy Press Marcus, 26 Oct

OpenAI11 Nov
OpenAI's quarterly revenue reportedly passes $10 billion, but Anthropic is still ahead
Bloomberg reports, from anonymous sources, that OpenAI took in more than $10 billion in the third quarter, up almost 50% on the second. Anthropic reported $17 billion for the same quarter, though the two count revenue differently.
●●●○○ 3/5
The details

Per Bloomberg, relayed by TechCrunch, OpenAI's quarterly revenue topped $10 billion for the first time, against $6.7 billion in the second quarter. Growth between the first two quarters had been about 18%, so this is a clear speed-up. OpenAI's adjusted operating margin (profit or loss before some costs, as a share of sales) improved from minus 150% to minus 109%, and its yearly revenue pace is $50 billion.

The comparison with Anthropic comes with a warning from TechCrunch: Anthropic counts sales made by its cloud partners, while OpenAI counts only its own share of some partner sales. The $50 billion and $81 billion yearly paces are therefore not like for like. OpenAI has not confirmed the figures.

The counter-case

No counter-voice found this week. No named sceptic was found reacting to this revenue figure; coverage of OpenAI's $11 billion raise last week as below target is journalists' framing, not a critic. A long-running argument is Gary Marcus's 14 October post citing a study that found no measurable financial effect of AI spending.

Marcus, 14 Oct

What to watch

OpenAI has not confirmed the figure; Anthropic's pricing on 18 November will invite direct comparison.

NvidiaTOP11 Nov
Nvidia's board met on removing chief Jensen Huang; as of 12 November morning no decision is public
Nvidia's board met on 11 November after reports of two relationships, one with a woman US intelligence believes is a Chinese spy. The outcome is open, there is no named successor, and reports say some directors favour the chief financial officer as interim chief executive.
●●●●○ 4/5
The details

The Wall Street Journal reported that the board was preparing to force Huang out; CNBC confirmed late on 11 November that the board was meeting to decide. Huang admitted on 8 November to an affair with a woman who US intelligence believes works for China's Ministry of State Security; the New York Times then reported a second relationship with a senior Nvidia employee. The board had already opened an independent investigation with the law firm Wachtell, Lipton, Rosen & Katz on the spying allegations. Neither CNBC, France 24 nor The Next Web reported a decision as of 12 November morning.

Succession is the open question. Huang says he has about 60 direct reports and Nvidia has no known succession plan; Bloomberg reports an outside firm is already identifying candidates, and Semafor reports some directors favour the chief financial officer, Colette Kress, as interim chief executive. The wider issue is Megaspeed, a company reportedly at the centre of a US smuggling probe over Nvidia chips reaching China. The shares fell more than 8% in after-hours trading on 11 November, after the second report (CNBC); our Markets briefing covers the earlier price moves.

The counter-case

Gary Marcus (an AI sceptic and author; 9 Nov, two posts) argues the Trump administration and Nvidia may have known earlier, that Congress will investigate, and that a Huang exit without a succession plan could hit the whole AI investment market; he marks parts as speculation. Ed Zitron (a tech critic who writes about AI finances; 10 Nov) argues Nvidia knew about Megaspeed and used its political influence to muzzle the Commerce Department probe. Both answer this week's event directly, and both are critics of Nvidia rather than of the reporting; neither has been confirmed by the board or the company.

Marcus, 9 Nov Zitron, 10 Nov

CoreWeave11 Nov
CoreWeave says it will fund AI building with customer prepayments and contract-backed loans, not bonds
The AI computing-rental firm expects about $9 billion in customer prepayments over three months and says it will not borrow unsecured at current rates. It is also selling more short contracts at higher prices.
●●●○○ 3/5
The details

In its third-quarter report (11 Nov), CoreWeave posted revenue of $3.66 billion, up 168% on a year earlier, a net loss of $709 million and $846 million of quarterly interest. Its order backlog is $132.5 billion. Chief executive Michael Intrator said it will not borrow without collateral (the "unsecured debt market") "at current yields" (interest rates). Instead it expects roughly $9 billion of customer prepayments and deposits over the next three months, with more than $3 billion tied to new contracts for capacity already built, and draws on loans secured against specific contracts.

It raised prices about 25% in July and another 10% later, and is putting more capacity into short-term deals priced above $40 million per megawatt a year (a megawatt is a unit of power). TechCrunch notes rivals Nebius and IREN (both rent out AI computing power) lean on Microsoft prepayments too. The catch is that prepaid, fixed-price contracts could look cheap if demand keeps rising.

The counter-case

No counter-voice found this week on this report. Long-running argument: Marcus (22 Oct) pointed to CoreWeave shares down more than half in six months, and Zitron's claim that AI firms' debt is circular (latest free post 3 Nov, not read) covers the same ground; neither answers this earnings report.

Zitron, 3 Nov

Power3–6 Nov
US power companies are trimming near-term data-centre forecasts even as announced projects grow
PJM, the grid operator for much of the eastern US, may shrink an emergency power purchase for data centres from 6,800 to about 630 megawatts, and several utilities cut forecasts. The figures predate this week; deadlines fall on 13 and 16 November.
●●●○○ 3/5
The details

Just before the window, PJM (the grid operator for 13 eastern US states) signalled that an emergency power purchase meant for data centres may shrink to about 630 megawatts from 6,800. Exelon (a US utility) cut the data-centre demand it rates as likely by 40% to 11 gigawatts (1 gigawatt is 1,000 megawatts), while American Electric Power reported demand from signed large customers up 22% to 84 gigawatts. Jefferies (an investment bank) counts PPL (a US power company), FirstEnergy and AES Ohio (an Ohio utility) cutting forecasts by a combined 6.5 gigawatts.

The pattern is a gap between announced and likely demand, which fits TechCrunch's 11 November piece arguing many announced data centres may never be built. I include the story for its two key dates, not for the 3 to 6 November figures, which come from trade-press summaries I could not open in full.

The counter-case

No counter-voice found this week. Long-running argument: Ed Zitron's claim that debt-fuelled AI spending is "circular" (Credit Crunch, 6 Oct; Annualized Enron Rate, 13 Oct) predicts exactly this gap, but neither piece addresses the utility figures.

New YorkTOP10 Nov
New York opens registration for the first binding US state oversight of leading AI labs
The state opened a registration portal on 10 November. Large AI developers such as OpenAI and Anthropic must register by 1 January 2027, report serious incidents within 72 hours and face fines up to $3 million.
●●●●○ 4/5
The details

The RAISE Act (New York's frontier-AI safety law) applies to developers with more than $500 million in yearly revenue. They must publish safety frameworks, report critical safety incidents within 72 hours and issue public model transparency reports. A new state office, DIGIT (the Office of Digital Innovation, Governance, Integrity, and Trust), runs it; fines reach $1 million for a first violation and $3 million after. The state can also act against firms that fail to register, though a spokesperson gave no specifics.

Governor Kathy Hochul also launched a research centre at Binghamton University to test the safety of advanced AI systems. California's similar law has applied since January, with a 15-day incident-reporting window; Illinois follows on 1 January 2027 and adds independent audits. New York's 72 hours is the tightest window of the three. A law-firm explainer notes the Commerce Department may flag state laws like this to be overridden by federal law.

The counter-case

No counter-voice found this week; only law-firm explainers were found. A long-running argument, not about this law: Princeton researchers Arvind Narayanan and Sayash Kapoor (21 May) say hard governance work beats extraordinary government intervention. Their view of the law itself is unknown.

Narayanan and Kapoor, 21 May Cooley, 31 Mar

Reuters says the allegations against Nvidia chief executive Jensen Huang should push lawmakers toward tighter rules, and the House has already passed a bill on cloud access to restricted chips. South Korea says Nvidia's troubles will not disrupt supply it has contracted.

Gene editing10 Nov
Beam's gene-editing sickle cell therapy hits its goal in a key late-stage trial, a first for this technique
Beam says 31 patients had no severe pain crises over 12 months of follow-up, and plans to ask the US drug regulator for approval as soon as the end of 2026. No detailed results have been released.
●●●○○ 3/5
The details

Beam Therapeutics announced on 10 November that its therapy risto-cel met the main goal of its pivotal trial: freedom from severe vaso-occlusive crises, the painful blockages that define sickle cell disease. The data cover 31 patients who had completed treatment and 12 months of follow-up by 20 October; hospital stays also fell. Beam released no specific rates. It is the first positive late-stage result for base editing, which changes single DNA letters instead of cutting the strand.

Beam would be third to market behind Casgevy (a gene-editing therapy from Vertex and a partner firm) and Lyfgenia (another approved sickle cell gene therapy). Casgevy reached 64 patients and $116 million in 2025 and $219 million in the first nine months of 2026, after a very slow start. Analysts at RBC (a bank) say risto-cel has a shorter manufacturing process and needs fewer rounds of collecting patients' cells.

The counter-case

No counter-voice found this week on Beam. Long-running argument: Healio's reporting (29 July) that stem-cell collection and manufacturing limit access to gene-editing treatments for sickle cell, which applies to the whole class.

Healio, 29 Jul

Beam's approval filing, possible by the end of 2026; full trial data at a later medical meeting (date not announced).
