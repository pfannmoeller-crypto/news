US equities · NvidiaMon 9 – Tue 10 Nov 2026

US shares post their best day since April, then give back some of it as Nvidia falls 5.5%

On Monday 9 November the S&P 500 (an index of 500 large US companies) rose 2.35% to 7,549.94, its best day since April, as oil prices fell. On Tuesday, after the inflation figure, it lost 0.38% to 7,520.93; the Nasdaq Composite fell 0.74% and the Dow Jones Industrial Average (30 large US companies) 0.30%.

Technology was the weak spot: the sector fell 1.4%, and Nvidia, the AI-chip maker, lost 5.5% and pulled the index down more than any other single share. Another report says the ten-year government bond yield moved back above 5%.

Context & background: The 9 November edition ended with the index 5.7% below its record after the worst day since April 2025 (Thursday 5 November). Monday recovered a large part of that fall; the 9 November close of 7,549.94 is still about 3.4% below the 6 October record of 7,818.93, and Tuesday's close is about 3.8% below it. Over the two days the index gained 1.95% from Friday's 7,376.86, the Nasdaq about 1.98% from 24,579.42 and the Dow about 1.84% from 51,285.73 (our arithmetic from the published closes).

The details: Monday's rise came with a drop in oil prices of almost 5% (see Commodities & energy), which lowers the inflation worry. Even so, one report notes that Nvidia, one of the largest shares in the index, fell that day. On Tuesday the move reversed: the report we read ties it to the higher-than-forecast inflation figure and to concern about the price of AI shares, and mentions "scrutiny" involving Nvidia's chief executive Jensen Huang. We could not establish what that scrutiny is, so we do not treat it as a cause. The technology sector's 1.4% fall was the worst of the 11 sectors of the index.

Why it still matters: Nvidia is the share the market is watching: its results are due later this month (date not confirmed in our sources), and it has now fallen sharply on two of the last four trading days. Wednesday 11 November's closing levels are missing here because no reliable report of them turned up; the snapshot therefore ends on Tuesday. A caveat on sources: the Tuesday figures come from one chain (a news agency report relayed by a German news-collection site), although the three index levels are consistent with Monday's closes and the stated changes.

Federal Reserve · US inflationTue 10 Nov 2026

US consumer prices rise 0.4% in October, above forecasts, and put a larger December rate rise in play

The US Bureau of Labor Statistics (the government statistics agency) reported on Tuesday 10 November that consumer prices rose 0.4% in October from September, and 0.3% excluding food and energy, the third month in a row at that pace. Forecasts were for about 0.3% and about 0.2%. One market-news site calls the report "in line" (as forecast); most others describe it as higher than expected.

No official figure compared with a year earlier exists for October: the 2025 US government shutdown left a gap in last year's data, so the monthly figures are the main reading this time.

Context & background: The 9 November edition left this as the open question. The Federal Reserve holds its rate at 3.75%–4.00% while inflation runs well above its 2% target, and the strong jobs report on 6 November had already pushed interest-rate futures to a 74% chance of a rise at the 8–9 December meeting (Bloomberg, via TheStreet Pro). This was the last inflation report before that meeting. Because the 2025 shutdown left no October 2025 survey, the Bureau could not publish its usual year-on-year rate for October; the last one, for September, was 3.7%.

The details: Month on month, the overall figure rose 0.4%, down from September's 0.6% but still high; the figure excluding food and energy was 0.3%. MUFG (a Japanese bank) put the middle of analysts' forecasts collected by Bloomberg (the financial news service) for that figure at 0.23%, so 0.3% is a clear step above. Components come from a single news-collection site (shelter 0.3%, food 0.5%, energy 0.7%); its gasoline figure conflicted with another table in the same release, so we omit it. One outlet, citing the Associated Press, reports that the Federal Reserve governor Christopher Waller had said beforehand that a stronger response could be needed if the figure excluding food and energy reached 0.3%. It did. A news-collection site's headline says the result raises the prospect of a larger December rise, meaning 0.50 percentage points instead of 0.25. Another site's headline quotes the Minneapolis Federal Reserve president, Neel Kashkari, as saying the direction is right; we could not read either piece in full.

Why it still matters: We found no interest-rate-futures probability for December after the report, so the 74% figure is the last confirmed one and has probably risen. Next dates: the written record (minutes) of the Federal Reserve's 28 October meeting, around 18 November, and the 8–9 December meeting itself. Caveats: we could not open the Bureau's release; the overall figure and the figure excluding food and energy are consistent across several outlets, but the component breakdown is not. Tuesday's fall in shares and rise in government bond yields (the interest rate the government pays to borrow) (see Equities) shows how the market read it.

Oil · Iran warMon 9 Nov 2026

Oil falls almost 5% to a two-month low after Trump signals he is unlikely to order new strikes on Iran

Brent crude, the standard international oil price, was down about 1.5% at $96.91 early on Monday 9 November and later fell almost 5%, to its lowest since early September, after US President Donald Trump said on Sunday he was unlikely to order new attacks while talks with Iran continue. One report puts the low at $93.22.

Ships were still being attacked near the Strait of Hormuz, the narrow passage for much of the world's oil, and senior US and Gulf officials doubted that a deal was close. We found no closing price for Monday, Tuesday or Wednesday.

Context & background: The 9 November edition had Brent at $98.29 on Thursday 5 November, after a fall of almost 9% on 4 November, and said the weekly change was unconfirmed (estimates of 12%–15%). One later report calls last week's fall 16%, the steepest weekly drop since the start of the Covid-19 pandemic; the sources still disagree, so we show a range. The war between the US and Iran is in its ninth month, and energy prices are the reason central banks, including the Federal Reserve, are leaning toward higher rates.

The details: The restraint remark came from Trump on Sunday 8 November. He was due to meet the prime minister of Qatar, who is mediating, on Monday. Reuters reported that senior US and Gulf officials cast doubt on an imminent deal. Fresh attacks on shipping near the Strait of Hormuz continued, but the price fell anyway. Some analysts also say the global oil market is returning to more supply than demand because high prices are cutting demand. The figures during the day differ between outlets, which is why we give the reading time with each number.

Why it still matters: Cheaper oil is the one force that could ease the inflation behind the rate rises (see Macro & central banks), and Monday's fall helps explain the large rise in shares the same day. But the move rests on a statement, not on a change in supply: it can reverse as quickly as it did on 5 November. The Geopolitics briefing covers the war and the strait. lightbulbEditor's view

Cheaper oil lifted shares on Monday; stubborn inflation took part of it back on Tuesday

1. The inflation figure is the story of the three days. Prices excluding food and energy rose 0.3% for the third month, above the 0.23% middle forecast, and the Federal Reserve now has a larger December rise to weigh. The honest gap: we have no post-report futures probability.

2. Two forces pulled in opposite directions. Oil at a two-month low is good for inflation; the October figure shows prices have not yet followed. Shares rose on the first and fell on the second.

3. Nvidia is carrying the market again. A 5.5% fall in one share pulled the index down more than any other on Tuesday. Its results, due later this month, are the next test, alongside the Federal Reserve minutes around 18 November.

4. The oil move rests on diplomacy. Monday's fall followed a remark, not a change in supply; see the Geopolitics briefing for the war and the Strait of Hormuz.
