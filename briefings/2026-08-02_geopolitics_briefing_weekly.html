Geopolitics · 27 July – 2 August 2026

Zelensky leaves Washington with a promise that Trump's envoys will finally visit Kyiv but no Patriots, a Russian cruise missile detonates on Polish soil and scrambles NATO jets, Iran fires on a US base in Jordan hours after Trump calls the talks "good," and Ukraine opens a fragile new front by striking an Iranian ship in the Caspian.

A week ago the frame was a first quiet night in the Iran war, a resolved question about who fired the drone that hit Romania, and a Pentagon casualty count reorganised into a new bureaucratic category. All of that has since been overtaken by faster-moving events: Zelensky's White House meeting on 28 July produced warmth but almost nothing tangible beyond a tentative envoy visit, even as the Senate voted 86-12 the same evening to advance the most structurally novel Russia sanctions bill yet; Russia then hit Kyiv twice in three nights, once sending a cruise missile into Polish territory and once killing nine people in the capital while Ukraine's Patriot interceptor stocks ran dry; Iran and the US swung from Trump's "good chance" of a deal to an IRGC missile attack and a CENTCOM retaliatory strike within about 30 hours; and Ukraine's own strike on an Iranian vessel in the Caspian Sea opened a new, deliberately de-escalated but genuinely dangerous bilateral front that did not exist a week ago.

Ukraine / Russia — a warm but thin Washington meeting, two brutal nights over Kyiv, and an election machine losing its footing

4 items
Diplomacy28 Jul

Zelensky's Trump meeting produces warmth, a tentative Kyiv-visit promise — and no Patriot commitment

President Zelensky's White House meeting with Trump on 28 July took place in what Ukrainian officials called a warm atmosphere but produced few concrete results, people familiar with the talks told the Kyiv Independent. Zelensky pressed for at least 300 Patriot interceptor missiles before winter and for Washington to follow through on a license letting Ukraine co-produce PAC-3 interceptors domestically; no new delivery commitments were made. The one apparent breakthrough was a renewed, still-tentative US promise that envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner — who have shuttled to Moscow repeatedly but never to Kyiv — would finally visit Ukraine, an idea that gained momentum after influencer Laura Loomer raised it directly with Trump by phone from Kyiv.

Context & background. Last week's edition left the Witkoff/Kushner Moscow trip unconfirmed and reported no corresponding Kyiv stop was planned at all. This week supplies a firmer, if still contested, answer: multiple Ukrainian outlets now report a Kyiv visit is actively being arranged, though the timing itself is disputed across sources — the Kyiv Independent's US-official sourcing (29 July) puts it "within the next two weeks," while Ukrainska Pravda, RBC-Ukraine and UNN (30–31 July) report Ukrainian sourcing pointing to "the second half of August," potentially timed to Ukraine's 24 August Independence Day. Ukraine's ambassador to Washington, Olha Stefanishyna, confirmed the visit was discussed but stressed "arrangements remained unfinished."

The details. Even if it happens, Kyiv would not be the envoys' first stop: officials briefed on the planning say Witkoff and Kushner are expected to travel to Moscow first, before continuing to Ukraine — partly logistics (Ukraine's closed airspace means the only practical route in is by rail from Poland) and partly sequencing, since the pair are "already on their way" once committed to a Moscow leg. Substantively, Kyiv came away with less than it wanted on Patriots: Zelensky's own account described discussing "licenses for Patriot interceptor production and several other ideas," but a Ukrainian delegate afterward called the outcome "very good-natured, but still vague." The Washington shift in framing is notable in its own right — US officials increasingly believe both Moscow and Kyiv may now have separate reasons to consider a narrower air ceasefire (rather than a full ceasefire), a lower bar than anything discussed in prior weeks, driven by Ukraine's expanded deep-strike campaign and Russia's intensified missile and drone attacks on Ukrainian cities.

Context. A trip that keeps slipping in scheduling and keeps being described as tentative by the same officials proposing it is a pattern worth naming rather than glossing over: it mirrors the Moscow-trip uncertainty this briefing flagged in prior weeks, and suggests Washington's diplomatic bandwidth on Ukraine remains secondary to its Iran-war and domestic priorities even as it makes repeated public gestures toward reviving the process.

Watch-Items. (1) Whether the Kyiv visit happens at all, and on which of the two disputed timelines. (2) Any concrete movement on the PAC-3 co-production license, separate from the Patriot-supply reversal covered below. (3) Whether the "air ceasefire" framing produces an actual proposal to either side. (4) Follow-through from Zelensky's Capitol meetings with senators the same day.

Confidence: medium (sourced to people familiar with talks; Kyiv-visit timing contradicts across outlets and is flagged above)
Air war30 Jul–1 Aug

Russia hits Ukraine twice in three nights — 8 dead nationwide, then 9 in Kyiv alone — as Trump walks back the Patriot license he'd floated

Russia launched a massive nationwide ballistic-missile-and-drone barrage overnight into 30 July, killing at least eight people (including children) and injuring more than 50 across Ukraine — the same barrage that sent a Russian Kh-101 cruise missile into Polish territory (see Hybrid warfare, below). Two nights later, overnight into 1 August, Russia struck Kyiv again with 35 missiles and 185 attack drones; only one of 27 ballistic missiles was intercepted, Zelensky said, "simply because there are no interceptors for the Patriot system." At least nine people were killed and more than 30 injured, with Mayor Vitali Klitschko reporting people trapped in a partially collapsed building. Trump, meanwhile, told reporters at Camp David on 31 July that his administration has "not agreed" to let Ukraine produce its own Patriot interceptors under license, calling it "a hard thing to give away that kind of technology" — a reversal from Zelensky's 9 July statement that the two sides had already reached agreement.

Context & background. Last week's edition recorded the front line as static and flagged Ukraine's Patriot shortage only implicitly, through the Pentagon casualty dispute. This week makes the interceptor shortage the story in its own right, with Zelensky naming the specific mechanism — Ukraine physically had no PAC-3 rounds left to fire at 26 of the 27 incoming ballistic missiles — rather than describing a general capability gap.

The details. The sequence matters: Zelensky told Trump directly on 28 July that Ukraine needs at least 300 Patriot interceptors before winter to prevent the energy grid from collapsing under Russian attack; three days later, at Camp David, Trump publicly walked back the co-production license Zelensky had described as already agreed on 9 July. Whether that reflects a genuine change of position or simply exposes that no firm agreement existed in the first place is not resolvable from available reporting — both readings are consistent with the "very good-natured, but still vague" characterisation of the White House meeting from the card above. On the ground, the toll pattern itself is a data point: the 30 July nationwide barrage (8 dead, 50+ injured, reaching as far as the Slovak border) and the 1 August Kyiv-focused barrage (9 dead, 30+ injured) are two distinct nights rather than one event, indicating Russia is sustaining rather than spiking its strike tempo.

Context. A public Trump reversal on Patriot licensing, delivered days after a "good" meeting and hours after Ukraine's heaviest single-night civilian toll of the window, is the sharpest visible gap this briefing has recorded between the atmosphere of US-Ukraine diplomacy and its material results — worth tracking as a leading indicator of whether Washington's renewed diplomatic attention translates into capability, or stays at the level of gesture.

Watch-Items. (1) Whether Trump's "we're talking about it" on the PAC-3 license becomes a firm decision either way. (2) Further barrage nights and whether Ukraine's interception rate recovers. (3) Any resumption of the Witkoff/Kushner Moscow-first diplomatic track discussed above. (4) Confirmed final casualty figures for both nights, which were still being updated at time of writing.

Confidence: high (Zelensky, Klitschko and Trump all on record; casualty figures were revised upward within hours and should be read as provisional)
Economic warfare24–31 Jul

Wildberries strikes spread to Volgograd and Tatarstan as the retailer calls them "terrorism"; Ukraine hits one of Russia's largest oil refineries

Ukrainian drones continued striking Wildberries facilities on 31 July, hitting a warehouse in Volgograd Oblast and another in Zelenodolsk, Tatarstan, prompting founder Tatyana Kim to call the campaign "acts of terrorism" against civilians affecting "millions of people in Russia and other countries" — a sharper framing than her earlier comparisons to Amazon. Separately, Ukraine's General Staff confirmed a strike on 30 July against a Lukoil-owned refinery in Perm, one of Russia's largest, part of the sustained campaign against Russian refining capacity that began weeks earlier. Wildberries has since begun hiding military-related goods from its own search results, while Russian billboards in occupied Crimea now openly urge shoppers to keep supporting Wildberries sellers.

Context & background. Last week's edition tracked the Wildberries campaign's escalation from roughly ten warehouses to five regions and Moscow's own airspace, calling it Ukraine's newly acknowledged "bringing the war home" doctrine. This week shows the campaign has neither slowed nor been contained to logistics: it has widened geographically (Volgograd, Tatarstan) and drawn a harder rhetorical response from the company itself, while Ukraine's parallel refinery campaign — a separate but related economic-pressure track — reached one of Russia's largest single refining assets.

The details. Kim's shift from "we're no different than Amazon" to calling the strikes "terrorism" reflects the campaign's cumulative bite: Forbes reporting cited last week already showed Wildberries and rival Ozon account for an estimated 8.5% of Russian GDP and over 5% of the workforce, in a retail sector already showing its first shop-count decline in two decades. Wildberries actively curating its own search results to de-emphasise military-adjacent goods is a tacit admission that Ukraine's stated targeting rationale — that the platform supplies Russian military logistics — has some public traction inside Russia, even as the company and Kremlin-aligned messaging (the Crimea billboards) work to reframe the strikes as an attack on ordinary consumers rather than military supply chains. The Lukoil-Perm strike sits in a longer-running, separate track: Ukraine has struck Russian refineries in Bashkortostan and elsewhere in preceding weeks, and Perm's inclusion signals the campaign continues to reach deep into Russian territory rather than concentrating near the border.

Context. Two parallel economic-pressure campaigns — retail logistics and refining capacity — running simultaneously and expanding rather than plateauing is consistent with a Ukrainian strategy of maximising domestic Russian economic friction ahead of September's Duma elections (see next card), on the theory that fuel shortages and disrupted deliveries translate more directly into public discontent than purely military losses do.

Watch-Items. (1) Whether the Wildberries damage estimate (10% of network as of last week) is updated. (2) Whether Ozon becomes a target, as flagged as a possibility last week. (3) Further refinery strikes and any measurable fuel-price effect beyond the Sevastopol data point tracked in earlier editions. (4) Any Kremlin policy response beyond the standing "military logistics" denial.

Confidence: high (Ukrainian General Staff, company founder and multiple outlets on record)

Moscow's election machine heads into September without a program, as United Russia's own polling shows it sliding toward 29%

With State Duma elections set for 18–20 September, United Russia — which holds 321 of 450 seats — has entered the campaign without a finalised platform, according to Russian political strategists cited by independent outlet Meduza, and support for the party has slipped to around 34% in recent state polling, having dipped as low as 29.3% in March; the exiled Navalny-founded Anti-Corruption Foundation puts it at just 16%. Putin's own approval has drifted down but state pollsters still put it no lower than roughly 65%. Reports say senior security officials had floated postponing the vote given the disruption from Ukraine's drone campaign, and separately that a fresh mobilisation wave could follow the election — both denied or unconfirmed by the Kremlin.

Context & background. Prior editions tracked the Kremlin's pre-election crackdown on individual critics (the Nadezhdin conviction, the Remeslo arrest) as evidence of a defensive posture ahead of September. This week's reporting adds the structural picture behind that posture: United Russia's own campaign apparatus is described by insiders as disorganised, and its polling — even by state-friendly VTsIOM — shows real erosion rather than stability.

None of this points to a contested election in the meaningful sense — United Russia's Duma majority is not genuinely at risk — but the internal Kremlin debate over timing and messaging suggests the war's domestic costs are now visible enough inside the system itself to require active management, not just suppression of individual critics.

Escalation28–29 Jul

Iran fires on a US base in Jordan hours after Trump calls the talks "good"; CENTCOM strikes back the next night

Hours after Trump told supporters in Michigan there was "a good chance" of progress in Iran talks, the IRGC launched multiple ballistic missiles at a US military base in Jordan at 5:45pm ET on 28 July in what CENTCOM called an "attempted surprise attack" — Iran's first such attack since Trump paused US strikes days earlier. All missiles were intercepted. At 10pm ET on 29 July, CENTCOM completed a "heavy wave" of retaliatory strikes against Iran. It was the first exchange of fire since the diplomatic pause began, and it happened while negotiators in Oman-led talks were, by Trump's own account, making progress.

Context & background. Last week's edition recorded Iran's first quiet night in two weeks (25 July) as a fragile but real signal. This attack, four days later, is the sharpest reversal of that signal available: it happened on the same day as Trump's most optimistic public comments about the talks, underlining how little the diplomatic and military tracks are currently coordinated on either side.

The details. Iran's Foreign Ministry has publicly and repeatedly denied holding direct talks with Washington at all, insisting only that "intermediaries may convey messages" — a framing at odds with Trump's characterisation of "very friendly negotiations," and one that leaves room for hardline elements inside Iran's government to authorise military action without formally contradicting the diplomatic track led by Foreign Minister Araghchi's more moderate wing. Analysts told Al Jazeera that Iran's underlying negotiating position may be strengthening, not weakening, despite the attack: reporting citing the Wall Street Journal says the US is grappling with dwindling air-defence interceptor stocks and a depleted munitions posture, which Middle East Institute fellow Alex Vatanka and Lancaster University's Simon Mabon both argue gives Tehran more leverage to "push harder" rather than capitulate. All Iranian missiles being intercepted, without loss, is itself informative: it suggests either a deliberately calibrated, lower-intensity signal from Tehran or a genuine miscalculation about US defences — this briefing cannot distinguish between the two from available reporting.

Context. The pattern here — strike, pause, resume — matches the "hot and cold" characterisation flagged in earlier editions, but this is the first time within the current diplomatic window that the "hot" side has come from Iran initiating rather than the US resuming. That reverses the sequence this briefing has tracked since 8 July and is worth reading as evidence the negotiating leverage genuinely may be shifting.

Watch-Items. (1) Whether the Oman-led talks continue despite the exchange, or collapse. (2) Any further IRGC attacks and their target selection. (3) Netanyahu's White House meeting outcome and its effect on US appetite for continued strikes (see below). (4) Whether Iran's public denial of direct talks persists even as substantive proposals keep moving.

Diplomacy27–28 Jul

Hormuz talks centre on a "regional consortium" or transit fees, as Iran signals it will never accept the pre-war status quo

Oman-led mediation, with Qatar, Pakistan and Egypt also involved alongside Witkoff and Kushner, is exploring proposals to resolve the Strait of Hormuz dispute that reignited fighting in July: either a regional maritime-security consortium modelled on the Malacca and Singapore Straits framework, or a fee system — administered either through a jointly run International Maritime Organization fund or by Iran directly — to replace the outright transit-route control Tehran claims under the June memorandum of understanding. Iran's lead negotiator, Parliament Speaker Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf, said plainly on 27 July that "the administration of the strait will never return to the way that it was before the war."

Al Jazeera · Hormuz negotiations explainer
Read moreexpand_more

Context & background. Prior editions described the Hormuz dispute only at the level of "who controls new transit routes" without detailing what a resolution might actually look like. This week's reporting, drawing on Wall Street Journal and Axios sourcing, is the first to lay out concrete proposed mechanisms rather than just the dispute's existence.

The details. Under international maritime law, tolls on passage through natural straits are generally not permitted even within territorial waters, though charges for specific rendered services (docking assistance, insurance-adjacent services) are a recognised workaround — which is the legal gap both the "regional consortium" and "service fee" proposals are trying to exploit to give Iran a monetisable role without technically violating freedom-of-navigation norms. Iran's argument rests on Article 5 of the June MoU, which commits Tehran only to a charge-free 60-day passage window and separately commits it to "dialogue with the Sultanate of Oman... to define the future administration" of the strait — language Tehran now reads as license to negotiate permanent administrative control rather than a return to pre-war open passage. Frozen Iranian assets are emerging as a linked bargaining chip: US officials increasingly believe economic pressure, not continued strikes, is the more effective lever, but one analyst (Obsidian Risk Advisors' Brett Erickson) estimates Iran still generated roughly $23bn in oil revenue in the first half of the year, ahead of its own government's budget forecast — undercutting the case that sanctions alone are decisive.

Context. Ghalibaf's public statement that the strait's administration will "never" revert is a harder negotiating line than anything reported in prior weeks, and should be read alongside Iran's improving relative leverage (see the missile-attack card above): Tehran appears to be negotiating from a position it believes is strengthening, not one it believes requires concessions.

Watch-Items. (1) Whether either the consortium or fee-based model gains formal traction beyond being "explored." (2) Any movement on frozen-asset release tied to strait compliance. (3) The durability of the ceasefire given the 28 July missile exchange covered above. (4) Whether Oman's Foreign Minister Albusaidi's shuttle diplomacy produces a joint statement.

Confidence: medium (proposal detail sourced to WSJ/Axios reporting relayed by Al Jazeera; Ghalibaf quote on record)

New front25–29 Jul

Ukraine and Iran's dangerous new standoff: a Caspian Sea strike, an Iranian retaliation threat, and a de-escalating phone call

Ukraine struck an Iranian vessel transporting military cargo between Iran and Russia in the Caspian Sea on 25 July, which Iran's Foreign Ministry described as a strike on a "commercial vessel" that killed one sailor and injured another, calling it "an act of aggression" that "cannot go unanswered." The New York Times reported on 28 July, citing Iranian and Western officials, that Iran had been considering attacking a Ukrainian seaport in response, and a source familiar with Ukrainian intelligence told the Kyiv Independent Iran was weighing a missile strike on Ukraine directly. Tensions eased the same day when Ukraine's acting Foreign Minister Andrii Sybiha and Iran's Araghchi held what a Ukrainian source called "de-escalatory diplomacy"; Araghchi afterward said Sybiha described the strike as unintentional.

Context & background. No prior edition of this briefing has covered direct Ukraine-Iran confrontation; the relationship until now has run entirely through Iran's drone supply to Russia. This week's Caspian Sea strike is the first direct Ukrainian military action against an Iranian target, marking a genuinely new axis in an already crowded set of overlapping conflicts.

The details. Analysts see the strike as an extension of Ukraine's existing strategy — disrupting the logistics that sustain Russia's war effort — rather than the opening of a deliberate second front against Iran: RUSI's Natia Seskuria told the Kyiv Independent the strike targeted "the flow of Iranian military support to Russia," and IISS analyst Sascha Bruchmann said the vessel was "likely transporting components for UAVs." Context. Multiple regional analysts quoted by the Kyiv Independent converge on the same read: neither Kyiv nor Tehran wants a direct war while each is already stretched (Ukraine against Russia, Iran against the US and Israel), making this most likely to continue as indirect friction — intelligence activity, strikes on Iran-Russia logistics, cyber — rather than open conflict. That the de-escalation call happened within three days of the strike supports this reading.

Sanctions & Trade — the Senate clears its most structurally novel Russia bill yet, and the EU eyes a record 1,600-firm package

2 items
Legislation28 Jul

Senate advances the Graham Act 86-12 — 100% secondary tariffs on Russia's top oil buyers, built to survive the Supreme Court's IEEPA ruling

The Senate voted 86-12 on 28 July to advance the Lindsey O. Graham Sanctioning Russia and Iran Act of 2026, hours after Graham's funeral and immediately following a closed-door Zelensky appeal to senators; Zelensky watched the vote from the Senate chamber. The bill authorises tariffs of up to 100% on the world's five largest buyers or facilitators of Russian oil and gas — likely China, India, Slovakia, Hungary and Azerbaijan — mandates sanctions on Putin, his inner circle and Russian financial institutions, and extends Iran sanctions through 2031. Rand Paul cast the only Republican "no" vote; eleven Democrats plus independent Bernie Sanders opposed it over concerns about unchecked tariff authority. The House does not return from recess until 31 August, making September the earliest realistic date for the bill to reach Trump's desk.

Tech Times · Graham Act detail, constitutional structure Axios · 86-12 procedural vote
Read moreexpand_more

Context & background. Prior editions tracked the EU's 21st sanctions package but had not yet covered this US Senate bill in detail. Its structure turns out to be the more significant development: on 20 February 2026 the Supreme Court ruled 6-3 in Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump that IEEPA does not authorise presidential tariffs, ending all IEEPA-based tariffs that day. The Graham Act was deliberately redesigned around that ruling — its tariff authority routes through the US Trade Representative under Congress's own Article I commerce power rather than a presidential emergency declaration, making it, per multiple analysts, the likely constitutional template for any future US tariff-based sanctions tool.

The details. The bill's theory of the case is structural, not incremental: rather than designating individual shadow-fleet tankers (of which Russia has roughly 600, replaceable faster than regulators can list them — 48% of Russia's seaborne oil moved on sanctioned shadow tankers in May 2026, rising back to 66% by June per CREA data), it targets the insurers, brokers and flag registries in Dubai, Istanbul and elsewhere that make the evasion network function, while forcing buyer countries into a binary choice via the tariff threat. Russia's fossil fuel exports earned roughly €726m ($826m) a day in May 2026 — the number the bill's authors are explicitly trying to squeeze. The Democratic dissent is substantive rather than symbolic: Senators Welch and Warnock argued the bill's "facilitator" designation is vague enough to let a president tariff virtually any country for any stated reason, a concern co-author Blumenthal disputes by pointing to the bill's mandatory congressional-override mechanism on any sanctions rollback. Notably, sanctions in this version are unconditional on Russian behaviour, unlike the 2025 predecessor bill, which had made them contingent on Russia refusing ceasefire talks.

Context. A bipartisan 86-12 margin masks a real fight still to come in the House, where the tariff-authority-delegation objection is likely to resurface with more force; the earliest this becomes law is September, meaning its practical deterrent effect on Russian oil buyers has not yet begun.

Watch-Items. (1) Whether the Senate holds a final passage vote before its own recess (targeted for on or around 6 August). (2) House Speaker Johnson's ability to bring Democrats along given the tariff-authority objections. (3) Any pre-emptive buyer response from China or India before the bill becomes law. (4) Whether Trump signals support strongly enough to overcome House Democratic resistance.

Confidence: high (roll-call vote, bill text and multiple senators on record)
EU sanctions23–29 Jul, effects into Oct

EU prepares a record 1,600-firm sanctions package as the 21st package's crypto and bank measures take effect

The EU is preparing to sanction more than 1,600 companies it says are helping Russia's war effort — the largest single blacklist expansion of the war, covering firms with a combined turnover above $20bn and more than 265,000 employees — with adoption targeted for October after weeks of internal review, Bloomberg reported on 29 July. Separately, elements of the already-adopted 21st package take effect through August: the crypto-asset services ban expands on 25 August, and banks including Energobank, Transcapitalbank, Unistream and Credit Ural Bank are newly designated.

Context & background. Last week's edition noted the 21st package's shadow-fleet listing reached 692 vessels and flagged an unresolved contradiction over the oil-price-cap adjustment date. This week's reporting moves past that package to the next one already in preparation, while confirming the 21st package's practical rollout continues on schedule through August.

The details. The new 1,600-firm proposal, prepared by the EU's External Action Service, targets specific companies rather than entire product categories or sectors — an approach meant to catch elements of Russia's military-industrial complex that broader, sector-wide sanctions have missed. Even so, Bloomberg's sourcing is explicit that it "would likely not reach the economic impact of prior packages that targeted Russia's oil industry and banking system" — a deliberate scope limitation given how hard the 21st package already was to pass: Greece forced a rollback of a proposed Russian LNG transfer ban, while France and Italy watered down a proposed entry ban on former Russian combatants. Politically, the timing is notable: Germany, France and the UK are separately leading a renewed push to bring Putin to the table, coordinated with Zelensky, and EU Council President Costa's office has opened its own Kremlin channel — meaning this sanctions package is being built explicitly as negotiating leverage rather than as an end in itself. Putin has so far rejected any ceasefire, arguing it would let Ukraine rearm, and continues to demand Ukraine cede all of Donetsk Oblast as a precondition — a position Kyiv has ruled out.
