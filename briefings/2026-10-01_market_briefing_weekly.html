Markets & Macro · Weekly

Bond yields hit a 24-year high, US shares slip, and a memory-chip maker posts record sales

US shares fell over September because the yield on ten-year US government bonds rose to its highest level in more than two decades. A bond is a loan to the government; its yield is the yearly return it pays at its market price, and the yield rises when the bond's price falls. Higher yields make shares less attractive and borrowing dearer. They rose even though the Federal Reserve's (the US central bank's) preferred inflation measure came in lower than economists expected, because the US economy keeps reporting stronger numbers than forecast, which keeps another interest-rate increase by the Federal Reserve possible. Two company reports gave the week its upside: Micron, which makes the memory chips used in artificial intelligence (AI) data centres, and Accenture, a consulting group. Germany's inflation jumped, oil rebounded on Thursday, and bitcoin, a digital currency, drifted lower while the Senate's crypto bill sat untouched.

US shares slip through a losing September as strong economic data keeps bond yields high

The S&P 500 (an index of 500 large US companies) fell 0.3% on Wednesday 30 September to 7,651.54, and the Dow Jones Industrial Average (an index of 30 large US companies) lost 0.9% to 50,906.05, while the Nasdaq Composite (an index dominated by technology shares) edged up 0.2% to 26,861.06. Since the end of trading on Friday 25 September — the end of the first week of gains after two weeks of losses — the three indexes have lost between 0.8% and 1.8%, and the S&P 500 and the Dow finished September with monthly losses.

On Wednesday, two stronger-than-expected reports on the US economy kept bond yields high even though the Federal Reserve's preferred inflation measure came in lower than economists expected (see the economy stories below). On Thursday morning the big indexes were mixed to slightly higher.

Context & background: A strong survey of company activity on 23 September had sent bond yields to near-two-decade highs, and the Nasdaq Composite had closed at a record on 22 September. Friday 25 September then brought relief: the S&P 500 rose 0.5% to 7,743.41, the Dow 0.9% to 51,828.62 and the Nasdaq 0.5% to 27,068.72, as a fall in the price of Brent crude below $98 a barrel meant less worry about inflation, so bond yields eased. That left the S&P 500 within 0.7% of its record high from the previous month, and the Nasdaq up 16.5% since 1 January.

The details: The slide since then is smaller in the Nasdaq (−0.8%) than in the Dow (−1.8%) or among small companies (the Russell 2000, −1.4%), which tend to carry more debt and so feel higher borrowing costs more. The gains since 1 January shrank in the same pattern: the S&P 500's from 13.1% to 11.8%, the Nasdaq's from 16.5% to 15.6% and the Russell 2000's from 14.3% to 12.7%. Wednesday's other reports were a private-sector hiring survey showing 90,000 new jobs when economists expected 68,000, and the final revision of April–June US economic growth: an annual pace of 2.2%, against 1.5% expected (US Bureau of Economic Analysis). On Thursday morning the S&P 500 was up 0.2%, the Nasdaq up 0.4% and the Russell 2000 down 0.4%.

Why it still matters: Good economic news is currently treated as bad news for shares. Faster growth makes further interest-rate rises by the Federal Reserve more likely and lifts the yield on safe government bonds, which compete with shares for investors' money. The opposite view is that the US economy is genuinely strong and company profits — Micron and Accenture both reported this week — can absorb higher rates. The monthly jobs report (the government's count of jobs added, published on Friday 2 October) and the Federal Reserve's meeting on 27–28 October will test which view wins.

Memory chips · artificial intelligenceWednesday 30 September, after US trading ended

Micron reports $54 billion of quarterly revenue, almost five times a year earlier

Micron, the US company that makes the memory chips inside artificial intelligence (AI) data centres, reported revenue of $54.23 billion for the quarter ending in early September (its financial year does not follow the calendar). That compares with $41.46 billion in the previous quarter and $11.32 billion a year earlier. Its gross margin — the share of sales left after production costs — was 87.0%, excluding one-off items. Micron forecast revenue of $61.5 billion for its next quarter, give or take $1.5 billion.

Yahoo Finance reported that revenue and profit per share both came in above analysts' expectations and that Micron's forecast was also stronger than expected; on Thursday its results were named as the factor offsetting rising bond yields in the stock market.

Context & background: Micron makes two kinds of chips that artificial intelligence (AI) computing is short of: ordinary memory, which feeds processors with data, and high-bandwidth memory, a very fast type used in the processors that train and run AI. Before the report Micron's shares were up 276% for 2026, and traders' bets on share-price options implied a move of about 8% up or down afterwards, so good news was already expected. The quarter is the sixth in a row with record revenue.

The details: The data-centre business alone brought in $18.0 billion in the quarter, with 90% of that left after production costs and 85% left after all running costs (the operating margin). For the full financial year, revenue was $133.19 billion against $37.38 billion, profit per share excluding one-off items was $75.52, and capital spending (money put into new factories and equipment) was $27.37 billion. Management expects about $25 billion of capital spending in the first half of its next financial year, including $11.5 billion in its current quarter. Micron has signed 26 multi-year agreements with customers covering more than 35% of the revenue it expects through 2030 and hopes to raise that to about 50%. It says more than 75% of its 2027 output is already committed, with most of the 2027 supply of high-bandwidth memory sold at significantly higher prices. Sanjay Mehrotra, the company's chief executive, said chips should stay in short supply — supply and demand 'much tighter' — through the financial year to 2028. Management also plans to return more money to shareholders, mainly by buying back the company's own shares, once its cash reaches target levels at the end of the current quarter.

Why it still matters: The figures are an unusually direct measure of how much money is flowing into artificial intelligence (AI) computing hardware, and they come from the company's own release rather than from an analyst's estimate. The counter-argument is the memory industry's history of swinging between shortage and glut: a gross margin of 87% leaves little room for disappointment if customers' orders slow. The multi-year contracts are management's answer to that cycle, but their prices and volumes are not published. The technology story behind the numbers is in the separate Frontier Tech briefing; here it matters as a price move and a company result.

Inflation · Consumer spendingWednesday 30 September

The inflation measure the Federal Reserve prefers comes in below forecast, but consumer spending jumps 0.9%

The personal consumption expenditures (PCE) price index — a measure of the prices households pay, and the inflation gauge the Federal Reserve (the US central bank) watches most — rose 0.3% in August and was 3.4% higher than a year earlier. Excluding food and energy, whose prices swing widely, it rose 0.2% on the month and 3.0% on the year, according to the US Bureau of Economic Analysis, the government statistics agency. Economists had expected the monthly figure excluding food and energy to be 0.3% and the annual figure 3.3%.

The same report showed spending running well ahead of income: household spending rose 0.9% before adjusting for inflation and 0.6% after, against income growth of 0.2%, and households saved only 4.1% of their income.

Context & background: The Federal Reserve raised its benchmark rate on 16 September to 3.75%–4.00% on a unanimous vote of its 12 voting members, saying inflation 'remains elevated' and economic activity is 'expanding at a solid pace'. It must now decide whether to raise rates again, and it watches this inflation measure closely. A separate government gauge, the consumer price index for August (published 11 September), showed prices up 3.4% on a year earlier and 0.4% on the month, with gasoline accounting for more than a third of that rise.

The details: The monthly figure excluding food and energy, 0.2%, was a step up from July's 0.1% but below the 0.3% economists expected; the annual figure, 3.0%, was unchanged from July. Household spending was $190.8 billion higher, with $114.1 billion more spent on goods and $76.7 billion more on services, while personal income rose by $66.6 billion; disposable income, what households have left after tax, rose $68.6 billion, or 0.3%. The final revision of economic growth for April–June, released the same day, was an annual pace of 2.2%, against 1.5% expected.

Why it still matters: A lower-than-expected inflation reading would normally help shares and bonds, because it makes further interest-rate rises by the Federal Reserve less likely. But strong spending and growth point the other way: households spending more than they earn can keep demand — and prices — high, which is exactly what the Federal Reserve worries about. That tension is why bond yields rose and shares slipped on a day with good inflation news. The next tests are the monthly jobs report on Friday 2 October and the consumer price index for September on 14 October.

Bond yieldsWednesday 30 September – Thursday 1 October

The ten-year US government bond yield reaches 5.33%, its highest since April 2002

A US government bond (a Treasury) is a loan to the government. Its yield is the annual return it pays at today's price, and it rises when the bond's price falls. The ten-year yield ended Wednesday at 5.289%, its highest in a year, and reached 5.3338% on Thursday, the highest since April 2002. The 30-year yield was 5.632% on Wednesday and 5.6702% on Thursday, the highest since July 2002.

The ten-year yield sets the cost of mortgages and company borrowing and the value investors place on future profits, which is why a rise at this speed unsettles stock markets.

CLARITY Act · Senate15 September – 1 October

The Senate crypto bill sits untouched until at least 5 October, with betting markets putting 2026 passage near 5%

The CLARITY Act (House bill 3633), which would set federal rules for crypto markets and divide oversight between the Securities and Exchange Commission (shares) and the Commodity Futures Trading Commission (commodities and futures), lost a Senate vote to begin debate 49–50 on 15 September; it needed 60 of the 100 senators. No new Senate action has been reported this week. The Senate's next work period runs from 5 October to 6 November, straight into the US midterm elections (the November vote for Congress halfway through a president's term). The most realistic chance is the lame-duck session, the weeks after the election when outgoing members still sit.

A betting market on whether the bill is signed into law in 2026 stands at 4.9%, down 2 percentage points on the week, with $23.4 million bet on Polymarket.

Context & background: The bill passed the House 294 to 134 on 17 July 2025 and has been in the Senate since. The 15 September vote was on whether to start debating it. The bill is stalled, and the betting market reads the vote as close to fatal for 2026, but a motion to reconsider remains open: Senator Thom Tillis has asked for the vote to be taken again.

The details: One report says the bill was sunk by ethics provisions rather than by the split of oversight between the two regulators: Democrats and Republicans disagreed over rules on officials' own crypto holdings, especially those of a president's family. Another unresolved issue is stablecoins (crypto coins pegged to the dollar) and whether their issuers may pay interest. On 17 September seven Democratic senators said again that they back a cross-party deal. Two bank lobby groups, the American Bankers Association and the Bank Policy Institute, want 'targeted changes' to the rules on stablecoin interest because stablecoins compete with banks. Meanwhile the two regulators can proceed by granting exemptions, by issuing letters promising not to take enforcement action, and by writing new rules, though future administrations can reverse those steps.

Why it still matters: For crypto, 2026 now depends on the lame-duck session, so the Senate's return on 5 October and any change to the text on ethics or stablecoin interest are what to watch. Bitcoin's price is not following the bill: it recovered and rose after the failed vote and eased this week with no legislative news, as the story above shows.
