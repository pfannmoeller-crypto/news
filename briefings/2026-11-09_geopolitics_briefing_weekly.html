Geopolitics · 5–9 November 2026

Talks get a date while the strikes continue

The week's main change is that things announced earlier are now scheduled or in motion: a three-way meeting on Ukraine, a wider American squeeze on Iran, and a formal try at replacing Israel's government. None of them has produced a result yet. The first test comes on 10 November, when the delegations are due to sit down.

Status Quo — where the 5 November edition left off

bookmarkPrior read (compiled 5 November, covered 30 October – 5 November)

  • Ukraine and Russia: Russia had said it would resume US-mediated talks in November, but no date, venue or delegation had been published. Russian missile strikes on Ukraine's power system continued.
  • Iran and Hormuz: The US and Iran have been at war since 28 February. Ships were still being attacked near the Strait of Hormuz, the narrow sea passage for much of the world's oil. US options for new strikes were reported as prepared but not approved.

  • Hybrid warfare in Europe: No confirmed incident had been found since early September (hybrid warfare means sabotage and drone attacks that Russia is suspected of).
  • US politics: Democrats won both the House of Representatives and the Senate on 3 November. The effect on Ukraine aid, Iran and sanctions was not yet visible.

Ukraine / Russia — talks scheduled, bombardment unchanged

Diplomacy7–9 Nov

The Russia–Ukraine talks now have a date, a place and delegation lists: Abu Dhabi, 10–11 November

The Kremlin said on 7 November that Russia will send a senior delegation to US-mediated talks with Ukraine in Abu Dhabi on 10 and 11 November, led by presidential aide Vladimir Medinsky. Ukraine will be led by Rustem Umerov, head of its foreign intelligence service. It would be the first three-way round (US, Ukraine, Russia) since February.

In a television interview reported on 9 November, Trump said "we're very close to making a deal" and that Ukraine will "end up with a little bit different shape". Nothing in the reports says what that would mean in practice.

Context & background. Our 5 November edition could only cite an unverified second-hand summary for the timing of the talks; the dates are now confirmed by the Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov, as reported by Reuters and relayed by the Kyiv Independent. The Russian side had earlier said the draft peace proposals prepared in Oslo (Norway) "do not entirely satisfy" it. France's foreign minister said on 8 November that European countries will take part in the Abu Dhabi discussions.

The details. Russia's delegation is wider than before: besides Medinsky it includes Deputy Foreign Ministers Sergei Ryabkov (who handles relations with the US and arms control, and joins for the first time) and Mikhail Galuzin, military intelligence chief Igor Kostyukov and a senior General Staff officer, Yury Zhigarlovsky. Bloomberg, as cited by the Kyiv Independent, reads this as a sign Moscow may be ready to discuss political and military questions together. That is Bloomberg's reading, not a Russian statement. Ukraine's team also includes the president's chief of staff Kyrylo Budanov and the parliamentary faction leader David Arakhamia. Putin discussed the preparations with his Security Council on 6 November. In the interview Trump also declined to say whether he trusts Putin or Zelensky more, said he does not think Putin is "stringing" him along, and defended the Russian diesel waiver: "I didn't lift diesel sanctions." We have only the Kyiv Independent's write-up of the interview, not the broadcast.

Why it still matters. A bigger delegation raises the odds of real bargaining but proves nothing: three earlier rounds this year and three in Istanbul in 2025 produced no breakthrough. The test is whether the two days end with a joint statement, a working group, or a new date. Trump's "different shape" remark is the first public hint from him that the territorial question is on the table, but the wording is too vague to treat as a position. Zelensky's own comment on the Kyiv strikes (next card) is that Russia is "preparing for the trilateral (three-way) talks" with missiles. Watch for: who actually flies to Abu Dhabi, whether Ukraine and Russia meet in the same room, and whether the US-set 27 November deadline for a revised plan, which we could not verify, is mentioned.

Strikes6–8 Nov

Russia fired 36 ballistic missiles at Kyiv and its region overnight, hours before the talks were confirmed

Several waves of Russian ballistic missiles hit Kyiv overnight on 6–7 November, shortly after President Volodymyr Zelensky warned of another large strike on bridges and energy sites. At least six people were injured in the city, Kyiv's Northern Bridge was closed after damage, and a kindergarten and homes were hit. In the surrounding region one person was killed and power substations were struck.

Ukraine hit back on Russian territory: its drones reportedly damaged a key bridge on the R-280 highway in Russia's Rostov region, which links Russia to occupied Ukrainian areas including Crimea, and local power stations, according to the regional governor.

Context & background. Kyiv has been under attack for weeks as Russia begins its annual winter campaign against Ukraine's power grid. On 3 November, according to the Kyiv Independent, the city endured the largest strike on energy infrastructure this year, with widespread blackouts and water cut-offs. City officials and the national government are trading blame over winter preparations, and Zelensky has asked the prime minister to set up free public transport in Kyiv because bridge attacks hinder travel.

The details. Ukraine's Air Force said it shot down or electronically jammed four of the ballistic missiles and 149 of 172 drones, 137 of them jet-powered. Zelensky said the substations hit in the Kyiv region connect the Rivne and Khmelnytskyi nuclear power plants to Kyiv and the central regions. Separately, the International Atomic Energy Agency said the Zaporizhzhia nuclear plant lost its only external power line on 8 November and ran on emergency diesel generators for about ten hours before power returned. Three people, including a child, died in the Kyiv region on 8 November from carbon monoxide after running a generator in a basement. The Rostov bridge damage is described as reportedly caused by drone debris, from the governor, and has no independent confirmation; the Kyiv Independent also carried an unconfirmed report of a strike on Russia's second-largest oil refinery, which we did not verify. All casualty and interception numbers are Ukrainian official figures.

Why it still matters. The pattern from our last edition repeats: a diplomatic opening and a heavy strike inside the same days. Zelensky said he is "counting on increased bilateral support from America" against the blackout threat. Heating and water failures in a city of three million are now a bigger risk to civilians than the missiles themselves. Watch for: whether the strikes pause during the 10–11 November talks, and what Ukraine asks for on air defence and energy repair.

War crimes allegation2–6 Nov

Rescuers are still digging out a shelter in Komyshuvakha where a Russian bombing left 53 confirmed dead

On 2 November Russian forces dropped guided aerial bombs on an underground aid centre in the front-line town of Komyshuvakha in the Zaporizhzhia region, then struck again when police and rescuers arrived, Ukrainian officials say. As of 6 November, 53 people were confirmed dead and 53 more were missing. Russia says it hit Ukrainian military units. It gave no evidence.

Context & background. The town had about 5,000 residents before the full-scale invasion. The strike came one day after a rare local ceasefire let hundreds of civilians leave Russian-occupied Oleshky in the neighbouring Kherson region.

The details. Ukrainian officials say Russia used bombs of 500 kilograms first, then a three-tonne bomb that collapsed the concrete shelter. Zelensky said nine more bombs hit the shelter in the second strike. Russia's defence ministry claimed up to 25 Ukrainian soldiers were killed and 15 vehicles destroyed; a soldier from Ukraine's International Legion (a unit of foreign volunteers) told the Kyiv Independent the building had not been used by the military for a long time. We cannot check either account from here. Rescuers can work only 15 to 20 minutes at a time because of drones and the threat of more bombs. Zelensky wrote that nearly 100 people may have been killed in this one settlement in one day, and complained that "almost the entire world is silent about this"; that figure is a projection, not a count.

Why it still matters. It comes eight days before the Abu Dhabi talks. The numbers are still moving; the last count we have is 6 November. The wider point is the front-line pattern: Ukrainian officials reported 785 Russian strikes on 51 settlements in the Zaporizhzhia region in one day.

Iran / Hormuz — the squeeze widens, strikes held back for now

1 item
US pressure7–9 Nov

The US has extended its blockade of Iran to land routes and says new strikes are "highly unlikely" if talks progress

On 7 November the US Central Command (the US military headquarters for the Middle East) announced "Task Force Iron Gate", a small unit meant to stop advanced weapons, banned technology and illicit money crossing Iran's land borders and the Caspian Sea. On 9 November Trump said this land blockade is in effect, and that renewed strikes are "highly unlikely" if there is progress on Iran's nuclear programme and on reopening the Strait of Hormuz.

Tehran threatened to "rain down hell" if the US resumes attacks. Al Jazeera's live coverage of 8 November also carried a headline that the US was blamed for explosions on Qeshm, an Iranian island near Hormuz; we could not read the detail.

Context & background. The US has blockaded Iranian ports since the talks failed earlier this year, and our last edition reported prepared options for new strikes, a third aircraft carrier group and about 10,000 extra troops heading to the region. The new unit widens the economic squeeze rather than adding military force. It sits in Tampa, Florida, and, as Al Jazeera reports, is staffed by about 20 civilian and military personnel.

The details. Iran's Revolutionary Guards (its elite armed force) called the task force "just a play on words" and said earlier US claims that Iran's economy would collapse within two weeks did not come true. The same day they announced plans for underground missile and drone factories able to keep producing under bombing. US media have reported that Russia sent drone parts and explosives to Iran across the Caspian Sea, so analysts quoted by Al Jazeera see the unit as aimed partly at that route; one warned it could bring a confrontation with Russia, another noted the US has no naval presence on the Caspian and that the sea carries only a fraction of Iran's trade (one figure of 11 percent could not be verified). Joe Kent, a former Trump counterterrorism official who resigned in March over the war, called the move the military "inviting Iran to escalate" while Vice President Vance negotiates; the Wall Street Journal reported that the Central Command chief, Admiral Brad Cooper, and Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth are pressing Trump to renew attacks. Those are single-source claims. Trump also said he expects to discuss the Iran war and "denuclearisation" with China's Xi Jinping and Russia's Putin at next week's Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation summit. Al Jazeera's video also reported a drone strike that halted production at a gas field in Iraqi Kurdistan; who launched it is not stated in what we could read.

Why it still matters. The two messages are consistent only if the blockade is the alternative to bombing: pressure now, strikes if talks fail. That makes the Xi and Putin conversations a possible channel, and a possible friction point, since both countries trade with Iran. We found no new ship-attack counts for Hormuz in this window, so the question of whether the strait reopens remains where we left it. Our Markets briefing covers oil. Watch for: any strike order, any ship attributed to a named attacker, and what Trump says to Xi.

No change found5–9 Nov

No new Russia sanctions action was found; Trump defended his diesel waiver

We found no change to the pause on the Graham Act (the US law punishing buyers of Russian energy) or the US Treasury's Russian diesel licence since 5 November. In the interview reported on 9 November, Trump said "I didn't lift diesel sanctions" and described the licence as a way for Russia to supply diesel to the rest of the world rather than to the US. The first real test of whether the new Democratic Congress moves on Russia sanctions has not yet come.

Kyiv Independent · Trump interview
Read more▾

Context & background. Trump signed the Graham Act on 18 September. On 16 October his order brought its tariff powers into force but immediately waived them. A separate Treasury licence issued on 9 October allows certain deals in Russian-origin oil, and a diesel licence runs to 7 April 2027.

The details. The Kyiv Independent's account has Trump arguing that he "made it possible for Russia to give" diesel to the world, not to the US, after the interviewer pointed to the waiver. We did not find any Treasury, Commerce or State Department notice for 5–9 November, and our searches for post-election sanctions news returned only older legal commentaries, so this is a gap in what we could find, not proof that nothing happened.

Why it still matters. The pause lasts until Trump or the Secretary of State ends it, and the Abu Dhabi talks give him a reason to hold it. If the talks fail, restoring the tariffs is the first lever he has already signed. We make no prediction here.

Israel5–8 Nov

Israel's president has asked opposition leader Gadi Eisenkot, not Netanyahu, to try to form the next government

President Isaac Herzog told Eisenkot of his choice on the evening of 7 November and was due to hand him the mandate formally on 8 November. Eisenkot, a former army chief, was recommended by 67 members of the 120-seat parliament (the Knesset), including Arab-led parties; Netanyahu by 46. Eisenkot has 28 days, extendable by 14, to assemble 61 seats. Netanyahu stays as caretaker prime minister meanwhile.

The numbers are not settled. Eisenkot's four-party alliance holds 48 seats and says it will not govern with the Arab-led parties, who hold 19.

Context & background. This resolves the open point from 5 November, when we listed 11 November as the deadline for Herzog to act and reported he might seek a Netanyahu–Eisenkot unity government. He instead followed the recommendation count. Arab-led party leader Ahmad Tibi said on 7 November his side had feared a unity deal and now expects a minority Eisenkot government with outside support from the Arab parties.

The details. Adding the 19 Arab-led seats gives 67, but Eisenkot rules that out as a coalition. Winning both ultra-Orthodox parties, United Torah Judaism and Shas, would give him exactly 61; United Torah Judaism declined to recommend anyone on 5 November after falling out with Netanyahu over military-service exemptions. Al Jazeera also notes he could govern with fewer than 61 seats if he wins a parliamentary confidence vote. Netanyahu, in a recorded speech hours before the decision, said a government dependent on Arab parties "will dismantle the state of Israel from within", which Tibi answered with an insult of his own. If Eisenkot fails, Herzog picks another candidate; failing that, parliament can back a third, and then a new election within 90 days. We did not find a report of the 8 November ceremony itself, so the formal handover is stated from the president's spokesperson, via Al Jazeera.

Why it still matters. It matters beyond Israel because Netanyahu and his allies have pushed for action against Iran, and a caretaker with the right wing behind him has less to lose; that second point is our inference. A government led by Eisenkot would shift on Iran and Gaza only if it survives, and it needs the votes of parties that he has publicly refused to partner with. Watch for: any deal with the ultra-Orthodox parties, a minority-government plan with outside Arab support, and whether Netanyahu challenges the election result further.

lightbulbEditor's view

The week was about things scheduled, not things settled

1. Abu Dhabi on 10–11 November is the real event. The delegations are named and the dates are public, which was the test we set on 5 November. The result of the talks themselves is not yet known.

2. Missiles and meetings run in parallel. Russia struck Kyiv with 36 ballistic missiles the night before the talks were confirmed, and Zelensky read that as Russia preparing for the negotiating table. That is his reading, not a Russian statement.

3. Trump's language on Ukraine is softening. "A little bit different shape" is the first public hint that territory is in play, but it is one phrase in one interview, summarised by one outlet.

4. Iran is being squeezed, not bombed, for now. The land blockade and the "highly unlikely" remark point to pressure ahead of a decision, while reports of officials pushing for strikes come from a single source.

5. Israel's government fight now has a clock. Eisenkot has 28 days, which runs into early December, and needs parties he has said he will not join. A failure leaves Netanyahu as caretaker. How that interacts with Iran is our inference only.

6. Two areas stay quiet. Sanctions and European sabotage show nothing new. We would rather say that than fill the space.

Next: what comes out of Abu Dhabi, whether Trump orders strikes or speaks to Xi and Putin first, whether Eisenkot finds 61 seats, and whether the Graham Act pause survives.
