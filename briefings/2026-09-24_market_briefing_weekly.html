Wall Street swings from back-to-back records to a bond-market jolt as bitcoin roars back

The Nasdaq Composite stock index set fresh record closes on Monday and Tuesday, lifted by Meta's new personal AI agent rocketing to the top of the mobile app store, before Wednesday's sharply stronger-than-forecast reading on US business activity sent bond yields — the annual return a bond pays at its current price, which rises when the bond's price falls and vice versa — to their highest level in nearly two decades and erased most of the week's stock-market gains. Goldman Sachs, the large investment bank, now expects the Federal Reserve, the US central bank, to raise interest rates again as soon as its 28 October meeting rather than waiting until December. Oil fell for a sixth straight session as United States and Iranian officials made diplomatic contact at the United Nations, easing fears over the Strait of Hormuz, even as President Trump prepared to host Chinese President Xi Jinping in Washington. Bitcoin, meanwhile, jumped to an eight-month high on renewed buying through bitcoin exchange-traded funds (ETFs — funds that trade on a stock exchange like an ordinary share and let investors gain exposure to bitcoin's price without holding the coins themselves) and a short squeeze (traders who had bet on falling prices being forced to buy back in to limit their losses, which pushes the price up further), while the Senate's crypto market-structure bill — the CLARITY Act, intended to set clear federal rules for how crypto markets operate — remained stalled with no new action this week. Gold gave back some of its own rally as the surge in bond yields made gold, which pays no interest to whoever holds it, less attractive by comparison.

Nasdaq strings together two record closes, then a Wednesday reversal wipes out the week's gain

The Nasdaq Composite stock index closed at a record for the second straight session on Tuesday, at 27,244.28, as Meta's new personal AI agent, called Muse, shot to the top of the mobile app store — Meta's own stock jumped 11% on Monday alone, adding about $192 billion in market value. That AI-agent story is covered in this edition's Frontier Tech briefing; here it shows up as a price move. Wednesday broke the streak: a much-stronger-than-forecast reading on US business activity (see this edition's macro story, below) sent bond yields to a near two-decade high and pulled all three main US indexes lower, with smaller, more rate-sensitive companies — those whose profits and stock valuations move more with the cost of borrowing, often because they carry more debt — hit hardest.

Context & background: Stocks had entered the week digesting the Federal Reserve's rate increase from the prior edition, with the S&P 500 having closed the previous week (14–18 September) almost exactly flat and the small-cap Russell 2000 — an index of smaller companies, ranked by the total market value of their shares — already lagging. Meta's Muse — a personal AI shopping and browsing agent that reached 2.8 million downloads in 12 days, faster than ChatGPT's early adoption — gave Monday's session a clear, single-stock story to rally around, with PayPal announcing a partnership letting shoppers check out through the new agent.

The details: The Nasdaq Composite's Tuesday close of 27,244.28 was its second consecutive record. By Wednesday, a flash survey of business activity — "flash" meaning a fast, preliminary reading released before the fuller, final data, to give an early signal — came in at its strongest level in more than five years, and 72% of individual US stocks fell as investors recalculated the odds of further rate increases. Germany's DAX (Germany's benchmark stock index) fell in sympathy — that is, it dropped in step with the US sell-off even though nothing new had happened in Germany itself — down about 1.05% that same Wednesday session — described by one report as bearing "the worst of Europe's selloff" — as European trading desks (the teams at banks and investment firms who buy and sell securities for clients or for the firm itself) marked down the same rate-sensitive names (industry shorthand for individual stocks).

Why it still matters: The Russell 2000's 1.20% Wednesday drop, sharper than the S&P 500's or the Dow's, is a reminder that smaller companies — which typically carry more debt and refinance it more often — are the most exposed part of the stock market to a fast move in borrowing costs. That sensitivity is exactly what the Federal Reserve's rate path over the next several months will keep testing.

A five-year-high business-activity reading pushes bond yields to their highest since 2007, and Wall Street now expects the Fed to move again in October

A closely watched survey of US business activity, released Wednesday, came in at its strongest level in more than five years, deepening concern that the economy is growing faster than the Federal Reserve would like, which could justify further rate increases. The five-year Treasury note — essentially a loan to the US government that pays interest, and the reference point this edition uses most for "bond yields" — saw its yield jump to 4.996%, its highest since 2007, and the ten-year note's yield hit a fresh 52-week high (its highest point in the past year) of 5.106%, before both eased back slightly later in the day. Goldman Sachs responded by raising its own forecast: it now expects the Federal Reserve to raise rates twice in 2026 rather than once, with the second increase coming as soon as the Fed's very next meeting, on 28 October, rather than in December as it had previously assumed.

Context & background: Last week's edition closed with the Federal Reserve having just delivered its first rate increase in three years, on a unanimous vote, with most officials already signaling one more move before year-end. The open question this edition inherited was timing: would that second increase land at the Fed's next meeting, in six weeks, or be pushed further out toward December.

The details: Sixteen of eighteen Federal Reserve officials are now projecting at least one further increase in 2026, and the Fed's own estimate of the longer-run "neutral" interest rate — the level that neither speeds up nor slows down the economy — was revised up to 3.25% from 3.06%, a technical signal that officials think policy still has room to tighten further before it becomes restrictive, meaning high enough to actively slow the economy down rather than just hold it steady. Fed Chair Kevin Warsh described September's move as having merely "removed a dose" of support for the economy, language Goldman Sachs's economists read as leaning toward further increases rather than a pause. Traders in interest-rate futures markets — where investors buy and sell contracts betting on where interest rates will be at a future date, and the prices they're willing to pay translate into an implied probability, or "odds," of a move — pushed the odds of an October increase to roughly a coin flip immediately after the data; Goldman Sachs's October call (its published forecast) is more aggressive than that of fellow large investment banks JPMorgan's or Morgan Stanley's, which still expect December.

Why it still matters: A faster path to a second rate increase raises borrowing costs across the economy sooner than markets had priced for as recently as last week — meaning investors had already built a slower, later timeline for rate increases into today's bond and stock prices, and that assumption has now been overturned — the five- and ten-year Treasury yields quoted above feed directly into mortgage rates, corporate borrowing costs and the stock-market moves covered in the equities story, above.

Iran · HormuzMon 21 – Wed 23 Sep

Oil falls for a sixth straight session as US and Iranian officials meet at the United Nations

Brent crude (the international oil-price benchmark) fell for a sixth consecutive trading session, easing from Friday's close of $103.87 a barrel to around $98 by Wednesday — a roughly 6% drop in less than a week, a large move for oil, which typically shifts by a percent or two over that span — as diplomatic contact between US and Iranian officials on the sidelines of the United Nations General Assembly eased fears of a wider disruption to the Strait of Hormuz — the narrow waterway at the mouth of the Persian Gulf that a large share of the world's oil shipments pass through. The US oil benchmark eased into the low $90s a barrel over the same stretch. President Trump told the United Nations he expects a deal with Iran "right after the election," referring to November's midterm elections, even as he separately told advisers he wants a ban on US diesel exports.

Context & background: Last edition's oil story described Brent easing back toward $100 a barrel as an earlier, sharper Gulf supply scare cooled, after Brent had traded close to $110 during the worst of the Strait of Hormuz tension in late August and early September. This week's decline extends that cooling trend rather than starting a new one.

The details: Brent had already fallen from nearly $110 the week before to about $98.46 by Tuesday morning, and kept falling into Wednesday, helped along by reports that US and Iranian officials had direct contact at the United Nations for the first time since the worst of the crisis. That contact, plus reports that Chinese oil imports were resuming after being cut back earlier during the Iran tensions, took some of the risk premium — the extra price buyers pay to cover the chance of a supply disruption — out of the market even before Thursday's Trump–Xi summit, which was expected to touch on Iranian crude alongside trade and artificial-intelligence issues.

Why it still matters: A sustained oil retreat is one of the few developments that could let the Federal Reserve stop after just one more rate increase rather than several, since fuel costs feed directly into the inflation readings the Fed watches most closely. Bitcoin jumps to an eight-month high as short-sellers get squeezed ahead of the Trump–Xi summit

Bitcoin climbed from Friday's close of $77,979.54 to about $85,600–86,200 by Wednesday morning, a rise of roughly 10% for the week and, measured over a slightly different seven-day window, a reported 14% — its highest level in about eight months — as renewed buying through bitcoin exchange-traded funds combined with a short squeeze, in which traders who had bet on falling prices were forced to buy bitcoin back at a loss to close out those bets. The rally came as President Trump prepared to host Chinese President Xi Jinping in Washington for talks covering trade, artificial intelligence and Iranian oil. The CLARITY Act, the Senate bill meant to set clear federal rules for crypto markets, saw no further action this week after the prior edition's failed procedural vote, leaving comprehensive crypto legislation stalled in the Senate for the year.

Context & background: Last edition's crypto story tracked bitcoin's whiplash around the CLARITY Act's failed cloture vote (the procedural motion that ends debate and needs 60 of 100 senators) — a roughly 4.2% one-day drop followed by most of that loss being recovered by Friday's close. This week's move is a continuation of that recovery rather than a new shock.

The details: One market strategist described the move plainly: "I think the crypto winter is over" — meaning the long stretch of falling crypto prices that had discouraged buyers — "although that does not necessarily mean the path higher will be linear." Bitcoin's one-month gain now stands at roughly 11.8%, even though it remains down about 23.6% from a year earlier. On the legislative side, nothing moved this week: the CLARITY Act's odds of becoming law in 2026 at all had already fallen to the low single digits after the failed vote, and no new Senate floor action was reported in this edition's window, consistent with one analyst's view that comprehensive crypto legislation is effectively off the table in the Senate until next year.

Why it still matters: Bitcoin rallying on ETF demand and short-covering mechanics — the same forced-buying mechanism as the short squeeze described above, just viewed from the trader's side — not on any crypto-specific policy news — suggests its price is currently being driven by the same broad appetite for risk (investors' general willingness to buy riskier, more volatile investments when confidence is high) that is moving stocks, rather than by anything unique to crypto markets. That makes it worth watching whether the rally holds once Thursday's Trump–Xi summit concludes and the news catalyst behind it fades.

lightbulbEditor's view

Rates, not earnings, are back in the driver's seat

1. One data release moved every market this week. A single stronger-than-forecast reading on business activity pushed bond yields to their highest level in nearly two decades on Wednesday, and by the end of that one session it had erased two days of stock-market gains, pulled Goldman Sachs's Federal Reserve forecast forward by two months, and taken the shine off gold's rally.

2. Bitcoin is trading like a risk asset again, not a hedge against Washington gridlock. A "risk asset" is an investment whose price tends to rise when investors are feeling confident and fall when they're not, moving with the broader mood rather than against it. The Senate's crypto rules bill is exactly as stalled as it was a week ago, yet bitcoin jumped to an eight-month high anyway — evidence that ETF demand and short-covering mechanics, not legislative news, are setting the price right now.

3. Oil's retreat is quietly doing the Federal Reserve's work for it. A sixth straight daily decline in Brent crude, tied to easing Strait of Hormuz tension around the Trump–Xi meeting, is one of the few developments that could let the central bank stop after one more rate increase rather than several — see this edition's Geopolitics briefing for the fuller diplomatic picture.

4. Smaller companies are absorbing the rate shock first. The Russell 2000 small-company stock index fell harder than the S&P 500 or the Dow on Wednesday, and Germany's DAX (Germany's benchmark stock index) fell in sympathy too — a pattern that has now shown up for three straight editions running.

5. Meta's AI-agent story is a technology story wearing a markets hat. The 11% stock jump and record Nasdaq closes are this edition's headline numbers; the substance of what Meta's Muse agent actually does is covered in this edition's Frontier Tech briefing.

Next edition should check whether the Federal Reserve's 28 October decision matches Goldman Sachs's now-faster call, whether bitcoin holds above $85,000 once the Trump–Xi meeting's news cycle fades, and whether oil's six-day slide continues or reverses in its aftermath.
