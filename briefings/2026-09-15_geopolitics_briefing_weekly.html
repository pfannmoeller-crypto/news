Geopolitics · 6–15 September 2026 · catch-up edition

Iran's war escalates again, Moscow grants Kyiv a rare pause, and Ukraine claims a first

The 5 September edition closed with two open threads: whether the US strikes on Iran and Iran's four-country retaliation would stay contained, and whether a reported Russian pause on strikes against Kyiv ahead of the Witkoff/Kushner shuttle was real. Neither stayed simple. Washington and Tehran traded a second, larger round of strikes — US forces destroying at least ten Iranian tankers, Iran hitting a Jordanian base with cluster munitions for the second time in two weeks — while Hormuz traffic stayed pinned near war-time lows and Iran published its own restricted-zone shipping maps. In Ukraine, Moscow's Kyiv-strikes pause turned out to be real but narrow: fighting continued everywhere else during the shuttle, even as Ukrainian forces claimed their first shared battlefield initiative since the 2024 Kursk offensive and Russia's own command asked Putin for 800,000 more troops. Ten days, seven cards, one honest accounting of what moved and what didn't.

Ukraine / Russia — a narrow Kyiv pause, a Lyman breakthrough, and a war everywhere else

3 items
Diplomacy5–12 Sep

Moscow grants a 72-hour Kyiv pause as Witkoff and Kushner leave with "substantive" talks and an October date

US envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner met Vladimir Putin in Moscow on 5 September, then held talks with Volodymyr Zelensky in Kyiv on 6 September — the shuttle the 5 September edition left mid-flight. Kushner said the two sides discussed "security and economic guarantees" for post-war Ukraine and called the talks "substantive"; Zelensky said territorial questions still need direct leader-to-leader negotiation among himself, Putin and Trump. Neither government disclosed the proposal's terms.

Moscow paired the visit with a reciprocal gesture the 5 September edition could not confirm: a roughly 72-hour pause on strikes against Kyiv covering the envoys' Kyiv leg, while Zelensky pledged to keep Russian airspace clear for their return to Moscow. On 12 September, Ukraine's presidential chief of staff Kyrylo Budanov told reporters the next round of trilateral talks is being prepared "for October," without naming a venue.

Context & background. The 5 September edition flagged two unresolved claims: the shuttle's actual Moscow and Kyiv dates, and a widely circulated report that Putin had ordered a pause on strikes against Kyiv ahead of the envoys' arrival — a claim that briefing could not verify because the source page was inaccessible, and which directly conflicted with ISW's 4 September assessment that Putin showed "no indication" Russia would halt strikes during the visit. This window resolves both: the meetings happened on the reported dates, and the strikes-pause claim turns out to have been real, though narrower than first reported — a fixed-duration, capital-specific pause rather than a general halt.

The details. NPR and Al Jazeera both describe the pause as reciprocal and time-boxed: Moscow held fire on Kyiv for roughly 72 hours — Al Jazeera separately frames it as a "three-day ceasefire pledge for both capitals," a minor wording difference this briefing could not fully reconcile — to cover the envoys' visit, while Zelensky separately vowed to keep Russian airspace clear so the delegation could fly to and from Moscow safely. Outside that narrow window the war continued without pause; see the card below. ISW's 6 September assessment separately notes Putin told the envoys Russia can take all of Donetsk Oblast by the end of 2026 — a timeline ISW says is not achievable "until 2032 at their current rate of advance, if they can seize it at all."

Why it still matters. A confirmed, if narrow, mutual pause during a high-profile diplomatic visit is a genuinely new data point — no prior edition of this briefing has recorded Moscow granting Kyiv any pause, disputed or otherwise. But its scope (two capitals, roughly three days) is also the clearest evidence yet that neither side is offering a general ceasefire, consistent with Budanov's own October target rather than an imminent deal. What would change this reading: Moscow extending the pause beyond its original window, or a disclosed proposal that addresses the territorial gap ISW says is the real blocker.

Front line2–12 Sep

Ukraine claims its first shared battlefield initiative since 2024 as Russia asks Putin for 800,000 more troops

Ukrainian forces have largely eliminated the Russian salient north of Lyman, with fighting now concentrated along the Zherebets River — the first time since the August 2024 Kursk offensive that Ukraine has contested Russian initiative at both the strategic and operational level simultaneously, according to ISW's 12 September assessment. Neither side advanced on 11 September, the assessment notes, and the gain disrupts Russian command's broader plan for a wide encirclement of Donetsk Oblast's "Fortress Belt."

Russian military leadership has separately asked Putin for 800,000 additional troops to sustain offensive operations, but ISW notes Russia "does not have the training and administrative capabilities to absorb, integrate, and train 800,000 recruits at one time" — a gap that overlaps with the country's normal October conscription cycle.

Iran / Hormuz — the war escalates again as Hormuz traffic stays near war-time lows

Strikes5–9 Sep

CENTCOM destroys Iranian tankers and Iran hits a Jordanian base again as Brent tops $100

US forces destroyed at least ten Iranian crude-oil tankers between 5 and 9 September — five of them in a single day — according to ISW's Critical Threats Project (ISW-CTP), which cites US Central Command (CENTCOM) statements alongside Wall Street Journal and Reuters reporting. Iran retaliated on 8–9 September with cluster-munition ballistic missiles against Jordan's Muwaffaq Salti Airbase near Azraq — the second Jordanian base Iran has targeted in two weeks — where Jordan's military says it intercepted 18 of 20 incoming missiles, with no casualties reported.

Iran also struck commercial shipping on 9 September and reportedly attempted to lay further mines in the strait; Brent crude crossed $100 a barrel for the first time since late July, according to the Council on Foreign Relations' (CFR) conflict tracker.

Context & background. The 5 September edition closed with the Iran war having just reignited: US strikes on seven sites on 1 September killed at least 11, and Iran retaliated against Jordan, Bahrain, Kuwait and the UAE across three days. This window shows that round was not the end of the escalation the 5 September edition tracked — a second, larger wave of US tanker strikes and Iranian retaliation followed within days, resolving the open question of whether the conflict would stay contained.

The details. Sourcing on the exact scale diverges: ISW-CTP's 9 September update counts ten tankers destroyed since 5 September (five specifically on 8 September) and names three commercial vessels Iran struck on 9 September — the Hercules Star off Dubai, an LNG carrier at Khor Fakkan, and the New Andros in Iraqi waters. CFR's tracker, covering the same window, describes Iran "attacking ten commercial ships" on 9 September without naming them — a figure this briefing could not reconcile against ISW-CTP's three named vessels and flags as the less-corroborated of the two. A separate, single-sourced Iranian claim that its Revolutionary Guard Corps (IRGC) seized an uncrewed US submarine near the strait's entrance on 8 September has not been confirmed by any US source this briefing found. Iran also announced on 6 September that it would establish a new restricted zone and publish its own shipping-corridor maps through the strait, a claim reported only by CFR's aggregator.

Why it still matters. A conflict the 5 September edition described as having just reignited from a genuine August lull has now produced two escalation waves in ten days, each larger in geographic and material scope than the last — US tanker strikes and a second Jordanian-base attack are a materially different register from the first wave's regional retaliation. Brent's move above $100 a barrel is covered further in this week's companion markets briefing. What would change this reading: a new ceasefire announcement, or a third wave that draws in Israel directly.

Hormuz shipping3–9 Sep

Hormuz traffic stays near war-time lows as Iran declares its own shipping corridor

The Strait of Hormuz saw only seven vessel transits on 9 September — four outbound, three inbound — against a 10-day average of 14 and a pre-crisis baseline of roughly 100–125 large commercial vessels daily, according to Radio Free Europe/Radio Liberty's (RFE/RL) tracking. Independent maritime-data firms cited by Al Jazeera (Kpler, Lloyd's List Intelligence, JMIC Advisory) put daily transits in the single digits to low teens through late August and early September, and the overall average since the conflict's outbreak at roughly seven vessels a day — far below the 30–40 ships and 10–18 million daily barrels US officials, including President Trump and Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent, have separately claimed.

Iran announced on 6 September that it would establish a new restricted zone in the Persian Gulf and publish maps for its own shipping corridor through the strait, following what it described as strikes on merchant vessels over the preceding weekend.

Context & background. The 5 September edition logged a 6-transit day on 30 August, about 7% of the pre-crisis baseline, arriving just before the reignition covered in the card above. This window's 9 September count (7 transits) shows that depressed level has persisted through the new escalation rather than recovering or collapsing further.

The details. This briefing found a wide, unresolved gap between US officials' public figures and independent tracking data: Trump has cited the US Navy assisting roughly 30 ships a day; two US officials told CNN 40 vessels carrying 18 million barrels passed through on a single Tuesday; Bessent cited "at least 10 million barrels" daily and up to 17 million on one Monday. Kpler, Lloyd's List and JMIC — three independent maritime-analytics firms — instead show single-digit-to-low-teens daily transits through the same period. Analysts quoted by Al Jazeera suggest the gap may partly reflect different counting methods (naval auxiliaries and support craft versus laden commercial cargo vessels) and "dark" transits with transponders switched off, but the two pictures remain far apart and this briefing could not adjudicate between them.

Why it still matters. A near-seven-month pattern of shipping data contradicting official US claims is now consistent enough across multiple independent trackers that this briefing treats the tracker figures as the better-supported picture — consistent with the read the 5 September edition already reached. Iran's own announcement of a parallel "shipping corridor" is a first: no prior edition recorded Tehran publishing its own routing map through the strait. What would change this reading: a named international shipping body confirming the higher US-claimed transit figures, or a sustained multi-week rise in independently tracked daily transits.

Legislation8–14 Sep

The Graham Russia-Iran sanctions bill gets a House vote date, bought with new Trump tariff powers

The House Rules Committee scheduled a meeting for Monday, 14 September, to take up the Lindsey O. Graham Sanctioning Russia and Iran Act, with a full floor vote expected later in the week — the first concrete scheduling step since the House returned from recess on 1 September, which the 5 September edition logged without a vote date. President Trump backed the bill after lawmakers added provisions granting him discretionary tariff powers of up to 100% on major buyers of Russian energy, including China, Japan and some European allies, alongside sanctions on Russian officials.

The Senate passed the bill 86–11 in August; House Democrats have separately voiced opposition over its potential effect on inflation and consumer costs. The bill is formally named for Senator Lindsey Graham, who died in July 2026.

No new NATO airspace incident surfaces as Germany's Bonn consulate deadline runs out the clock

No new North Atlantic Treaty Organization (NATO) airspace incident or drone attack surfaced in this briefing's research for the 6–15 September window, extending the pattern that followed Germany's 1–2 September attribution of the Leipzig/Halle airport drone attack to Russia. Germany's announced deadline to close Russia's Bonn consulate — 18 September — falls just after this window closes and remains unresolved.

European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen said on 1 September that EU foreign ministers would prepare additional sanctions in response to the Leipzig attack, though no such package had surfaced by this edition's compile date. Russia's threatened "mirror" retaliatory measures, promised by its ambassador to Germany after the attribution, also remain unspecified.

Two open threads from 5 September both resolved — and both resolved into "yes, but narrower"

1. The Iran war's 31 August/1 September strikes were not the escalation's end point. A second, larger wave — at least ten US tanker strikes and a second Iranian attack on a Jordanian base — followed within days, which is the clearest answer this briefing can give to the question the 5 September edition left open.

2. Moscow's Kyiv strikes-pause claim turned out to be real, but only for the two capitals. Roughly 72 hours, tied directly to the diplomatic visit, not a general ceasefire — the war continued at its normal pace everywhere else during the same window.

3. Ukraine's Lyman-area gains are this window's most significant single claim. ISW frames it as Kyiv's first shared battlefield initiative since the 2024 Kursk offensive, arriving the same week Russian command reportedly asked Putin for 800,000 more troops it may struggle to train.

4. Hormuz's independent shipping data keeps drifting further from US officials' public figures. Three separate maritime-analytics firms now show single-digit-to-low-teens daily transits against Washington's repeated claims of 30–40 ships a day — a gap this briefing has tracked since the 5 September edition and still cannot close.

5. The Graham sanctions bill finally has a date. After a month "stuck behind recess," a Rules Committee meeting and a tariff-power concession moved it forward — though the floor vote itself remains unconfirmed as this edition closes.

6. Hybrid warfare in Europe stayed quiet, but not resolved. Two concrete deadlines — Germany's Bonn consulate closure and the EU sanctions package von der Leyen promised — sit just past this window's edge rather than inside it.

Wildcard to watch into the next edition: whether the House floor vote passes the Graham bill, whether Germany's Bonn consulate closure and the EU sanctions package land on schedule, and whether either war produces a third escalation wave or a genuine pause before then.
