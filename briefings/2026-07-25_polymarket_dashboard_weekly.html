Four days ago the story was "risk assets bought the dip despite the war." This week the story is that the dip-buying was premature, but for reasons that turned out to be about AI spending, not Iran.

Geopolitics · 5 underlying markets · The ceasefire window closed; Gulf states now fighting back
trending_downDeteriorating — the mediator proposal went nowhere, strikes hit a 13th consecutive night, and oil settled above $100 for the first time since May

The genuinely hopeful thread from last edition — the Qatar-Egypt-Pakistan-Oman 10-day ceasefire proposal — did not survive contact with the week. Iran continued its military response rather than pausing to negotiate, and the underlying campaign has now run 13 consecutive nights of American airstrikes with no sign of a reopened Strait of Hormuz. The most consequential new fact is who is now doing the fighting: coverage this week described Bahraini and Kuwaiti warplanes striking Iranian targets directly — "rare retaliation" from Gulf states that had, until now, mostly been on the receiving end of Iranian drone and missile fire. Iran itself claimed drone attacks on facilities in Bahrain, Jordan and Kuwait on July 24, and explosions were reported near a US base in Iraq the same day. The UN Secretary-General warned the region was "being pulled into an ever-widening circle of confrontation," which is now a literal description rather than a rhetorical one. And the human cost widened further — a dignified transfer was held July 22 for two more service members, Sgt. Michael Emmanuel Swinton and 1st Lt. trending_upSharply hawkish — odds of a hike at the actual July 29 FOMC meeting roughly tripled in a week as $100 oil compounds a hawkish new Fed chair

This is the most consequential single move on the entire dashboard this edition. Last week this board carried the July 29 no-change probability near 91%, treating a hike at the imminent meeting as functionally off the table. That assumption broke this week: money markets now price the July 29 FOMC meeting itself at roughly a 35% chance of a 25bp hike, up from about 10% a week prior — a more-than-tripling in days, not weeks. The September meeting is priced even more aggressively: CME FedWatch shows roughly 78% odds of a hike by September, up from 52% a week earlier. The proximate driver is unambiguous: Brent's move through $90 to a $100.69 close removes the "look-through" argument the Fed had been leaning on, and new Fed Chair Kevin Warsh continues to be read by markets as the more hawkish voice in the room. The Polymarket-specific hike-in-2026 (any) leg is more muted by comparison — ~64%, up only modestly from ~63% carried last edition — which is itself informative: the "any hike this year" question was already close to fully pricing the oil shock, while the specific-meeting odds (July 29, September) had further to move because they had been anchored to an assumption of Fed patience that just broke. The clearest cross-asset confirmation is in gold, which should be the most natural safe-haven beneficiary of a widening war and instead fell to ~$4,050 from a two-week high of $4,165, as a stronger dollar and rising Treasury yields (~4.71%) on hawkish repricing outweighed war-driven haven demand. This is happening at the same time Brent settled above $100 for the first time since May — two separate, simultaneous risk-off triggers landing in the same session, one geopolitical and one AI-specific.
