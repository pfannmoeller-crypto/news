POLITICS2016Nov 9, 2016 (vote held Nov 8)

Donald Trump won the presidency overnight, losing the popular vote but taking the Electoral College

In the early hours of Wednesday 9 November 2016, US networks projected that Donald Trump, a property developer who had never held public office, had won the presidency against Hillary Clinton, the Democratic candidate. Clinton telephoned him to concede and spoke publicly later that morning.

The result contradicted almost every pre-election poll average. Trump won through narrow margins in Michigan, Wisconsin and Pennsylvania, while Clinton received about 2.9 million more votes nationwide.

Context & background. The US picks its president through the Electoral College: each state has a number of electors roughly matching its size, and a candidate needs 270 of the 538. Nearly every state gives all its electors to whoever wins its popular vote, so a candidate can win nationally in raw votes and still lose. This had last happened in 2000. Forecasters had given Clinton a clear lead for months, which is why the overnight result landed as a shock rather than an ordinary change of government.

The details. The vote itself took place on Tuesday 8 November; the projection of Trump as winner came around 2:30 a.m. Eastern time on the 9th, so the 9th is the day the outcome became known. The 306–232 figure is the election-night count. When the electors met in December, seven of them voted for someone other than their pledged candidate (five Democratic, two Republican), which left the final tally at 304–227. Trump took the office on 20 January 2017.

Why it still matters. The election reset US policy on trade, immigration and alliances, and Trump returned to office after the 2024 election. It also left a lasting argument about whether the Electoral College should stand, and about the reliability of polling, that is still part of how each US election is covered.

DISASTER2016Nov 14, 2016, just after midnight local time

A magnitude 7.8 earthquake tore through several faults at once and cut off New Zealand's Kaikōura

At two minutes past midnight on 14 November 2016 (still the afternoon of 13 November in Europe and the Americas), a magnitude 7.8 earthquake struck the north of New Zealand's South Island. Two people died. The coastal town of Kaikōura was isolated when landslides closed the only highway and the main railway.

It was the strongest New Zealand earthquake since 2009, and geologists found it had broken at least a dozen separate faults in one continuous event.

Context & background. New Zealand sits on the boundary between the Pacific and Australian tectonic plates and is hit by damaging earthquakes regularly. The country's official geological hazard service, GeoNet, had a dense network of instruments in place, which is why the rupture could be reconstructed in unusual detail.

The details. Instead of slipping along one fault, the rupture jumped from fault to fault and travelled north-east up the coast over roughly two minutes. Parts of the coastline were lifted by several metres, exposing seabed, and one fault shifted the ground sideways by more than ten metres. Hundreds of landslides blocked roads, and the main road and rail link was closed for weeks, stranding tourists and local residents; the road reopened fully in December 2017. Insurance claims ran into the billions of New Zealand dollars, though published totals differ by source and date.

Why it still matters. Seismologists had assumed faults rupture one at a time when estimating regional risk. Kaikōura showed that several can link up and produce a bigger earthquake than any single fault would, and hazard models in New Zealand and elsewhere have been revised to allow for it.

SCIENCE2016Nov 14, 2016

The closest full moon since 1948 rose on 14 November 2016, and the next will not come until 2034

On Monday 14 November 2016 the full moon coincided with the moon's closest point to Earth, about 356,500 kilometres away. That made it the closest full moon since 26 January 1948, and it appeared up to about 14 percent wider and about 30 percent brighter than the smallest full moon of a year.

The next full moon this close falls on 25 November 2034.

Context & background. The moon travels in an oval orbit, so its distance from Earth varies between roughly 357,000 and 406,000 kilometres. When a full moon happens near the closest end of that range it is popularly called a supermoon. The word is not an astronomical term; an astrologer coined it in 1979, and astronomers prefer “perigee full moon”.

The details. The 2016 event was unusual because the full moon and the closest approach almost coincided: the closest point came a couple of hours before the moment of full moon on 14 November. Also, the orbit's shape is not constant, so some years bring a closer approach than others. It was widely photographed around the world.

Why it still matters. Supermoons are one of the few astronomical events that millions of people actually go outside to watch, and they have become an annual fixture of science communication. The 2016 date is the benchmark that later ones are measured against.

DISASTER2001Nov 12, 2001

A passenger jet lost its tail over Queens two months after 11 September, and pilot inputs were blamed

On the morning of 12 November 2001, American Airlines Flight 587, an Airbus A300-600 bound for the Dominican Republic, crashed into the Belle Harbor neighbourhood of Queens, New York, about two minutes after leaving Kennedy Airport. All 260 people aboard and five people on the ground died.

Coming two months after the 11 September attacks, it was first feared to be terrorism. Investigators found no evidence of sabotage.

Context & background. New York was still in shock, and air travel was only partly back to normal. The jet climbed behind a Japan Airlines Boeing 747, and the crash happened as it flew through the larger plane's wake turbulence, the swirling air a heavy aircraft leaves behind it.

The details. The National Transportation Safety Board concluded in October 2004 that the first officer, flying the plane, reacted to the turbulence with repeated, forceful rudder pedal movements that put such a load on the tail fin that it snapped off. The Board named two contributing factors: a rudder-pedal design that needed very little force and travel for large rudder movement, and an American Airlines training programme that, in its words, taught inappropriate use of the rudder in upset situations. The fin itself was found to meet its strength requirements.

Why it still matters. Airbus and the airline industry changed pilot guidance: rudder pedals are not to be used aggressively to counter turbulence, and training on upset recovery was rewritten. The accident remains the second-deadliest in US aviation after 1979, and the deadliest involving an Airbus A300. Many victims were from the Dominican community of northern Manhattan, which marks the date each year.

BUSINESSPOLITICS2001Nov 10, 2001

The World Trade Organization approved China's entry in Doha, and the global factory floor shifted

On 10 November 2001, trade ministers meeting in Doha, Qatar, approved China's accession to the World Trade Organization by consensus, ending fifteen years of negotiation. China became the organization's 143rd member on 11 December 2001. Taiwan's accession was approved the day after.

The same Doha conference launched a new global trade round, the Doha Development Agenda, which never reached a final deal.

Context & background. China had applied to join the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade, the World Trade Organization's predecessor, in 1986. Talks dragged on over tariffs, farm trade, state-owned enterprises and the protection of foreign intellectual property. The United States agreed to give China permanent normal trading relations in 2000, which cleared the main obstacle.

The details. Membership obliged China to cut tariffs, open parts of banking, insurance and telecoms, and accept the organization's dispute-settlement system. The Doha conference was the first ministerial meeting after the 11 September attacks. The date is the approval vote; the date China actually became a member is a month later, and sources sometimes blur the two.

Why it still matters. China's share of world goods exports rose from about 4 percent in 2001 to more than 10 percent by 2011. Western economists and politicians still argue over how many manufacturing jobs in the US and Europe were lost as a result, and the dispute shapes today's tariff and export-control fights.

CONFLICT2001Nov 13, 2001 (fighting overnight Nov 12–13)

The Taliban abandoned Kabul without a fight, five weeks after US bombing began

During the night of 12–13 November 2001 Taliban fighters left Afghanistan's capital, Kabul, and fled south towards Kandahar. Forces of the Northern Alliance, an Afghan anti-Taliban coalition, entered the city on 13 November while crowds celebrated, and residents came out to play music that the Taliban had banned.

The Taliban had ruled Kabul since September 1996. Their loss of the capital came only days after they lost the northern city of Mazar-i-Sharif on 9 November.

Context & background. After the 11 September attacks, the Taliban government refused to hand over al-Qaeda's leader, Osama bin Laden. The US and its allies began air strikes on 7 October, supported by a small number of American special forces and intelligence officers working with Northern Alliance commanders on the ground.

The details. The pace surprised even planners: Mazar-i-Sharif fell on 9 November, and the Taliban front north of Kabul gave way within days. Sources disagree on whether to date the fall of Kabul to the 12th or 13th; reports agree that the Taliban left overnight and Northern Alliance troops, who had been told by their American allies to stay outside the city, entered on the 13th. Kunduz fell on 25 November, and Kandahar, the Taliban's last stronghold, on 7 December. At a conference near Bonn, Afghan factions agreed an interim government under Hamid Karzai, an Afghan political leader, who took office on 22 December.

Why it still matters. The quick victory led many to expect a short mission. Instead the US and its allies stayed twenty years, until the Taliban retook Kabul on 15 August 2021, almost without resistance, in a mirror image of how they had left it.

POLITICS1976Nov 15, 1976

Quebec elected a government committed to leaving Canada, the first of its kind

On 15 November 1976 the Parti Québécois, led by the former journalist René Lévesque, won the Quebec provincial election, taking 71 of 110 seats on 41 percent of the vote. It was the first time a party that openly favoured separation from Canada had formed a provincial government.

Lévesque told supporters he had never thought he could be so proud to be a Québécois.

Context & background. Quebec, where about eight in ten people speak French as their first language, had been changing quickly since the 1960s, a period called the Quiet Revolution, as the province took control of its schools, health care and electricity from the Church and from English-speaking business owners. The Parti Québécois was founded in 1968 and had won only six seats in 1973.

The details. The 1976 win owed as much to unhappiness with the long-governing Liberals, under Premier Robert Bourassa, as to support for independence; polls showed most voters were not yet in favour of leaving. The party had promised not to act without a referendum. The government's first major law, in August 1977, made French the language of the workplace, of public signs and of schooling for immigrants. The referendum came on 20 May 1980, and 59.6 percent voted no.

Why it still matters. Quebec voted again on independence in 1995 and the “no” side won by about one percentage point. The language law, Bill 101, still stands, and the question of Quebec's status still shapes federal politics in Canada.

CULTURE1976Nov 11, 1976

Alexander Calder, who made sculpture move, died in New York weeks after a major retrospective opened

The American sculptor Alexander Calder died in New York on 11 November 1976, aged 78. He had invented the mobile, an artwork of balanced metal shapes that turns in the air, and later made huge stationary steel works he called stabiles.

His death came shortly after the opening of a large retrospective at the Whitney Museum of American Art.

Context & background. Calder trained as an engineer before turning to art, and moved to Paris in 1926, where he made a wire-and-wood “Cirque Calder” that he performed himself. A 1930 visit to the painter Piet Mondrian's studio pushed him toward abstraction, and in 1931 the artist Marcel Duchamp coined the word “mobile” for his motorized and later wind-driven pieces.

The details. Reference works agree on the date and place of death; sources differ on details, so no cause is given here. The retrospective at the Whitney Museum was one of the largest shows of his work in his lifetime. The Calder Foundation, set up by his family, has since catalogued more than 22,000 works.

Why it still matters. Mobiles hang today in the entrance halls of airports, banks and museums on every continent, and his work is among the most reproduced and most expensive of any 20th-century sculptor. The idea he started, that a sculpture can be a thing that moves and changes shape, runs through later kinetic art.

CONFLICT1976Nov 15, 1976 (some reports 17–18 Nov)

A Syrian-led Arab force moved into Beirut, ending the first phase of Lebanon's civil war

On 15 November 1976 the Arab Deterrent Force, an Arab League peacekeeping body made up mostly of Syrian soldiers, took up positions in Beirut to enforce a ceasefire. The Lebanese civil war had begun in April 1975 and had killed tens of thousands of people.

The ceasefire was agreed at an Arab summit in Riyadh on 17–18 October and took effect in Beirut on 15 November.

Context & background. Lebanon's civil war pitted mostly Christian right-wing militias against a Palestinian-left alliance. Syria first intervened in June 1976 on the Christian side, against the Palestinians, which angered other Arab states. The Riyadh summit gave that intervention an Arab League cover by turning Syria's troops into an official peacekeeping force.

The details. The main reference for the 15 November date is the encyclopedia entry for the force; Reuters film from 17–18 November shows the force moving into central Beirut and tearing down barricades, so the entry may have been spread over several days. Troop numbers vary by source, from about 25,000 to 30,000 Syrians. Fighting calmed for about a year, then resumed in the south, and in 1978 Israel invaded.

Why it still matters. The “temporary” Syrian presence lasted 29 years, until April 2005, and left Damascus deeply influential in Lebanese politics. Many of today's lines of power in Lebanon can be traced to the arrangement made in 1976.

Editor's view

Two of this week's events, the 2001 fall of Kabul and the 1976 Syrian entry into Beirut, were quick military successes that came to be seen as the start of long occupations rather than the end of a war. The other seven share no thread worth forcing. The weakest link is the Beirut date, which rests on thinner sourcing than the rest.
